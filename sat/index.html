Fundamentals
What is an Orbit?
An orbit is the path something follows around Earth because of gravity. A satellite in orbit is not floating — it is actually falling toward Earth the whole time. But it is also moving sideways so fast that the ground keeps curving away before it can land. Think of throwing a ball — if you could throw it fast enough, it would circle the whole planet without ever hitting the ground.
All orbits are shaped like an oval. Earth sits off to one side — not in the center. This is why every satellite has a closest point (called perigee) and a farthest point (called apogee) in its path.
Easy way to picture it: Spin a ball on a string above your head. The string pulls inward but the ball keeps going around. Gravity works the same way in space — it pulls the satellite inward, and the satellite's sideways speed keeps it from falling down.
🪖 17E — WHY YOU NEED TO KNOW THIS EW RELEVANCE

Understanding how orbits work tells you how enemy satellites behave. A satellite in a circular orbit is predictable — you can calculate exactly when it will pass overhead. An elliptical orbit means the satellite speeds up and slows down, spending more time over specific regions. Knowing orbit shape helps you predict when an enemy recon or comms satellite is watching your AO.

  • Circular LEO satellites pass overhead in predictable windows (~90 min repeat). You can time OPSEC events around them.
  • Elliptical (HEO) satellites like Russia's Molniya spend 8+ hours over one region — don't assume they've moved on.
  • Atmospheric drag causes LEO satellites to slowly change altitude. Older ISR birds may not follow their published orbital parameters exactly.
🌐
Circular Orbit
Looks like a circle from the side. The satellite stays about the same distance from Earth the whole orbit. Easy to predict because the satellite always moves at the same speed.
🥚
Elliptical Orbit
Looks like a stretched-out oval. One end of the path is close to Earth and one end is far away. The satellite goes fast at the close end and slow at the far end. Useful when you want a satellite to spend more time over one region.
📐
Inclination
How tilted the orbit is. 0° means the satellite circles above the equator. 90° means it passes over both poles. A bigger number means it flies over more of Earth.
☀️
Sun-Synchronous Orbit
A specific 97.8° tilt that makes the satellite pass over the same place at the same time of day, every day. This means the sunlight angle is always the same when it takes photos — which makes it much easier to compare images over time.
⚡
Delta-V (Δv)
The amount you need to speed up or slow down to change your orbit. Think of it as the price tag for any move in space. Bigger orbit change = more fuel used.
🌄
Atmospheric Drag
Even 250 miles above Earth there is a tiny bit of air. Over time that air friction slows a satellite down and lowers its orbit. The ISS loses about a mile of altitude every month and has to boost itself back up with engines.
Orbital Geometry
What is an Orbital Plane?
An orbital plane is the flat imaginary surface that a satellite travels in. Think of it like a frisbee thrown at an angle through Earth's center — the frisbee always stays flat and level to itself, no matter how it is tilted.
You can tilt an orbital plane to any angle you want. A 0° tilt means the satellite always flies above the equator. A 90° tilt means it flies over both poles. In between, different parts of Earth get covered. To see the whole planet, you spread several orbital planes around Earth — like slices of an orange where each slice covers a different strip.
Real example: GPS uses 6 orbital planes, each tilted at 55° and spaced evenly around the Earth like wheel spokes. Working together, they guarantee you can always see at least 4 GPS satellites from anywhere on Earth at any time — and you need 4 to get an accurate position fix.

🪖 17E — WHY YOU NEED TO KNOW THIS EW RELEVANCE

Knowing which orbital plane a satellite is in tells you what part of Earth it covers. Enemy satellites in polar or near-polar planes can see the entire Earth over time — including your AO. Satellites in lower-inclination planes may never pass over high-latitude operations.

  • Russian GLONASS uses 64.8° planes — strong coverage over Russia and Arctic regions where your forces may operate in a northern contingency.
  • GPS at 55° covers everywhere including your position. Chinese forces using BeiDou and Russian forces using GLONASS rely on the same math — 55° or close to it — so their satellites have similar coverage patterns.
  • Starlink's ~72 orbital planes mean near-continuous coverage globally — friendly battlefield C2 is enabled by this but also creates a detectable signal environment.
Satellite Networks
What is a Satellite Constellation?
A constellation is a group of satellites that work together as a team. Any single satellite can only see a small part of Earth at a time. Put dozens of them in different orbits and angles though, and you can make sure there is always at least one satellite overhead no matter where you are on Earth.
GPS needs you to be able to see at least 4 satellites at the same time to know where you are. GPS has 24 satellites spread across 6 different angles around Earth — just enough to always have 4 visible from anywhere. Starlink has over 6,000 satellites so there is almost always one right above you for fast internet.
📡
Walker Delta Pattern
A pattern where satellites are spread evenly across several orbital planes all at the same tilt angle. GPS and Galileo both use this. It covers the middle parts of Earth really well.
🌍
Walker Star Pattern
Near-polar orbits spread evenly around Earth so the whole planet gets covered, including the poles. Iridium satellite phones and some Starlink satellites use this pattern.
🔗
Inter-Satellite Links
Some satellites can send data to other satellites using lasers instead of bouncing it through a ground station. The data hops from satellite to satellite across the sky. For long distances this can actually be faster than going through undersea internet cables.
♻️
GEO Orbital Slots
The band of GEO satellites above the equator has limited space. Countries and companies reserve specific positions — called slots — so two satellites do not interfere with each other's signals. Think of it like reserved parking spots in space.
🪖 17E — WHY YOU NEED TO KNOW THIS EW RELEVANCE

Constellations are the networks behind every signal you'll intercept, jam, or protect. GPS is a constellation. Starlink battlefield C2 is a constellation. Iridium satellite phones are a constellation. Knowing how constellations work tells you why some signals are always available and how to disrupt or exploit them.

  • Inter-satellite laser links (Starlink Gen2): Data can route satellite-to-satellite without hitting a ground station — making it harder to cut off by attacking ground terminals alone.
  • Iridium (Walker Star, 86.4°): Truly global coverage including poles. Enemy special operations may use Iridium for comms — detectable at L-band (~1.6 GHz).
  • GEO comms slots: A GEO satellite's slot number tells you its longitude. If you know a hostile military is using a specific GEO slot, you can point a dish at that longitude and look for their uplink.
Orbital Extremes
Apogee & Perigee
Because every orbit is oval-shaped, a satellite gets closer to Earth at one end and farther away at the other. When it is close, it moves fast. When it is far, it slows down. This happens automatically because of gravity — no engine needed. Scientists call this Kepler's Second Law, but you can just remember: close = fast, far = slow.

The highest point in the orbit. The satellite moves slowest here because gravity's pull is weakest. For the Moon, when a full moon happens near perigee (closest), it looks bigger and brighter — this is a "supermoon."

🪖 17E — WHY YOU NEED TO KNOW THIS EW RELEVANCE

Apogee and perigee directly determine when and for how long a satellite can see your AO. For HEO satellites like Russia's Molniya, the apogee is the long dwell period — that's when they're watching. The perigee is a quick flyby. Know which part of the orbit applies to your threat window.

  • HEO early-warning satellites (SBIRS): Their apogee is parked over Russia — they watch ICBM launch zones for the 8+ hour dwell. Understanding apogee explains why 2–3 satellites can provide continuous coverage of a threat region.
  • LEO ISR pass timing: A recon satellite's perigee over your AO is its highest-resolution window. Images taken near perigee are sharper. Plan sensitive activity away from predicted perigee crossings.
  • Signal strength changes: A satellite's signal is stronger at perigee (closer) and weaker at apogee (farther). For SIGINT, this affects detectable range.

🪖 17E — WHY YOU NEED TO KNOW THIS GNSS / SIGINT

Every major adversary has their own navigation satellite system. Knowing which GNSS a threat force uses tells you what signals to monitor, what jamming they're vulnerable to, and how precise their weapons and navigation actually are.

  • Russia uses GLONASS (L1/L2 FDMA): Different frequency per satellite — harder to blanket-jam than GPS. Russian forces fall back to GLONASS when GPS is jammed. Know both frequencies.
  • China uses BeiDou (B3I military): Chinese precision-guided weapons use BeiDou B3I. If China is your OPFOR, understanding BeiDou frequency and accuracy tells you their weapon CEP (circular error probable).
  • EU Galileo (E6 PRS): NATO allies may use Galileo encrypted PRS signals for military use. Be aware so you don't misidentify friendly encrypted nav signals as hostile.
  • GPS L1 (1575 MHz) is the weakest link: Civilian L1 is unencrypted and easily jammed or spoofed. Military L2 P(Y) and M-code are encrypted and jam-resistant. Know which signal your equipment uses.
  • Multi-constellation receivers are harder to attack: A receiver using GPS + GLONASS + Galileo simultaneously requires jamming all three frequencies at once — much harder for an adversary.
What Satellites Actually Do
Types of Satellites by Purpose
There are over 8,000 working satellites orbiting Earth right now. Engineers do not just randomly pick an orbit — the orbit is chosen based on what the satellite needs to do. Imaging satellites go to LEO where they are close enough for sharp photos. Navigation satellites go to MEO for wide coverage. Weather and TV satellites go to GEO so they stay in one spot in the sky.
📡

Satellites that carry phone calls, internet, and TV signals from one place to another. Some stay fixed above one spot (GEO). Others move in low orbits (LEO) for faster connections.

Orbital Inclinations
How Inclination Shapes Every Orbit
Inclination is the tilt of a satellite's orbital path compared to Earth's equator. The bigger the tilt, the higher the latitude the satellite can reach. It is the most important number for figuring out which parts of Earth a satellite flies over.
A 0° orbit stays above the equator the whole time. A 90° orbit passes over both poles and eventually flies over every place on Earth. Tilts in between cover a middle range. The higher the tilt, the more it costs to launch there — because rockets naturally launch in the direction Earth spins, and fighting that spin takes extra fuel.
🪖 17E — WHY YOU NEED TO KNOW THIS ISR / OPSEC

Inclination tells you which latitudes a satellite covers. A satellite at 28° inclination will never fly over Germany or Korea. One at 97° will fly over every country on Earth. Knowing a satellite's inclination instantly tells you whether it can see your AO.

  • If your operation is north of 75°N (Arctic, Norway, Alaska), only polar or near-polar satellites can see you — GEO cannot.
  • GLONASS at 64.8° sees further north than GPS — Russian forces have better navigation above 60°N than they would if they relied on GPS.
  • Sun-synchronous (97.8°) imaging satellites pass at the same local time daily — predict when they'll image your AO and mask activity during those windows.

Low Earth Orbit200 – 2,000 km
LEO is the most crowded part of space. Satellites here circle Earth in about 90 minutes — 16 full orbits every day. There are still faint traces of atmosphere at this altitude that slowly drag satellites down, so most need engine boosts every few months. Signals travel very fast from LEO (under 20 milliseconds of delay), which makes it great for internet and communication. But one satellite can only see a small patch of Earth, so you need hundreds or thousands of them to cover the whole planet.

Geostationary Orbit35,786 km — fixed above equator
At 35,786 km above the equator, a satellite takes exactly one day to orbit — the same time Earth takes to spin. So the satellite appears to hang motionless in the sky. Your TV dish at home can point at a fixed spot and never need to move. Just three GEO satellites positioned around the equator can cover almost the whole planet. The downside is the huge distance — signals take almost a quarter of a second just to get there and back, which causes a noticeable delay.

Highly Elliptical OrbitPerigee ~500 km · Apogee ~39,300 km
HEO is an extreme stretched-out oval orbit. At one end the satellite skims just above the atmosphere. At the other end it goes far beyond GEO satellites. Because satellites slow way down at the far end, they spend most of their time (8+ hours) hovering high above one region before swinging fast back around. This makes HEO great for covering the Arctic and polar regions that GEO satellites cannot reach. Soviet engineers discovered the classic version of this — the Molniya orbit at 63.4°.

Lunar Orbit~384,400 km average distance
The Moon's orbit around Earth is the longest-studied trajectory in astronomy. At an average of 384,400 km — roughly 30 Earth diameters away — the Moon completes one orbit every 27.3 days. Its orbit is slightly elliptical, so the Moon is slightly closer at perigee (362,600 km) and farther at apogee (405,400 km). The Moon is also tidally locked, meaning it rotates at exactly the same rate it orbits — so the same hemisphere always faces Earth and the "far side" was never seen until spacecraft rounded it in 1959.

🌕The Moon causes Earth's ocean tides through gravitational pull — the tidal force is so strong it's gradually slowing Earth's rotation and pushing the Moon ~3.8 cm farther away every year.
🚀Apollo 8 (1968) was the first crewed spacecraft to enter lunar orbit. All 12 humans who walked on the Moon did so via lunar orbit rendezvous — the Command Module orbited while the Lunar Module descended.
🛰️NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) has been mapping the Moon from a 50 km polar orbit since 2009 — creating the highest-resolution maps of any planetary body other than Earth.
🏗️NASA's Gateway — a small space station to be built in the 2020s — will orbit the Moon in a Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO), providing a staging point for Artemis lunar surface missions and eventual Mars voyages.
🌑A "supermoon" occurs when a full moon coincides with perigee — the Moon appears up to 14% larger and 30% brighter than at apogee. The difference between the largest and smallest apparent Moon is visible to the naked eye.

Apogee & Perigee — Orbital Extremes Explained
Apogee

Farthest Point

405,400 km

Highest point in an elliptical orbit. The satellite moves slowest here — gravity is weakest. For the Moon, a "supermoon" occurs when full moon coincides near perigee.

Perigee

Closest Point

362,600 km

Lowest point in an orbit. Satellite moves fastest here — gravity accelerates it. LEO satellites at perigee experience atmospheric drag that slowly decays the orbit.

Kepler's Second Law — A satellite sweeps equal areas in equal times. It covers more orbital distance near perigee (fast) than apogee (slow).

Satellite Radio Frequency Bands
The L-Band & All Satellite Frequency Bands
Satellites communicate using radio waves, just like your FM radio or Wi-Fi — but at much higher or lower frequencies. Different frequencies travel through the atmosphere differently, bounce off rain differently, and require different sized antennas. Each frequency "band" has a letter name and is used for specific types of satellite links. L-band is the most important for military operations, navigation, and handheld satellite comms.

VHF 30 – 300 MHz λ = 1–10 m
VHF stands for Very High Frequency. It's the lowest radio band used for satellite communication. The waves are very long — up to 10 meters each — which means the antennas are large and you can't send much information at once. VHF is good at going through trees and buildings, and it can bounce off the upper atmosphere (the ionosphere) to travel thousands of miles — without a satellite at all. Most modern systems have moved to higher bands, but VHF is still used for old military backup links and emergency weather broadcasts.

🪖 17E NOTE

VHF is not usually a satellite threat today, but it matters for one big reason: VHF signals can travel around the curve of the Earth by bouncing off the upper atmosphere. That means if your unit transmits on a VHF radio, an enemy listening station thousands of miles away might be able to pick it up — even without a satellite. Always follow emissions control (EMCON) rules on VHF just like any other radio.

UHF 300 MHz – 1 GHz λ = 30 cm – 1 m
UHF stands for Ultra High Frequency. This is the most important frequency band for military tactical radio communication through satellites. Almost every army, navy, and air force in the world uses UHF for satellite comms because the antennas are small enough to hold in your hand or mount on a vehicle. The signals can push through trees, buildings, and rough terrain pretty well. The downside is that you can't send very much data at once — so UHF is great for voice calls and short messages, but not for streaming video.

🪖 17E NOTE — CRITICAL FOR YOUR MOS

UHF military satellite comms are one of your most important targets and biggest risks. Here's what you need to know:

  • Radios like the AN/PRC-117G, -152, and -163 all send signals in the UHF SATCOM range. Enemy signals intelligence teams can pick up those signals with basic equipment. Always follow EMCON — don't transmit unless you have to.
  • MUOS is the US military's newer UHF satellite system. It works like a 3G cell phone through satellites. It sends more data than older systems but it still puts out a detectable radio signal.
  • Russian UHF signals: Russian forces use their own VHF and UHF satellite links. Those signals can be collected and used to figure out where their units are and what they're doing.
  • Jamming: A vehicle-mounted UHF jammer can block satellite comms over a wide area. If your unit's satellite radio stops working suddenly and without explanation, suspect jamming and switch to backup comms.
L-Band ⭐ 1 – 2 GHz λ = 15–30 cm
L-band is the most important satellite frequency for military operations. This is where all GPS navigation signals live, along with handheld satellite phones like Iridium and Inmarsat. L-band signals push through rain and clouds well, and the antennas can be as small as a patch inside your phone. The problem is that by the time these signals reach the ground, they are very weak — like trying to see a flashlight beam from 12,000 miles away. That weakness is why these signals are easy to block or fake.

🪖 17E NOTE — MOST CRITICAL L-BAND KNOWLEDGE

L-band is where GPS lives — and where GPS can be blocked or faked. This is the most important band for a 17E to understand.

  • GPS L1 (1575.42 MHz) is the easiest to jam: The signal is incredibly weak by the time it reaches the ground. A cheap jammer you can buy online can block civilian GPS in a 1–5 mile area. A military vehicle jammer can cover 20+ miles. Russia, China, Iran, and North Korea all jam GPS regularly.
  • GPS spoofing on L1: Instead of just blocking GPS, spoofing sends a fake signal that makes your receiver show the wrong location — and you cannot tell it is wrong. Russia has done this near Moscow and in Syria. Warning signs: your position jumps suddenly, your speed reading makes no sense, or your altitude is wrong.
  • M-code GPS (same 1575 MHz frequency but much stronger): The newest US military GPS signal is built to resist jamming. It spreads the signal over a wider pattern and uses more power. Military radios with M-code can keep working even when civilian GPS in the same area is being jammed.
  • GLONASS L1 (1598–1606 MHz): Russian forces navigate with GLONASS instead of GPS. Each satellite uses a slightly different frequency (called FDMA). This means you cannot block all of them at once with a single jammer — you have to jam a whole range of frequencies simultaneously.
  • BeiDou B3I (1268 MHz): China's military-only GPS signal. Chinese precision-guided weapons use this to navigate. If you are working against Chinese forces, this is the frequency their weapons depend on — and knowing it helps you assess whether you can interfere with their guidance.
  • Iridium (1616–1626 MHz): Special operations forces and remote teams often use Iridium satellite phones. The signal comes out in short quick bursts. With the right direction-finding equipment, you can detect those bursts and locate where the transmitter is.
  • BGAN terminals (Inmarsat L-band): Portable satellite internet terminals used by deployed headquarters and field teams. They send a steady signal up toward a GEO satellite. That signal can be detected and used to find exactly where the terminal is located.
S-Band 2 – 4 GHz λ = 7.5–15 cm
S-band sits just above L-band and is used for deep space tracking, weather radar, some military SATCOM, and regional navigation augmentation. It handles rain better than higher bands but worse than L-band. NASA uses S-band for spacecraft tracking and ISS comms. India's NavIC uniquely uses S-band for navigation to penetrate monsoon rain.

🪖 17E NOTE

S-band is not usually a top priority for a 17E, but there are two things worth knowing. First: NavIC's S-band signal (2.492 GHz) is notable — India built NavIC to work in monsoon rain — so if you are operating in South Asia, Indian forces may still have reliable GPS-like navigation during a heavy storm even when US GPS signals are being degraded. Second: SiriusXM satellite radio (2.33 GHz) comes down from HEO satellites using Tundra orbits. It is a good real-world example of how HEO satellites stay over one region for hours at a time.

C-Band 4 – 8 GHz λ = 3.75–7.5 cm
C-band was the original commercial satellite TV and broadcast band and remains heavily used for video distribution, backhaul, and some military links. It handles rain better than Ku/Ka and works with moderate-sized antennas. The large dishes you see at TV studios and broadcast centers are usually C-band. It's also used for radar altimeters, weather radar, and some ISR downlinks.

🪖 17E NOTE

C-band is worth knowing because some ISR satellite imagery downlinks use it — especially older systems and some foreign military satellites. C-band downlinks (3.7–4.2 GHz) need a medium-sized dish to receive, but the signals on commercial satellites are often not encrypted, which means imagery or broadcast feeds can sometimes be picked up with basic signals intelligence equipment. Also note: C-band holds up better in rain than Ku or Ka — so if an adversary switches their SATCOM from Ku to C during a storm, they are probably protecting a high-value link.

X-Band 8 – 12 GHz λ = 2.5–3.75 cm
X-band is the main military satellite band for sending large amounts of data quickly. This is where live drone video, spy satellite imagery, and military command networks travel. It can carry far more data than UHF or L-band — enough to stream full-motion video from a drone in real time. It also handles rain reasonably well. The US military's main satellite data system — called WGS (Wideband Global SATCOM) — uses X-band, and so does NATO's satellite network.

🪖 17E NOTE — HIGH PRIORITY

X-band is where high-value military data flows. Understanding it is critical for both protecting friendly comms and exploiting enemy links.

  • WGS terminals (AN/GSC-52, AN/TSC-156): These are large dish antennas that point at military satellites in space. When they transmit, they send a detectable X-band signal straight up. A 17E team with the right equipment can use that signal to figure out where the terminal is located.
  • Drone control links: Predator and Reaper drones are controlled through X-band satellite links when they fly too far away for direct radio contact. If you pick up X-band SATCOM signals from a small terminal in the field, the source may be a drone ground control station.
  • Radar imaging satellites: X-band radar imaging satellites can see through clouds, work at night, and even see through light tree cover. Do not assume cloud cover is protecting you from satellite observation — X-band SAR satellites are not affected by weather.
  • Enemy X-band SATCOM: Russia and China both have military X-band satellite systems. If you detect X-band satellite uplink signals from an enemy location, that can tell you where their command post or intelligence processing center is.
Ku-Band 12 – 18 GHz λ = 1.67–2.5 cm
Ku-band is the main frequency for satellite internet, TV broadcasting, and Starlink. You can send a lot of data using Ku-band with a fairly small dish — about 60–90 cm across. The biggest problem with Ku-band is rain. When heavy rain falls between your dish and the satellite, the signal gets absorbed and weakens or drops out. Despite that, this is the band that most forward-deployed military units use for satellite internet through commercial services and Starlink flat panel terminals.

In heavy tropical rain, Ku-band signals can lose so much strength that the link drops completely. Starlink and VSAT terminals can go offline during bad storms. If your area of operations has heavy rain seasons, plan for this and keep UHF backup comms ready.
🪖 17E NOTE — STARLINK & VSAT KEY

Ku-band is where most deployed SATCOM terminals live today — including friendly Starlink and enemy VSAT equivalents.

  • Starlink terminals (Ku-band, ~14 GHz uplink): These are the flat panel antennas used for fast satellite internet. They emit a Ku-band signal straight up toward a satellite. Enemy electronic warfare teams can detect that signal and figure out where the terminal is located. Both Ukraine and Russia have done this in combat to find and target SATCOM users.
  • VSAT terminal OPSEC: Any VSAT dish pointing at a GEO satellite sends a powerful Ku-band signal straight up. An enemy with direction-finding equipment and knowledge of the frequency can use that beam to find your satellite communications hub. Keep SATCOM terminals physically separated from your command post.
  • Rain fade in tropical areas: Ku-band links can drop out completely in monsoon rain or heavy tropical storms. If your unit depends on Ku SATCOM in areas like Southeast Asia, the Philippines, or Central Africa, always have a UHF satellite radio backup ready for when the Ku link fails.
  • Enemy Ku-band SATCOM: Russian and Chinese forces also use Ku-band satellite systems for their deployed headquarters communications. Those uplink signals can be detected by signals intelligence equipment and used to find where their command posts are.
Ka-Band 26.5 – 40 GHz λ = 0.75–1.1 cm
Ka-band ("Kurz-above") offers the highest bandwidth of any common satellite band but is most vulnerable to rain fade. It enables gigabit-speed satellite internet (ViaSat-3, HughesNet Gen5, Starlink's inter-satellite laser links). The US military uses Ka-band on the Advanced EHF (AEHF) satellites for jam-resistant, high-bandwidth command links. Small, high-gain antennas are needed — typically 75 cm or larger for GEO, but small for LEO like Starlink.

🪖 17E NOTE

Ka-band and EHF are where hardened military strategic comms live — harder to jam, harder to intercept, but still detectable as signal sources.

  • AEHF (EHF at ~44 GHz uplink): This system handles the most important military communications — including nuclear command and control. It is extremely resistant to jamming because it uses spread signals, frequency hopping, and special antennas that reject interference. You cannot realistically jam AEHF, but you can still detect the physical terminal and its location.
  • WGS Ka-band: The military's WGS satellite system also has Ka-band channels for very large data transfers — things like high-resolution video and large intelligence files. The terminals emit signals around 30 GHz when they transmit.
  • Rain fade at Ka-band is severe: A heavy tropical storm can completely knock out Ka-band signals. Military systems using Ka-band have backup systems built in — like automatically switching to a different frequency or beam when rain blocks the primary link.
  • Starlink laser links between satellites: Some Starlink satellites pass data to each other using lasers instead of radio waves. Laser signals cannot be detected by radio equipment at all. This means data can travel through space from satellite to satellite without ever touching a ground station — making interception nearly impossible.
