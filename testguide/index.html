1 · Measuring Beats Guessing

You will be able to
  • Explain why you compare what you expect with what you measure.
  • Name the three jobs of a multimeter and an oscilloscope.
  • List the safety rules that apply before you touch a probe to a circuit.

A circuit that does not work is a puzzle with a limited number of causes: power is missing, a connection is open, two points are shorted, or a part is wrong or dead. You cannot see which by looking. You find out by measuring.

The habit that matters most: expect, then measure

Before you put a probe anywhere, decide what you expect to read there. A 9 V battery feeding a 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ divider should give 6 V at the middle. If you read 6 V, move on. If you read 9 V or 0 V, you have learned something specific: the number itself points at the fault. Measuring without an expectation just makes a pile of numbers.

Two instruments, three jobs each

MultimeterOscilloscope
ShowsOne number, such as volts, ohms, or ampsVoltage plotted against time
Best atIs it there? How big? Is the path complete?What shape? How fast? Does it change?
Blind toFast changes and shape. An average hides a lot.Exact small DC values and any current without a probe.

Together they cover almost everything. A meter tells you a supply is 5.02 V. A scope tells you it has 400 mV of 100 kHz ripple riding on it that the meter averaged away.

Safety first

  • Current is what hurts. Even modest voltages can be dangerous across wet skin or through the chest. Treat any mains-connected equipment as lethal.
  • Know what you are measuring. Meters are rated by category (CAT I to CAT IV) for how much energy the circuit can deliver. Use a meter rated for the place you are working.
  • Check your tools. Use leads with intact insulation and shrouded tips. Replace damaged ones.
  • One hand where you can. When probing live circuits, keep your other hand away from grounded metal.
  • Power off to measure resistance, continuity, and components. You will see why in Chapter 3.
  • Never connect an ammeter across a voltage source. It is a near-short and blows the fuse, or worse.

Follow your unit's safety procedures and your instructor's directions. This guide is training material, not a replacement for electrical safety training.

Try it: how many numbers does a fault tell you?
The board is a 9 V battery, R1 = 1 kΩ and R2 = 2 kΩ in a divider. Pick a fault and see what each measurement would read. Notice that each fault leaves a different set of numbers.
Why a 17E caresRadios, power supplies, and antennas fail in the field, and you rarely get a replacement unit on the spot. The person who can quickly tell “no power” from “bad cable” from “bad radio” keeps the net up.

  • A meter gives one number. A scope shows shape and timing. Use both.

2 · The Multimeter: Volts, Amps, Ohms

You will be able to
  • Connect a meter correctly for voltage, current, and resistance.
  • Explain what meter input resistance does to a measurement.
  • Say what an AC reading means and why true-RMS matters.

How it connects

MeasureConnect the meter…Circuit power
VoltageIn parallel, across the part (two points)On
CurrentIn series: break the circuit and put the meter in the gapOn
ResistanceAcross the part, isolated from the rest of the circuitOff

The red lead normally goes in the VΩ jack and the black lead in COM. For current, many meters use a separate jack (and a fuse). If you leave the leads in the current jack and then measure voltage, you have created a short. A good habit: after any current measurement, move the lead back.

On a DC reading, a minus sign means the red lead is on the more negative point. Nothing is wrong. You just swapped the leads.

The meter is part of your circuit

A voltmeter has input resistance. A typical digital meter has about 10 MΩ. When you connect it, that resistance is in parallel with the part you measure and steals a little current. For most circuits it does not matter. But across a high-resistance circuit it can pull the reading down noticeably.

reading = V × R2' ÷ (R1 + R2'), R2' = R2 in parallel with the meter’s input resistance
Try it: does the meter disturb the circuit?
The supply is 10 V and the meter reads across R2. Try two 100 kΩ resistors with a 10 MΩ meter, then with a 1 MΩ meter. Then try two 10 MΩ resistors. A low-impedance meter on a high-resistance circuit reads low.

AC readings and true RMS

On the AC setting the meter shows an RMS value. For a pure sine wave, RMS = peak × 0.707. Cheap meters measure the average and then multiply by a constant that is right only for sine waves. A true-RMS meter calculates the real RMS of any shape. Use true-RMS when you measure square waves, switching supplies, or anything not sinusoidal. Also check the meter's frequency range: many are only accurate up to a few hundred hertz or a few kilohertz.

The AC setting ignores DC, and the DC setting averages out AC. A signal with both, such as a ripple on a supply, needs both readings (or a scope).

Why a 17E caresBattery packs and supplies in the field are measured under load, not just open circuit. A battery that reads 12.6 V with nothing connected and 9 V under load is bad, and only the second reading tells you.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Voltage: in parallel. Current: in series. Resistance: part isolated and power off.
  • The meter has input resistance, about 10 MΩ on a typical digital meter. It loads high-resistance circuits.
  • AC reads RMS. Use a true-RMS meter for non-sine waves.

3 · Testing Parts and Reading Resistors

You will be able to
  • Read a four-band resistor.
  • Explain why resistance on a powered or connected circuit reads wrong.
  • Use continuity and the diode test to find opens, shorts, and bad diodes.

Resistor color code

Four-band resistors use the first two bands as digits, the third as a multiplier, and the fourth as tolerance. Digits: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, gray 8, white 9. Multiplier is a power of ten. Tolerance: gold ±5%, silver ±10%, none ±20%.

Try it: read the resistor

Measuring resistance: the in-circuit trap

The meter pushes a small current through the part and reads the voltage. If anything else is connected across the part, that current also flows through the other paths, so the meter reads the parallel combination, which is always lower than the part alone. To measure a part properly, power off, discharge capacitors, and lift one end of the part out of the circuit.

Try it: a part in a circuit
The meter reads the part in parallel with whatever else connects across it. Make the other path huge and the error vanishes. Make it small and the reading is dominated by it. If the circuit is powered, the reading is meaningless and can damage the meter.

Continuity and the diode test

  • Continuity beeps when the resistance is very low (typically under a few tens of ohms). It checks wires, fuses, switches, and solder joints. No beep where there should be one is an open. A beep where there should not be one is a short.
  • Diode test pushes a small current through the diode and shows its forward voltage drop. It should conduct one way and block the other.
Try it: diode test
OLDIODE
Typical readings: silicon about 0.5 to 0.7 V, Schottky about 0.15 to 0.45 V, red LED about 1.7 to 2.1 V, blue or white LED about 2.6 to 3.3 V. “OL” means open loop, which is the correct reading in the blocking direction. A shorted diode reads about 0 V both ways, and an open one reads OL both ways.

Measuring current

To measure current you put the meter in the path, so all the current goes through it. The meter has a small internal resistance, which drops a little voltage, called burden voltage. On low-voltage circuits that can disturb what you are measuring. Start on the highest current range and work down. Never exceed the meter's current rating.

Why a 17E caresCables, connectors, and fuses cause a large share of field failures. A continuity check across a cable end to end takes seconds and separates a bad cable from a bad radio.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Digits, multiplier, tolerance. Brown black red gold is 1 kΩ ±5%.
  • Measure resistance with power off and the part isolated. Connected parts read low.
  • Continuity finds opens and shorts. The diode test shows forward drop one way and OL the other.

4 · The Oscilloscope

You will be able to
  • Set volts per division, time per division, and trigger to show a stable signal.
  • Read peak-to-peak voltage, period, and frequency off the screen.
  • Explain probe attenuation, coupling, and bandwidth.

A scope draws voltage (up) against time (across). The screen is a grid. Each square is a division. Reading a scope is counting divisions and multiplying by the setting.

The controls that matter

ControlWhat it does
Volts per divisionVertical scale. 2 V/div means each square up is 2 V.
Time per divisionHorizontal scale. 1 ms/div means each square across is 1 ms.
TriggerStarts each sweep at the same point on the waveform, so a repeating signal holds still. A level and an edge (rising or falling) set the start point.
CouplingDC shows everything. AC blocks DC so you can see a small ripple on a large DC level. GND shows the zero line.
Probe settingThe scope must be told whether the probe is x1 or x10.

Reading it

peak-to-peak = divisions tall × volts/div
period = divisions per cycle × time/div, frequency = 1 ÷ period

For a sine wave, peak is half of peak-to-peak and RMS is peak × 0.707. For a square wave, duty cycle is the high time divided by the period.

Try it: read the scope
V
Count the divisions, multiply by the settings shown, then enter your answers. The centre line is 0 V.

Probes: the x10 setting

A x10 probe divides the signal by ten before it reaches the scope. It has a much higher input resistance (about 10 MΩ) and much lower capacitance (about 10 to 15 pF) than a x1 probe (about 1 MΩ and around 100 pF, mostly the cable). Lower capacitance loads the circuit less, so a x10 probe is the default choice. You must tell the scope the probe is x10, or every reading is ten times too small. Probes also need compensation: adjust them on the scope's square-wave test output until the corners are square.

Bandwidth

A scope's bandwidth is the frequency at which it reads only 70.7% of the true amplitude (−3 dB). For accurate amplitude, use a scope with at least five times the bandwidth of your signal. The sample rate must also be high enough to catch the detail: it must be well above twice the highest frequency, and typically four to five times.

Try it: bandwidth and probe loading
The scope itself reads a single-pole roll-off. The probe adds capacitance across the source, which forms a low-pass filter with the source resistance. A x10 probe loads a high-resistance circuit far less.
Why a 17E caresSpectrum analyzers and SDRs show you signals in frequency. A scope shows the same signals in time, which is how you check clock lines, power supply ripple, and pulse trains. Ripple on a radio’s supply can show up as noise on its output.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Volts/div and time/div turn grid squares into volts and seconds. Frequency = 1 ÷ period.
  • Trigger holds the picture still. AC coupling shows ripple on a DC level. Set the probe to x10 on the scope.
  • Use a scope with at least five times the signal frequency. A x10 probe loads the circuit less.

5 · Reading Waveforms and Symptoms

You will be able to
  • Recognize clipping, offset, ripple, noise, and a dead signal on a scope.
  • Match a symptom to likely causes in a power supply or filter.
  • Predict ripple in a simple supply.

A waveform is a symptom. The shape tells you which kind of fault to look for.

You seeLikely meaning
Flat line at 0 VNo signal or no power. Check the source, supply, and the path before it.
Flat line at a high DC levelA stuck output, or the signal is blocked and you are only seeing bias.
Clean shape, wrong sizeGain error, a loose probe, or a wrong probe setting.
Flat tops or bottoms (clipping)The signal is too big for the supply or stage and runs out of room.
Shifted up or down (offset)DC bias is wrong, or you are using DC coupling and a DC level is added.
Fuzz riding on the lineNoise, interference, or poor grounding. A long ground lead makes it worse.
Sawtooth bumps on a DC levelRipple from a rectifier supply. Too big means a weak filter capacitor or too much load.
Rounded square edgesToo much capacitance or too little bandwidth. Check the probe and the circuit.
Try it: what is wrong with this waveform?

Ripple in a rectifier supply

A half-wave rectifier charges the filter capacitor on each peak. Between peaks the capacitor supplies the load and sags. The size of the sag is the ripple. For a simple supply it follows from how long the capacitor has to hold the load:

ripple (peak-to-peak) ≈ Iload ÷ ( f × C ) f = 60 Hz for half-wave, 120 Hz for full-wave
Worked example

A half-wave supply feeds a 1 kΩ load at about 10 V, so the load current is about 10 mA. The filter is 100 µF and f = 60 Hz.

Ripple ≈ 0.010 ÷ (60 × 0.0001) = 1.7 V peak-to-peak. A full-wave bridge doubles f and halves the ripple to about 0.8 V. Doubling the capacitor also halves it.

Try it: how much ripple?
The estimate is for the ripple on a lightly loaded simple supply, which is why it is a rough guide and not a specification.
Why a 17E caresSupply ripple and noise leak into radios and receivers. If a receiver is deaf only when it is on its charger or generator, a scope on the supply line is the fastest way to see why.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Flat zero means no signal or power. Flat tops mean clipping. Fuzz means noise. Sawtooth on DC means ripple.
  • Ripple ≈ load current ÷ (f × C). Full-wave halves it by doubling f.
  • Use AC coupling to see small ripple on a large DC level.

6 · Systematic Troubleshooting

You will be able to
  • Follow a repeatable troubleshooting sequence.
  • Use the half-split method to find a fault in few measurements.
  • Tell the common fault types apart by their readings.

The sequence

  1. Observe the symptom. What exactly is wrong, and when? Is it steady or intermittent?
  2. Check the easy things first. Power, connections, fuses, switches, settings.
  3. Know what is normal. A schematic, a manual, or a working unit gives expected values.
  4. Split the circuit. Measure in the middle and decide which half contains the fault.
  5. Repeat in the faulty half until you have isolated one stage or one part.
  6. Confirm the cause. Do not just replace a part: show why it is bad.
  7. Fix it, test it, and record it.

Half-split: a measurement is a question

If a signal passes through eight stages and the output is dead, you could check each stage in order, and it could take eight measurements. A better way is to measure after stage 4. If the signal is good there, the fault is in stages 5 to 8. If not, it is in stages 1 to 4. Each measurement halves the search, so eight stages take at most three.

measurements needed ≈ log2(number of stages), rounded up
Try it: find the dead stage

The signal goes in at the left. One stage is dead. Tap a test point to see whether the signal reaches it.

Try measuring in the middle of whatever range is still possible. Compare your count with the best possible.

Common faults and what they read

The open case is the one that surprises people: across an open part you read the full supply voltage, because no current flows to drop voltage on the other parts. A reading of 9 V across a resistor that should drop 3 V is a strong clue that something in series with it is open.

Why a 17E caresThe same method works on a radio, an antenna feed, a network cable, or a software problem: observe, split, measure, confirm. The half-split idea is also how you narrow a problem down in a network.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Observe, check the easy things, know the normal, split, repeat, confirm, fix.
  • Half-split: each measurement halves the possibilities. N stages need about log2N measurements.
  • An open shows full voltage across the gap and no current. A short shows near zero volts and extra current.

Try it: predict the divider

A fault from symptom to cause

Divider board: the LED is dark

Symptom. The LED branch is dark. The 9 V battery is connected and the switch is on.

Expect. VCC = 9 V, midpoint = 6 V, LED anode = about 2 V and the LED lit.

Measure. VCC reads 9.0 V, so the supply is fine. The LED anode reads 9 V, not 2 V.

Reason. The anode is at the full supply voltage, so no current is flowing and nothing is dropping voltage across R3. If R3 were open, nothing would feed the anode and it would read 0 V. Because the anode sees the full 9 V, R3 is passing the voltage along, and the open must be after it: the LED.

Confirm. Power off and use the diode test across the LED: it reads OL both ways. The LED is open.

Fix. Replace the LED with the right polarity, and check that R3 limits the current to a safe level (about 15 mA here).

Check yourself
Summary
  • Predict, then measure. Pick the instrument that shows the property you care about.
  • Meter for exact DC values, continuity, and parts. Scope for shape, ripple, timing, and phase.
  • Walk from symptom, to expectation, to measurement, to reasoning, to confirmation, to fix.
