1 · What an Order Is

You will be able to
  • Tell a warning order, an OPORD, and a FRAGORD apart.
  • Give the five paragraphs of the order in sequence.
  • Say why a standard format exists.

An operation order (OPORD) tells subordinates what the unit will do, why, and how they fit in. It is written to be understood quickly, by tired people, in the same place every time. Because every order uses the same five paragraphs, a Soldier who has read one knows where to look for the mission, the timeline, or the radio plan in the next.

ParagraphNameAnswers
1SituationWhat is going on? Enemy, friendly, and attachments.
2MissionWhat must we do, and why? The five Ws.
3ExecutionHow will we do it? Intent, concept, tasks, coordinating instructions.
4SustainmentWhat supports us? Logistics, personnel, medical.
5Command and SignalWho is in charge, and how do we communicate?

A memory aid is SMESC: Situation, Mission, Execution, Sustainment, Command and Signal.

Three kinds of order

  • Warning order (WARNORD): an early heads-up so units can start preparing. It is short and goes out as soon as there is something useful to say.
  • OPORD: the full five-paragraph order.
  • Fragmentary order (FRAGORD): a quick change to an order already in effect. It gives only what has changed.

Squad and platoon leaders write the order during troop leading procedures (see the TLP guide, steps 2, 6, and 7). Battalion and higher staffs produce it at the end of the MDMP. The format in this guide is the small-unit version. Higher echelons use the same five paragraphs with more subparagraphs and annexes.

2 · Paragraph 1: Situation

You will be able to
  • Name the parts of paragraph 1.
  • Say what “most likely” and “most dangerous” mean for the enemy.
  • Put a statement in the right paragraph.

Paragraph 1 gives the picture: who is out there, who is with you, and what is attached.

  • a. Enemy forces. Composition, disposition, and strength; capabilities; recent activity; and the enemy’s most likely and most dangerous courses of action. Weather and terrain are often summarized here.
  • b. Friendly forces. The higher unit’s mission and intent (one and two levels up), the units on your left and right, and units in support.
  • c. Attachments and detachments. What was added to or taken from your unit for this operation.
  • d. Civil considerations (when it matters): civilians, key structures, and local conditions.

Say what you know and what you do not. Mark an estimate as an estimate. Paragraph 1 is not where you say what you will do. That starts in paragraph 2.

Which paragraph?

The commonest mistake with order format is putting a thing in the wrong paragraph. Try sorting these.

3 · Paragraph 2: Mission

You will be able to
  • Write a mission statement with all five Ws.
  • Spot what is missing from a faulty statement.
  • Say why the mission has no “how”.

The mission is one sentence, and it answers five questions:

WAnswersExample
WhoThe unit1st Squad, 2nd Platoon
WhatThe taskconducts a reconnaissance patrol
WhenTimeNLT 0600 on 14 June
WhereLocationof OBJ RAVEN, vicinity grid 123456
WhyPurposeto confirm enemy strength before the platoon’s attack

The mission says what and why, not how. The “how” is paragraph 3. The purpose matters most: if the plan falls apart, subordinates who know the why can still do something useful.

The same mission statement appears in the WARNORD (once it is known) and in paragraph 2 of the OPORD.

Try it: what is wrong with this mission?
Why a 17E caresA mission with a clear “why” also tells you what the spectrum is for: protect a link, deny one, or watch one. Without it you cannot choose your EW task.
Check yourself
Summary
  • A mission has five Ws: who, what, when, where, and why.
  • The mission states the task and the purpose, not the method.
  • The purpose lets subordinates act when the plan fails.

4 · Paragraph 3: Execution

You will be able to
  • Write a commander’s intent with purpose, key tasks, and end state.
  • Tell the intent from the concept of operations.
  • Name what goes in coordinating instructions.

Paragraph 3 is the heart of the order. It says how the mission will be done.

  • a. Commander’s intent. A short statement of the purpose of the operation, the key tasks that must be done, and the end state (the desired result). It is the part subordinates use when the plan no longer fits.
  • b. Concept of operations. The scheme of maneuver and how the pieces fit: which element does what, in what order.
  • c. Tasks to subordinate units. What each element must do.
  • d. Coordinating instructions. Things that apply to everyone: timeline, CCIR, rules of engagement, risk and safety, EEFI, and so on.

The intent is not a restatement of the concept. If you could change the whole scheme of maneuver and the intent would still be true, it is a good intent.

5 · Paragraphs 4 and 5: Sustainment, Command and Signal

You will be able to
  • Say what goes in sustainment.
  • Say what goes in command and in signal.
  • Name the parts of a PACE plan.

4. Sustainment

  • a. Logistics: supply (food, water, ammunition, fuel, batteries), transport, and maintenance.
  • b. Personnel: casualty and prisoner handling.
  • c. Health service support: medical plan, casualty collection, and MEDEVAC (see the 9-Line MEDEVAC & TCCC guide).

Batteries are logistics too. A radio that dies at the objective is a sustainment failure.

5. Command and Signal

  • a. Command: where the leader and key leaders will be, and the succession of command, who takes over if the leader goes down.
  • b. Signal: the SOI (frequencies, call signs, and codes), the nets and who is on them, pyrotechnic and visual signals, challenge and password, and the PACE plan.

A PACE plan names a primary way to communicate, an alternate, a contingency, and an emergency. A common set for a small unit: FM voice, then a backup net or data, then visual signals, then a runner or pre-set meeting point. It also lists what to do about jamming. Short EW and spectrum plans fit here, and longer ones go in an annex (the Signal annex or the cyber and EW annex).

6 · Delivering the Order

You will be able to
  • Plan the timeline backward from execution.
  • Say how to brief and how to confirm understanding.
  • Say how orders connect to TLP and MDMP.

A good order delivered too late is a bad order. Work the clock backward from the time you must move.

leader planning time ≤ total time ÷ 3 subordinate time ≥ total time × 2 ÷ 3

The one-third, two-thirds rule leaves two thirds of the time for subordinates to prepare, rehearse, and inspect. Time you spend perfecting the plan comes straight out of theirs.

Try it: timeline calculator
Hours after the mission is received until departure. Latest times assume the leader uses no more than one third.

Giving the order

  1. Orient everyone to the ground with a terrain model, sketch, or map, so people can see what you describe.
  2. Brief, do not just read. Look at your team. Keep it short enough to remember: the leader may have a few minutes, and the team will hold it in their heads.
  3. Ask for questions, and ask some of your own.
  4. Confirm understanding. A back-brief has subordinates tell the plan back to you in their own words.
  5. Rehearse. Walk through it. Rehearsals find the gaps that reading never will.

The commonest failure is an order that is complete but not understood. Check understanding more than you check format.

How it fits

In TLP, the order comes in steps 2 (WARNORD), 6 (complete the plan), and 7 (issue the order), then step 8 supervises and rehearses. In the MDMP, step 7 produces and issues the order after the commander approves a course of action.
