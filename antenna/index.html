◆ Building Principle
An antenna is a tuned conductor that converts RF current into radiated EM waves (and vice versa — reciprocity). Its physical length is set by the operating wavelength. Get the length right, feed it at the correct impedance point, and use a balun to keep RF off the feedline shield. Everything else — gain, directivity, bandwidth — comes from how you arrange multiple elements.

◆ How a Yagi Works
Only the driven element connects to the feedline. The reflector (longer, behind) and directors (shorter, in front) are parasitic — they re-radiate the field with a phase shift that reinforces forward and cancels backward, steering energy toward the directors. More directors = more gain and a tighter beam, with diminishing returns.

BUILD TIP: Elements mount perpendicular to the boom, all in the same plane. A metal boom slightly lengthens elements (~1–3%); insulated/through-boom mounting changes the correction. Keep the driven element insulated from a metal boom unless using a gamma match.

◆ Reading the Pattern
The big forward lobe is where the antenna "hears" and transmits best — point it at the target. The small rear lobe is suppressed by the front-to-back ratio. Side lobes are weak off-axis responses. For direction finding, the sharp main lobe gives bearing; the deep nulls off the sides can be even more precise than the peak.
NOTE: These are first-order estimates from standard design ratios. Real builds vary with element diameter, boom effects, and tuning. Use them to compare configurations and as a starting point — confirm with a field-strength check or analyzer.

◆ Why Matching Is Needed
A lone dipole is ~73 Ω, but adding a close reflector and directors drops the driven element's feed impedance to roughly 20–35 Ω. Feeding 50 Ω coax straight into that raises SWR and reflects power. A matching network transforms the low feed impedance up to 50 Ω, and a balun stops feedline-shield radiation that would distort the pattern.
