1 · What “Software-Defined” Means

You will be able to
  • Describe what an SDR does in hardware and what it leaves to software.
  • Explain I and Q samples and why two numbers are needed.
  • Say how ADC bits set the receiver’s dynamic range.

A traditional radio has circuits wired for one job: a filter, a mixer, a demodulator, each built for one kind of signal. A software-defined radio keeps the hardware simple. It turns radio waves into a stream of numbers, and a computer does everything else.

StageJobWhere
AntennaCatches the signalOutside
RF front endFilter, amplify, and shift the chosen slice down to a low frequencyHardware
ADCTurns the voltage into numbersHardware
Demodulation, filtering, decoding, displayEverything after thatSoftware

That is why the same $30 dongle can play an FM station, decode aircraft position reports, or listen to a ham repeater: only the program changes.

I and Q

One number per sample is not enough to describe a radio wave. A wave has a strength and a phase, so each sample is stored as two numbers, I (in-phase) and Q (quadrature). Together they place a point on a circle: its distance from the centre is the amplitude, and its angle is the phase. With both you can rebuild AM (amplitude), FM (how the angle changes), and digital modes after the fact.

I = A × cos(φ) Q = A × sin(φ) A = √(I² + Q²) φ = atan2(Q, I)
Try it: IQ phasor
The point turns when the signal is not exactly on the tuned frequency. Its turning rate is the offset. Set the offset to 0 and it holds still, because the signal sits at the centre frequency.

ADC bits and dynamic range

The ADC measures each sample with a fixed number of steps. More bits means finer steps, and a bigger gap between the loudest signal it can hold and the quantization noise floor. An ideal ADC gives about 6 dB per bit.

ideal SNR (dB) ≈ 6.02 × bits + 1.76

An 8-bit ADC (a typical $30 dongle) gives about 50 dB, a 12-bit ADC about 74 dB, a 14-bit ADC about 86 dB. Real devices do a little worse. This is why a strong nearby signal can bury weak ones on a cheap SDR.

Why a 17E caresSDRs are how most modern receivers and EW sensors are built. Knowing the signal path tells you where each limit comes from: the antenna, the front end, the ADC, or the software.

Summary
  • An SDR does only the radio-to-numbers step in hardware. Everything after that is software.
  • Each sample is I and Q. Amplitude = √(I²+Q²) and phase = atan2(Q, I).
  • Ideal ADC dynamic range ≈ 6.02 dB per bit, plus 1.76 dB.

3 · Sample Rate, Span, and Tuning

You will be able to
  • Explain how sample rate sets the span you see.
  • Work out FFT resolution and the time per waterfall line.
  • Tell whether a signal sits inside the tuned span.

An SDR does not tune like a dial. It captures one block of spectrum at a time. Two numbers define that block:

  • Center frequency: the middle of the block.
  • Sample rate: for IQ samples, the sample rate in samples per second equals the span in hertz. At 2.4 MS/s you see 2.4 MHz, from 1.2 MHz below centre to 1.2 MHz above.

The software turns each block into a spectrum with an FFT of N points. Each point (a bin) is one slice of the span:

span = sample rate bin width = sample rate ÷ N time per FFT line = N ÷ sample rate

A bigger FFT gives narrower bins, so you can separate close signals, but each line takes longer, so quick bursts smear. You cannot have both. Bin width × time per line is always 1.

Signals near the edge of the span are weak and may show an image or roll-off. The tuner also makes a spike at the exact centre (the DC spike). Do not read that as a signal. Tune slightly off, and use the middle of the span.

4 · Gain, Noise, and Overload

You will be able to
  • Explain what gain does to weak and strong signals.
  • Say what overload looks like and how to fix it.
  • Pick a gain setting for a given situation.

Gain amplifies the signal before the ADC. It is a balancing act:

  • Too low: a weak signal never rises above the ADC’s quantization noise and disappears.
  • Too high: a strong signal exceeds the ADC’s full scale and clips. That makes false signals (spurs and images) all across the display and can bury the real ones.

The usual method: start low, raise gain until the noise floor just lifts a little, and stop. If strange signals appear in a pattern, or the whole display fills with junk when you tune near a strong transmitter, reduce gain.

Gain helps only up to a point: it raises the signal and the front-end noise together. Once the front-end noise is above the ADC noise, more gain gains nothing.

5 · Demodulation Modes

You will be able to
  • Match a signal to the right demodulation mode.
  • Say what happens when you pick the wrong one.
  • Give typical bandwidths for the common modes.

The SDR cannot guess how the information is carried. You tell the software, and it applies the matching math to the I and Q samples. Wrong mode means wrong sound.

ModeTypical bandwidthUsed for
AM6 to 10 kHz (aviation voice about 8 kHz)AM broadcast, airband voice
NFM (narrow FM)12.5 to 25 kHzLand mobile, public safety, amateur FM
WFM (wide FM)about 200 kHzFM broadcast
USB / LSB (single sideband)about 2.4 to 3 kHzHF voice, amateur and some military. By convention, LSB below 10 MHz and USB above.
CWabout 100 to 500 HzMorse code

Single sideband sounds like cartoon chatter when slightly off tune, because the carrier is missing. Fine-tune until the voice is natural. A narrowband filter on a wideband signal chops the audio. A wideband filter on a narrow signal lets in extra noise. Digital modes need a decoder program on top of the demodulated audio.

6 · Waterfall, Antennas, and Recording

You will be able to
  • Read the main features of a waterfall.
  • Say why the antenna matters more than the SDR.
  • Estimate the size of an IQ recording.

A waterfall shows frequency across, time down, and brightness for power. A steady vertical streak is a fixed-frequency signal. A streak that jumps hops. A diagonal line is a sweep. Short bright dashes are bursts. The Signal ID Explained guide covers reading these in depth.

The antenna comes first

The stock antenna on a dongle is a compromise. A longer or better-matched antenna for your band, mounted high and clear, often does more than a better SDR. Cables and USB hubs add noise: put the dongle on a short USB extension away from the computer, and keep the antenna away from it.

  • Frequency error: cheap dongles drift. A PPM correction shifts the display so a known signal lands where it should.
  • Bias tee: a small DC voltage up the cable for an active antenna or amplifier. Turn it on only when the antenna needs it.

Recording

An IQ recording stores every sample. The file is large.

bytes per second = sample rate × 2 (I and Q) × bytes per value

7 · Law, Responsibility, and Putting It Together

You will be able to
  • Separate receiving from transmitting in law and practice.
  • Name the main limits on what you may listen to.
  • Plan a listening session from start to finish.

This is a general overview, not legal advice. Laws differ by place, and military and government networks have their own rules. Ask your unit and your legal office if you are unsure.

Receiving

In the United States, listening to many signals is lawful: broadcast, aviation, weather, amateur radio, and a good deal of public safety traffic (though some jurisdictions restrict mobile scanners or certain services). Limits include cellular calls, which federal law protects from interception, private communications, which you may not use or pass on, and decrypting anything encrypted. A receive-only dongle does not give you permission to do any of that.

Transmitting

Transmitting needs authority for the frequency and power you use: for example an amateur licence for the amateur bands, or a licence for any other service. Transmit-capable hardware gives you no permission. Unlicensed devices follow technical limits (FCC Part 15 in the United States). Interfering with licensed users, or jamming, is illegal outside of authorised military use.

A listening session

  1. Know your rules and your purpose.
  2. Choose an antenna and a clear spot.
  3. Set centre frequency, sample rate, and FFT size for the band.
  4. Start gain low and raise it to just lift the noise floor.
  5. Find signals on the waterfall, then pick the mode and filter.
  6. Log what you find and record only what you need and are allowed to keep.
