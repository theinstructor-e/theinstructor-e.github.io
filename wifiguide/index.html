1 · What Wi-Fi Is

You will be able to
  • Say what an access point, an SSID, and a BSSID are.
  • Explain why Wi-Fi is a shared, half-duplex medium.
  • Compare how the three bands lose signal with distance.

Wi-Fi is a set of radio standards, the IEEE 802.11 family, for a local wireless network. The name “Wi-Fi” belongs to the Wi-Fi Alliance, which certifies that devices work together. It is a link-layer technology: it carries ordinary Ethernet-style traffic over radio, and everything above it (IP, TCP, DNS) works the same as on a cable.

TermMeaning
Access point (AP)The radio that clients join. A home “router” usually includes one.
SSIDThe network name people see, such as “HomeWiFi.” Many APs can share one SSID.
BSSIDThe MAC address of one AP radio. It identifies the actual device, even if the SSID is shared or spoofed.
BSS / ESSOne AP and its clients, or several APs with the same SSID that act as one network.

One shared channel

All devices on a channel take turns on the same frequency. Wi-Fi is half duplex: a radio sends or listens, not both at once. Each device listens first and waits if the channel is busy. So speed is shared, and one slow or distant device can drag the others down (Chapter 4).

Three bands

  • 2.4 GHz: longest reach and best through walls, but crowded (microwaves, Bluetooth, baby monitors) and only three clear channels.
  • 5 GHz: more channels and faster, shorter reach.
  • 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E and 7): the newest and cleanest, with the most room, and the shortest reach.

Free-space path loss rises with frequency: FSPL = 32.44 + 20·log10(f in MHz) + 20·log10(d in km). The same distance costs about 6.4 dB more at 5 GHz and about 8 dB more at 6 GHz than at 2.4 GHz, before walls are counted.

Free space only, with no walls or people. Real indoor loss is higher, and it is higher again at 5 and 6 GHz because walls absorb more at higher frequencies.

The headline rate is the PHY rate: the speed on the air, with the best conditions, the most streams, and no overhead. It comes from a formula you can calculate:

PHY rate = data subcarriers × bits per symbol × coding rate × spatial streams ÷ symbol time

Wider channels carry more subcarriers. Higher-order QAM packs more bits into each. More MIMO streams multiply the rate. Newer standards also shorten the guard time between symbols. Real throughput is typically 50 to 70 percent of PHY rate or less, because every frame costs headers, acknowledgements, and waiting turns, and because rates fall as signal weakens.

3 · Bands and Channels

You will be able to
  • Work out channel frequencies and which 2.4 GHz channels overlap.
  • Count how many channels each band holds.
  • Explain DFS and why wide channels cost room.

2.4 GHz

Channels are 5 MHz apart, but a Wi-Fi signal is about 20 MHz wide, so neighbouring channels overlap heavily. Channel n is centred at 2407 + 5n MHz (channel 6 is 2437 MHz). To avoid overlap you need to be at least 25 MHz apart, which in the US leaves just 1, 6, and 11.

5 GHz and 6 GHz

5 GHz has about 25 usable 20 MHz channels in the US. Some (the UNII-2 groups) must give way to weather and military radar using DFS (dynamic frequency selection): if the AP hears radar, it must leave the channel. 6 GHz holds up to 59 channels of 20 MHz in the US, and needs no DFS in most cases.

Width costs room. Doubling the channel width roughly doubles the speed but halves how many channels fit and makes it more likely that you hear neighbours. In busy places, 20 MHz on 2.4 GHz and 40 or 80 MHz on 5 GHz are common choices.

4 · How a Frame Gets on the Air

You will be able to
  • Describe how a client finds and joins a network.
  • Explain CSMA/CA and why a slow client slows everyone.
  • Name the three frame types.

Finding and joining

  1. Discovery. APs send beacon frames about every 100 ms announcing the SSID, rates, and security. A client can listen passively, or send a probe request and get a probe response.
  2. Authentication. A short open-system exchange (or SAE under WPA3).
  3. Association. The client asks to join and gets an ID.
  4. Security handshake. For WPA2 and WPA3 personal, the 4-way handshake sets the encryption keys.
  5. Data. Encrypted traffic flows.

Frames come in three types: management (beacon, probe, authentication, association, deauthentication), control (RTS, CTS, acknowledgements), and data.

Taking turns

Wi-Fi uses CSMA/CA: listen, wait a random short time if busy, send, and wait for an acknowledgement. No ACK means a retry at a lower rate. Two devices that cannot hear each other (the hidden node problem) can collide at the AP. RTS/CTS reserves the air to help.

5 · Wi-Fi Security

You will be able to
  • Rank Wi-Fi security modes from broken to strong.
  • Say what protected management frames do.
  • Say why a hidden SSID and MAC filtering are not real security.
ModeStatusNotes
OpenNo protectionAnyone can read traffic. (OWE adds encryption to open networks.)
WEPBrokenCracked in minutes. Never use.
WPA (TKIP)ObsoleteStopgap after WEP. Avoid.
WPA2-Personal (AES-CCMP)Acceptable with a long passphraseA captured handshake can be attacked offline, so a weak passphrase fails.
WPA3-Personal (SAE)BetterStops offline guessing of a captured handshake. Protected management frames required.
WPA2/WPA3-Enterprise (802.1X)Strongest for organizationsEach user has own credentials through a RADIUS server.

Protected management frames (802.11w) authenticate management frames such as deauthentication, so they cannot be easily forged. Without them, anyone can send a fake deauth that kicks a client off.

False comforts

  • Hidden SSID. The beacon omits the name, but it appears in probe requests and association frames. It hides nothing from a listener and adds trouble for clients.
  • MAC filtering. MAC addresses are sent in the clear and can be copied.
  • WPS PIN. A short PIN that can be guessed. Turn it off.

The strength of a personal-mode passphrase is its randomness. Bits of entropy = length × log2(number of possible characters), if chosen at random.

A Wi-Fi radio in monitor mode hears every frame on a channel without joining a network. A passive survey cycles through the channels and records what it hears.

Evil twin and forged deauth

An evil twin is an AP made to look like a real one: same SSID, different device. Clues: a BSSID you do not know, a different security mode (an open copy of a WPA2 network), an odd channel, unusually strong signal, and often a burst of deauthentication frames aimed at real clients to push them over. A forged deauth looks like a flood of deauth frames from the AP’s own BSSID that the AP did not send.

Signal strength to distance

RSSI = P1m − 10 × n × log10(distance in m) so distance = 10(P1m − RSSI) ÷ (10n)

The exponent n is about 2 in free space and 3 to 4 indoors. You rarely know it, so a single RSSI gives only a rough range. Moving the receiver and taking several readings (and a directional antenna) finds a direction.

The three estimates use different path loss exponents. The spread between them is why one reading cannot be trusted.

7 · Put It Together

You will be able to

Rules of thumb for signal

RSSIQuality
−30 to −55 dBmExcellent
−56 to −67 dBmGood for video and calls
−68 to −75 dBmFair: web works, expect slowdowns
weaker than −80 dBmPoor or unusable

What matters is SNR, the signal above the noise floor. About 25 dB or more is good. Noise from neighbours and other devices raises the floor.

Worked example

A two-stream Wi-Fi 6 laptop on an 80 MHz channel

PHY rate at best = 980 × 10 bits × 5/6 × 2 streams ÷ 13.6 µs = about 1,201 Mb/s. In practice: at a good signal it will hold perhaps 60 percent, near 700 Mb/s. At fair signal the radio drops to lower modulation, and a few hundred Mb/s is normal. If a distant client is also on the AP, everyone slows (Chapter 4).

Diagnose it

Most bad Wi-Fi comes from signal, channel, or sharing, not the internet service.
