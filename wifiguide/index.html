🤔 Why "Wi-Fi"? It doesn't actually stand for anything official! The Wi-Fi Alliance just liked how it sounded — like "Hi-Fi" (high fidelity) for music. People assume it means "Wireless Fidelity," but that was made up after the name was already chosen.
📛 Why the new naming? Names like "802.11ac" were confusing for shoppers. In 2018 the Wi-Fi Alliance started using simple numbers — Wi-Fi 4, 5, 6, 7 — so people could tell at a glance which one is newer.
🔢 Why the weird letters? Each new Wi-Fi standard got the next unused letter in the alphabet from the IEEE committee — skipping ones already used for other engineering standards, which is why you see gaps and odd jumps.
🛰️ Wi-Fi vs cellular data: Wi-Fi is free once you're connected to a router, while cellular data (4G/5G) comes from cell towers and usually costs money through your phone plan. Wi-Fi is also typically faster for short distances.

🔐 Wi-Fi security — how your network gets locked
WEP
Wired Equivalent Privacy (1997)
The very first Wi-Fi security. It's so weak that hackers can crack it in minutes using free tools. Nobody should still be using this!
WPA
Wi-Fi Protected Access (2003)
A quick fix for WEP's problems. Better, but people later found ways to break into it too.
WPA2
Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 (2004)
A huge security upgrade using a strong scrambling method called AES — the same one banks use! Most routers still use this today, and it's considered safe.
WPA3
Wi-Fi Protected Access 3 (2018)
The newest and strongest. Makes it nearly impossible to guess your password through repeated attempts, and protects you even better on public Wi-Fi.

When you set up a router, always pick WPA2 or WPA3 — never WEP! Check your router's password settings to make sure you're not stuck on something outdated.

LAN
Local Area Network
A LAN is a network that covers a small area — like your house, a classroom, or a single office floor. Your Wi-Fi router creates a LAN! All the devices connected to it (your tablet, phone, smart TV) are part of the same LAN, and they can talk to each other directly without going through the internet at all.

How it connects

Devices on a LAN connect using Wi-Fi or Ethernet cables, all plugged into the same router or switch. Because everything is so close together, a LAN can move data almost instantly — that's why local file transfers feel instant compared to downloading from the internet.

Cool facts

  • The first LAN, called Ethernet, was invented at Xerox PARC in 1973 by Robert Metcalfe.
  • A LAN doesn't need the internet to work — two devices on the same Wi-Fi can send files to each other even if the internet is down!
  • Most homes have one LAN, but big offices can have dozens of separate LANs joined together.
🌆
MAN
Metropolitan Area Network
A MAN is bigger than a LAN — it covers a whole city or a big campus, like connecting every school building in a school district, or every traffic light in a city. It's too big for one Wi-Fi router, so it usually uses underground cables or special long-range wireless links to connect lots of LANs together across town.

How it connects

A MAN usually relies on fiber optic cables buried under city streets, or special microwave towers placed on tall buildings. These links carry huge amounts of data between neighborhoods, connecting smaller LANs across town into one bigger network.

Cool facts

  • Cable TV companies actually run a type of MAN — the same cables that bring you TV channels often carry your home internet too.

  • MANs are the "middle layer" of the internet — they gather traffic from many LANs before sending it out across a WAN.

A WAN is the biggest network of all — it can stretch across countries or even the whole planet! The internet itself is the largest WAN that exists. It connects millions of smaller LANs and MANs together using undersea cables, satellites, and giant data centers. When you video call a friend in another country, your message travels across a WAN.

How it connects

WANs rely on enormous undersea fiber optic cables (the longest stretch over 25,000 miles!), satellites orbiting the planet, and giant data centers full of servers. No single company owns the whole WAN — instead, thousands of internet providers around the world agree to connect their networks together, forming the global internet.

Cool facts

  • There are over 500 undersea cables crossing the world's oceans right now, carrying almost all international internet traffic.
  • A message sent from the US to Japan can travel through cables on the ocean floor and arrive in well under a second.
  • Companies with offices in many countries build their own private WANs to connect all their buildings securely.
  • Even Wi-Fi satellites like Starlink are part of a WAN — they beam internet down to areas with no cables at all.

💡 Fiber optics — the light-speed cables behind it all
A fiber optic cable is a super thin strand of glass — about as thin as a human hair — that carries data as pulses of light instead of electricity. The light bounces back and forth along the inside of the glass, kind of like a ball bouncing down a long hallway, and it barely loses any strength even after traveling huge distances. This is what makes fiber the backbone of MANs and WANs: it's how undersea cables connect entire continents, and how your home internet "fiber" connection beams data into your house at incredible speeds.

Old-school copper cables (like the ones used for telephones) send data as electrical signals, which weaken over distance and can pick up interference from nearby power lines. Light doesn't have that problem — it just keeps zooming along the glass. That's why "fiber internet" plans advertised by ISPs are usually the fastest and most reliable option available to homes, often reaching 1 Gbps or more.

📍
Single-Mode Fiber
Used for long distances
Has an extremely thin glass middle, so the light travels in almost one straight line. This keeps the light strong over huge distances, which is why it's used for undersea cables and connections between cities.
🔀
Multi-Mode Fiber
Used for short distances
Has a wider glass middle, letting light bounce around more on its way through. It's cheaper to make and easier to install, which is why it's used inside buildings and data centers where distances are much shorter.

What actually creates the light? Tiny lasers or LEDs at one end of the cable flicker on and off incredibly fast — billions of times per second — turning your data into a pattern of light flashes. A sensor at the other end reads those flashes and turns them back into the data your device needs. On really long cables, special boxes are placed along the way to "re-boost" the light, kind of like a relay runner passing a baton, so the signal stays strong the whole trip.

How does fiber reach an actual house? The fiber company runs a cable from their building all the way to a small box on the outside or inside of your house. That box turns the light flashes back into the kind of signal your router understands, and from there your home Wi-Fi takes over using the standards covered earlier in this guide!

An IP address (Internet Protocol address) is like a house address, but for a device on a network. Every phone, laptop, and smart fridge gets one so that data knows exactly where to go. Without an IP address, the internet wouldn't know the difference between your tablet and your neighbor's! Most home devices use IPv4 addresses like the one above — four numbers between 0 and 255, separated by dots. Newer IPv6 addresses look different and use letters too. That's because the world is running out of IPv4 addresses to hand out — there are over 8 billion people but only about 4.3 billion IPv4 addresses!
✂️ CIDR — slicing up a block of addresses
192.168.1.0/24
Network part — stays the same for every device on this network Host part — the unique part that changes per device
CIDR stands for Classless Inter-Domain Routing — that's a mouthful, so let's skip the name and focus on what it actually does. CIDR is just a quick way to say "how big is this group of addresses?" The number after the slash tells you how many addresses are in that group. A bigger number after the slash means a smaller group of addresses. A smaller number means a bigger group. It's a bit like splitting a big box of crayons: a smaller label leaves more crayons inside for everyone to use!
🧮 Why is it written as a number like /24? Here's how it's worked out
1
Every IP address is secretly made of 32 tiny switches called bits, each either ON (1) or OFF (0). They're grouped into four sets of 8 — that's why an IP address has four numbers separated by dots, like 192.168.1.0. Each group of 8 bits is called an "octet."
2
The number after the slash (like /24) tells you exactly how many of those 32 bits, counting from the LEFT, are "locked" as the network part. The rest are free for individual devices to use as the host part.
3
So /24 means the first 24 bits (the first three octets: 192.168.1) are locked, leaving the last 8 bits (the last octet) free. That's exactly 2⁸ = 256 possible combinations for the host part — which is why a /24 always gives you 256 addresses.
4
The math behind every CIDR size is the same trick: count how many bits are left over for hosts, then calculate 2 raised to that number. A /28 leaves 4 bits free (32 − 28 = 4), so it gets 2⁴ = 16 addresses. A /30 leaves just 2 bits free, so it gets 2² = 4 addresses.

192 . 168 . 1 . 0 — the first 24 bits (purple) are locked as the network, the last 8 bits (orange) are free for up to 256 devices

Why does this matter in real life? This bit-counting system is exactly why CIDR is so flexible — instead of being stuck with fixed-size "classes" like networks used in the 1980s, any network can be sized to fit exactly how many devices it actually needs, with nothing wasted. That's the whole point of the word "Classless" in CIDR's name!

🎭 The subnet mask — CIDR's "translated" twin

Older devices and some network settings still ask for a subnet mask instead of a CIDR number — it's the exact same information, just written differently. A subnet mask turns every "locked" network bit into a 1 and every "free" host bit into a 0, then writes the whole 32-bit pattern back out as four dotted numbers, just like an IP address.

/24 = 255.255.255.0

Each 255 means "every bit in this octet is locked" (8 ones in a row), while the final 0 means "every bit here is free" (8 zeros in a row). That's why /24 and 255.255.255.0 always mean the exact same thing — they're just two different ways of writing the same 32-bit pattern!

🔢 ON and OFF — building the mask bit by bit

Here's the actual rule: starting from the LEFT side of the address, flip bits ON (1) for as many positions as the CIDR number says, then everything after that is OFF (0). No skipping around — it's always a solid block of 1s followed by a solid block of 0s. Once you have your 32 ones-and-zeros, chop them into four groups of 8 and convert each group to its decimal number to get the dotted mask.

/24 = 255.255.255.0
11111111 11111111 11111111 00000000
/26 = 255.255.255.192
11111111 11111111 11111111 11000000
/28 = 255.255.255.240
11111111 11111111 11111111 11110000

Notice the last octet in each example: 11111111 = 255, 11000000 = 192, and 11110000 = 240. Each ON bit (from left to right within that octet) is worth 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, then 1 — add up only the ON ones and you get the decimal number. For /26, the first two bits in that last octet are ON (128 + 64 = 192). For /28, the first four bits are ON (128 + 64 + 32 + 16 = 240). That's the entire trick behind every subnet mask on this page!

🔍 Going backwards — finding the CIDR from an IP address

So far we've gone CIDR → mask. But what if someone hands you a subnet mask (or you see one in your router settings) and you need to figure out the CIDR number? Just run the whole process in reverse!

1
Take the subnet mask, like 255.255.255.224, and convert each of the four numbers back into 8 binary bits (1s and 0s).
2
Count every 1 across all 32 bits, left to right. Stop counting the moment you hit the first 0 — remember, a real subnet mask is always a solid block of 1s followed by a solid block of 0s, never mixed.
3
Whatever number you counted to is your CIDR number. That's it — the count of 1s IS the slash number.
255.255.255.224 = /27
11111111 11111111 11111111 11100000

Counting: 8 ON bits in the first octet, 8 more in the second, 8 more in the third, then just 3 ON bits in the last octet before hitting a 0. That's 8 + 8 + 8 + 3 = 27 ones total, so this mask is a /27.

Shortcut: You almost never need to convert all 32 bits by hand. Since the first few octets in a mask are usually plain 255 (which is always 8 full ON bits, contributing 8 to your count each time), you really only need to memorize what each possible value in the LAST non-255 octet means:

One more important thing: the IP address itself doesn't tell you the CIDR — only the subnet mask does. The same IP, like 192.168.1.10, could belong to a /24 network or a /27 network depending entirely on what mask is paired with it. That's why CIDR notation always writes the two together, like 192.168.1.10/27 — the address tells you WHO, and the CIDR number tells you the SIZE of the group it belongs to.

📍 The other direction — finding the IP range from a subnet

Once you know an IP address and its CIDR, you can actually work out every single address that belongs to that same group — without anyone needing to tell you! Every subnet has exactly four important addresses worth knowing.

1
Start with the address and CIDR you were given, like 192.168.1.10/27. Find the host bits for /27 — that's 32 − 27 = 5 bits, meaning groups of addresses come in blocks of 2⁵ = 32.
2
Figure out which block of 32 your address falls into. Counting in steps of 32 from 0: 0–31, 32–63, 64–95... Since 10 falls between 0 and 31, your block starts at 192.168.1.0. That's the network address — it always has every host bit turned OFF, and nobody can actually use it as a device address.
3
The very last address in that same block — every host bit turned ON — is the broadcast address: 192.168.1.31. It's reserved for sending a message to every device on the subnet at once, so it can't be assigned to a single device either.
4
Everything in between is fair game for actual devices — the usable host range: 192.168.1.1 through 192.168.1.30. That's 30 usable addresses (32 total, minus the network and broadcast addresses).

Quick trick for finding block boundaries: Take the size of the block (32, in this example) and that's your "step size" — block boundaries always land on multiples of that number. Find which boundary your address falls just above, and that's your network address!

🎯 Given a subnet and CIDR, how do you get an actual IP address?

This is the flip side of the last section. Instead of starting with a single device's address and working out the whole subnet, here you're handed the subnet itself — like 10.0.0.0/28 — and need to pick out valid, individual addresses to actually assign to devices.

1
Start with the subnet you were given: 10.0.0.0/28. The first number, 10.0.0.0, is already the network address — it's reserved and can't be handed to any device.
2
Find the block size from the CIDR: /28 has 4 host bits, so the block holds 2⁴ = 16 addresses. That means this subnet spans from 10.0.0.0 up through 10.0.0.15.
3
The very last address in that span, 10.0.0.15, is the broadcast address — also reserved, also off-limits for a single device.
4
Every address strictly between those two — 10.0.0.1 through 10.0.0.14 — is fair game! To assign an IP to an actual device, just pick the next free one in that range, the same way a router's DHCP server does it automatically.

How do you actually get a CIDR block? Regular people never have to pick one — your home router automatically uses /24 (192.168.1.0/24) and hands out addresses to your devices for you, using a system called DHCP. Bigger organizations request their CIDR blocks from a Regional Internet Registry (like ARIN in North America), which is the official organization in charge of handing out blocks of IP addresses so nobody accidentally gets the same one as someone else.

Which one should you use? If you're just setting up devices at home, you don't need to choose at all — stick with your router's default. If you're building a small business network with under 250 devices, /24 is almost always the right size. Only go smaller (like /28 or /30) if you specifically need to limit a tiny group of devices, such as connecting two routers directly together.

MAC address — the other kind of address
An IP address can change every time a device reconnects. But every network adapter also has a second, permanent ID burned in at the factory — the MAC address.
A4: 83: E7: 2C: 11: F0

A MAC address (Media Access Control address) is like a permanent serial number for a network adapter — six pairs of letters and numbers that never change, unlike an IP address which can be reassigned every time you reconnect. When a device joins a network, the router remembers which MAC address it gave which IP to. You can even set up a DHCP reservation, telling the router "always give this exact MAC address the same IP" — getting the convenience of automatic DHCP with the reliability of a fixed address.

📬 DHCP — how your device gets an IP address automatically

Imagine moving into a new apartment building and the front desk automatically assigns you a mailbox number — that's basically what DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol) does every time your phone or laptop joins a Wi-Fi network. It happens in 4 quick steps, nicknamed DORA:

D
Discover — your device shouts out to the whole network: "Is there a DHCP server out there? I need an address!"
O
Offer — your router (acting as the DHCP server) replies: "Sure! How about 192.168.1.42?"
R
Request — your device says: "Great, I'll take that one — please reserve it for me!"
A
Acknowledge — the router confirms: "Done! That address is officially yours for now." Your device is online!

This whole DORA exchange takes a fraction of a second and happens automatically — that's why you almost never have to manually type in an IP address yourself. DHCP also "leases" the address for a set amount of time (often 24 hours), then quietly renews it or hands it out to someone else if your device leaves the network. Without DHCP, every single device would need someone to manually type in a unique IP address — imagine doing that for every phone, laptop, and smart light bulb in your house!

Most addresses handed out by DHCP are dynamic — meaning they can change each time your device reconnects (though in practice your router often gives you back the same one, since it remembers your device). Sometimes you want an address that NEVER changes, called a static IP. People set these up by hand for things like a printer or a security camera, so other devices on the network can always find it at the exact same spot.

What if there's no DHCP server around? Your device has a backup plan. What's a URL, really?
A URL (Uniform Resource Locator) is the friendly text you type to visit a website — but it's secretly hiding a lot of useful information packed into one line!
🔍 Anatomy of a URL
https://www.example.com/articles/wifi?sort=newest
Protocol (https://) — the set of rules your browser uses to talk to the website. The "s" means it's encrypted and secure — nobody can spy on what you're sending.
Subdomain (www.) — a smaller section of the main website, like a department inside a store. Other examples: mail.google.com or store.apple.com.
Domain + TLD (example.com) — the actual website's name, registered and owned by someone. The TLD (.com, .org, .edu) is like the "category" of the website.

Query string (?sort=newest) — extra instructions tacked onto the end, often used for search filters, sorting, or tracking which link you clicked.
🧭
From URL to webpage — the full journey: When you type a URL, your computer first looks up the domain name using DNS (like a phone book that turns "example.com" into an IP address like 93.184.216.34). Then it opens a TCP connection to that IP address using the three-way handshake. It connects to port 443 (since it's HTTPS). Finally, it asks for the specific path you wanted, and the website sends back the page — all of this happening in well under a second!
💡
Putting it together: Every time you send anything online, your computer wraps it in layers like a present — first the IP address layer (where it's going), then a TCP or UDP layer (how carefully to send it). It's a bit like writing a letter (your data), putting it in an envelope with delivery instructions (TCP or UDP), then writing the address on the outside (IP). All three work together every single time you load a webpage, send a text, or join a video call!
📖 What's DNS? The Domain Name System is the internet's phone book. It translates the words you type ("google.com") into the numerical IP address computers actually use to find each other.
🚦 HTTP status codes: When a webpage loads, the server replies with a 3-digit code. 200 means "all good," 404 means "page not found," and 500 means "something broke on the server's end."
📉 What's packet loss? Sometimes tiny pieces of data ("packets") get lost on their journey across the internet. TCP automatically resends lost packets, but UDP doesn't — which is why video calls sometimes glitch for a second.
🔐 Why "https" matters: The "s" scrambles your data using a lock-and-key system, so anyone snooping on the network (like on public Wi-Fi) can't read your passwords or messages.

Duplex — can both sides talk at the same time?
Uplink and downlink are the two directions data can travel. "Duplex" describes whether those two directions can happen AT THE SAME TIME, or whether they have to take turns.
🎙️
Walkie-talkie vs. phone call: A walkie-talkie is "half-duplex" — only one person can talk at a time, and you have to say "over" before the other person can respond. A phone call is "full-duplex" — both people can talk and listen at the exact same moment, just like a real conversation. Modern cell networks and Wi-Fi are built to act like a phone call, not a walkie-talkie.
📻

One direction only, ever
Simplex means data can ONLY travel one way — never both. There's no "talking back" at all. Think of an old-fashioned radio station: the station broadcasts to your radio, but your radio can never send anything back to the station. Very few modern devices use pure simplex, since almost everything needs at least some way to send data back, even if it's just a tiny confirmation signal.

📡 GSM vs CDMA — the two old cell network "languages"
For years, American carriers were split between two different and incompatible systems. GSM (used by AT&T and T-Mobile) always relied on a removable SIM card to identify your account. Every device has to ignore the messages that aren't meant for it. This wastes a lot of bandwidth and slows everything down as more devices are added, which is why hubs are almost never used anymore.

Why "collisions" happen

Since every device on a hub hears every message, two devices can sometimes try to "talk" at the exact same moment, causing what's called a collision — both messages get scrambled and have to be resent. The more devices you add to a hub, the more often this happens, which is the main reason hubs get slower and slower as a network grows.

  • Hubs were common in the 1990s and early 2000s, but switches eventually became so cheap that hubs disappeared from stores entirely.
  • You'll almost never see a hub for sale today — even cheap "switches" sold now are true switches, not hubs.

This makes networks much faster and more efficient, especially as more devices get added. Most wired connections in homes, schools, and offices today run through a switch (your Wi-Fi router usually has a small switch built right into it!).

How it "learns"

A switch keeps a little address book inside it, called a MAC address table. The first time a device sends any data, the switch notes down which port that device is plugged into. The internet isn't one single road — it's millions of connected paths. Every device connects to multiple other devices, creating lots of backup paths. This is how mesh Wi-Fi systems (like Google Nest Wifi or Eero) work — if one connection breaks, data just reroutes another way, so coverage stays strong across a whole house. The tradeoff is cost and complexity: mesh setups need more hardware and smarter software to manage all those extra connections, which is why a basic single router is usually cheaper.
Setup difficultyModerate
If one node failsRest keep working
Common useLarge homes, big offices
📏 Bus Topology
All devices connect to one single shared cable, like beads on a string. It's cheap and simple to set up since it needs the least amount of cable of any layout. But there are big downsides: if that one main cable breaks anywhere along its length, the WHOLE network goes down at once, and only one device can successfully send data on the cable at any given moment — everyone else has to wait their turn. This made bus networks slow once more than a handful of devices were added, which is part of why this layout faded out of use.
Setup difficultyVery easy
If cable failsWhole network goes down
Common useMostly historical today
🔁 Ring Topology
Each device connects to exactly two neighbors, forming a closed loop — like kids holding hands in a circle. Data travels around the ring, passed from device to device, until it reaches where it's going. Some ring networks can send data both directions around the loop, so if one connection breaks, data can still get through the long way around. This layout was popular in some older office networks (called Token Ring) but has mostly been replaced by star setups using switches today.
Setup difficultyModerate
If one link failsCan often reroute
Common useMostly historical today

🏠 Why mesh Wi-Fi for big houses? A single router can't always reach every room. Mesh systems place several small units around the house that all talk to each other, smoothly handing your phone off as you walk from room to room.
📦 What's a modem? A modem is a separate device that connects your home to your internet provider's wires (cable or fiber). Many home setups now combine a modem and router into one box.
🔁 What's a Wi-Fi extender? An extender repeats your existing Wi-Fi signal to reach farther rooms, but it's a simpler fix than mesh — it usually creates a second weaker network instead of one smooth connected one.
🔌 What's a port (on a router)? Those little slots on the back of a router are physical ports for plugging in Ethernet cables — different from the "port numbers" used for software like web traffic.
💡 Easy way to remember it: A hub talks to everyone. A switch talks to the right person. A router talks to a totally different building. Same basic idea, three very different jobs.
🔄 Restarting your router: Turning a router off and back on clears out small errors and forces it to grab a fresh connection — which is why "have you tried restarting it?" actually works so often!
