Math for EW, Made Simple

Four math skills come up constantly in EW work: rearranging equations, right-triangle trig, logarithms, and decibels. Master these and every RF formula stops looking like a wall of symbols.

Related: 🧮 Math for EW Toolkit 🔢 Fraction Calculator ⚡ AC Circuit Theory & Impedance

Rearranging equations without fear

Almost every formula you'll use in EW work — Ohm's Law, decibel formulas, link budgets — is the same handful of variables rearranged. Get comfortable moving things around and the rest gets much easier.

⚖️ The balance scale idea: think of an equation like a balance scale — both sides have to stay equal. Whatever you do to one side (add, subtract, multiply, divide), you have to do to the other side too, or the scale tips.

Order of operations first

Before rearranging anything, you need to know which operation happens first when a formula has several in a row. The standard order — often remembered as PEMDAS (Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication/Division, Addition/Subtraction) — always goes: parentheses first, then exponents, then multiplication and division (left to right), then addition and subtraction (left to right). Get this wrong and you'll get a completely different, wrong answer even though every individual step looked correct.

Solving for an unknown

Take a·x + b = c. To get x by itself, undo the operations in reverse order — subtract b from both sides first, then divide by a:

x = (c − b) ÷ a

This exact pattern is how you'd solve Ohm's Law for resistance (R = V ÷ I) or rearrange a decibel formula to solve for a power ratio instead of the dB value.

Worked example: rearranging Ohm's Law

Say you know a circuit runs at 12 volts and draws 0.5 amps, and you need the resistance. Ohm's Law is normally written V = I × R, but resistance isn't isolated — so isolate it the same way as the a·x + b = c example above:

V = I × R   →   R = V ÷ I   →   R = 12 ÷ 0.5 = 24Ω

Notice the process: identify what's isolated (V), identify what you actually need (R), then undo whatever's standing between them (divide both sides by I). Every rearrangement in this whole course — decibels, link budgets, radar range — follows that same three-step logic.

Right triangles are everywhere in RF

Antenna angles, direction-finding bearings, and signal phase all reduce to the same shape: a right triangle. Three ratios — sine, cosine, tangent — unlock all of it.

🏔️ The ramp idea: picture a ramp leaning against a wall. The ramp is the hypotenuse, the wall is the "opposite" side, and the ground is the "adjacent" side. The angle between the ramp and the ground determines how those three lengths relate to each other — that relationship is trigonometry.

Working backwards: inverse trig

SOHCAHTOA gets you a ratio if you already know the angle. But in the field, it's usually the other way around — you know two sides and need the angle itself. That's what the inverse functions are for: arcsin (sin⁻¹), arccos (cos⁻¹), and arctan (tan⁻¹) undo sine, cosine, and tangent to hand back the angle instead of the ratio.

angle = tan⁻¹(opposite ÷ adjacent)

Worked example: finding a bearing

A DF sensor sits 4 km north of a baseline and picks up an emitter 3 km east of that same point. What's the bearing angle off the baseline?

Opposite = 3 km (east), Adjacent = 4 km (north) — so:

angle = tan⁻¹(3 ÷ 4) = tan⁻¹(0.75) ≈ 36.9°

That's exactly the calculation running under the hood every time a DF tool draws a bearing line from a sensor toward a contact — it's arctan on the north/east offset, every time.

Logarithms compress huge numbers

A logarithm answers one question: "what power do I have to raise this base to, to get this number?" That's it — everything else is a consequence of that one idea.

📏 The zoom-out idea: imagine trying to fit a ruler that measures from 1 to 1,000,000 on a single page — the small numbers would be invisible. A log scale "zooms out" evenly, so 1, 10, 100, 1,000, and 1,000,000 are all equally spaced. That's why log scales show up wherever numbers span huge ranges.

The basic idea

log_b(N) = x   means   bˣ = N

For example, log₁₀(1000) = 3, because 10³ = 1000. The log just asks the exponent question backwards.

Log rules

log(A×B) = log(A) + log(B) log(A÷B) = log(A) − log(B) log(Aⁿ) = n·log(A)

Multiplication turns into addition, and division turns into subtraction — this is exactly why logs made hand calculation of huge numbers manageable for centuries before calculators existed, and it's why they show up in decibels next.

Which base — and why "ln" shows up too

Log base 10 (written log₁₀ or often just "log") is the natural fit for decibels, since our number system is base 10. But you'll also see ln — the natural log, base e (≈2.718) — in formulas describing exponential growth or decay, like signal attenuation over distance or capacitor charge/discharge curves. Same underlying idea, different base for a different kind of problem.

Worked example: converting a formula

Suppose you're told a value is log₂(32) and need it in log₁₀ terms for a different formula. Using the change-of-base relationship, any log can be rewritten as a ratio of two log₁₀ values:

log₂(32) = log₁₀(32) ÷ log₁₀(2) = 1.505 ÷ 0.301 = 5

Which checks out — 2⁵ = 32. You won't need to do this by hand often, but recognizing that any log can be converted this way explains why calculators only need one "log" button to handle every base.

Decibels: the log scale built for RF

A receiver might handle signal power spanning trillions-to-one in raw ratio. Decibels use the log idea from the last tab to turn that mess into small, easy numbers.

🔊 Why not just use watts? going from 1 watt to 1,000,000 watts is a huge jump to write and compare. In decibels, that same jump is just 60 dB — a small, easy-to-work-with number, because dB is a logarithmic (not linear) scale.

The two formulas

Power ratio: dB = 10 × log₁₀(P2 ÷ P1)
Voltage/amplitude ratio: dB = 20 × log₁₀(V2 ÷ V1)

Power ratios use 10; voltage/amplitude ratios use 20. The difference exists because power is proportional to voltage squared — squaring a number doubles its logarithm, which is where that extra factor of 2 comes from.

dBm, dBi, and friends

dBm is power measured relative to 1 milliwatt — 0 dBm is exactly 1 mW. dBi is antenna gain measured relative to a theoretical isotropic radiator (one that spreads energy perfectly evenly in all directions). Both use the exact same dB math from above — they just pick a different reference point to measure against.

Why dB is the real workhorse: cascading a signal chain

Here's the payoff for all of this: once a value is in dB, gains and losses down an entire signal chain just add and subtract instead of multiplying and dividing — exactly like the log rules from the last tab predicted. That's the whole reason the RF world runs on dB instead of raw watts.

Say a transmitter puts out 30 dBm, loses 3 dB in a cable, gains 10 dBi from the antenna, then loses 6 dB to free-space path loss by the time it reaches a receiver:

30 − 3 + 10 − 6 = 31 dBm at the receiver

Try doing that same chain in raw watts — you'd be multiplying and dividing four separate ratios instead of just adding four numbers. This is exactly the math behind the Power Budget concept covered in the Systems/Space guide.
