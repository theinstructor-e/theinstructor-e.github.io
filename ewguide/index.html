Simple Tech

What is Electronic Warfare?
The spectrum fight, made simple.

Everything from radios to radar to GPS rides on invisible radio waves. Electronic warfare is the fight to use that spectrum for yourself — and deny it to the enemy.

The invisible battlefield

The electromagnetic spectrum is the range of all radio waves — the same invisible highway used by radios, phones, Wi-Fi, radar, and GPS. Whoever controls it has a huge advantage.

🌊 Think of the spectrum like the ocean. Every signal is a boat on the water. Electronic warfare is naval strategy for that ocean: you want to sail freely yourself (use your radios and radar), listen for enemy boats (find their signals), and when needed, make waves that swamp their boats (jam them) — all while keeping your own vessel steady.

📻
Everything modern runs on the spectrum
and that's the vulnerability

Command radios, GPS navigation, drone control links, cell phones, radar — all of it depends on radio waves traveling through the air. That dependence is a strength and a weakness: anything that transmits can be heard, and anything that receives can be overwhelmed.

Electronic warfare (the Army now calls it Electromagnetic Warfare) is all about exploiting that fact — for you and against the enemy.

🔇The best signal is often no signal. If you transmit, you can be located. Good EW discipline (EMCON) means knowing when to stay silent so you don't hand the enemy your position.
⚡It's non-kinetic. EW can stop a drone or blind a radar without firing a shot — using energy and information instead of bullets.

Spectrum management: your own side is crowded too

The spectrum isn't just contested with the enemy — it's crowded with your own side's radios, radars, drones, and jammers all trying to use it at once. Electromagnetic spectrum management (EMSO) is the deconfliction process that assigns frequencies so friendly systems don't interfere with each other. A jammer that isn't coordinated with friendly radio nets can knock out your own communications just as easily as the enemy's — which is exactly why EW planning always happens alongside spectrum management, not separately from it.

📖Doctrine: the core references are JP 3-85 (Joint Electromagnetic Spectrum Operations) and FM 3-12 (Cyberspace Operations and Electromagnetic Warfare). Always confirm the current version.

The three jobs of EW

Everything in electronic warfare falls into one of three buckets. Learn these three and the whole field organizes itself.

👂
ES
Electronic Support

Sense & listen. Search for, intercept, and locate enemy signals. This is where you gather the picture — who's transmitting, on what frequency, and from where.

💥
EA
Electronic Attack

Deny & disrupt. Use energy to attack the enemy's use of the spectrum — jamming their radios, spoofing their GPS, or overloading their sensors.

🛡️
EP
Electronic Protection

Defend & survive. Protect your own systems from enemy EA and from your own jamming — frequency hopping, encryption, emission control.

🥊 A boxing analogy: ES is watching your opponent to read their next move. EA is throwing the punch. EP is your guard and footwork that keep you from getting hit. You need all three to win the round.

DivisionGoalExamples
ES — SupportFind & understand signalsSignal search, direction finding, threat warning
EA — AttackDeny the enemy the spectrumJamming, GPS spoofing, deception
EP — ProtectKeep your own systems workingFrequency hopping, encryption, EMCON

Worked scenario: a convoy under threat

A patrol detects an unfamiliar signal on a frequency known to trigger roadside IEDs. ES identifies the frequency and rough bearing. EA jams that specific frequency band as the convoy passes the threat area. EP — built in beforehand — means the convoy's own radios were frequency-hopping and encrypted, so the friendly jamming doesn't knock out friendly communications in the process. Miss any one leg and the plan breaks: no ES means EA is jamming blind, no EP means the convoy's own radios go down right alongside the threat.

🔄They work together. ES finds the target and hands it to EA to attack — while EP keeps your own gear running through the whole fight. It's a loop, not three separate boxes.

Jamming — drowning out the signal

Jamming is the classic form of electronic attack: transmit enough noise on the enemy's frequency that their receiver can't pick out the real message underneath.

🗣️ The crowded-room analogy: you're trying to hear a friend across a noisy room. If someone stands next to you and shouts louder than your friend, you can't make out the words. Jamming is exactly that — shouting on the enemy's channel until their message is lost in the noise.

It's a contest of signal vs. noise

A receiver can only understand a message if the wanted signal is stronger than the jamming (plus background noise). That ratio — signal versus jamming — decides whether comms get through. Push enough jamming power into the channel and you win; back off, or let the target get closer to its own transmitter, and the real signal "burns through."

Worked example: will the signal burn through?

A friendly radio transmits at a strength the enemy jammer can't overcome as long as the friendly transmitter stays close enough. As the friendly unit closes distance with its own base station, its signal gets stronger relative to the jammer (path loss favors whoever is closer) — at some point the wanted signal "burns through" the jamming even though the jammer's power never changed. This is exactly why jamming range and communication range are a moving target, not a fixed one: closing distance to your own station is itself a counter-jamming tactic.

🎯Jamming cuts both ways. A jammer is a very loud transmitter — which makes it easy to detect and locate. Using EA can expose your own position, so it's a deliberate trade-off.
🚁Counter-drone: jamming a drone's control link makes it lose contact with its pilot — then it falls back to its failsafe (return home, hover, or land). Jam its GPS too and it can't navigate at all.

Finding signals — direction finding

Before you can jam or exploit a signal, you have to find it. Direction finding (DF) figures out where a transmitter is by measuring the direction its signal comes from.

👂 The two-ears analogy: you can tell roughly where a sound comes from because it hits your two ears at slightly different times. DF antennas do the same with radio waves — comparing what several antennas hear to work out the bearing to the transmitter.

One bearing gives a line. Two give a point.

A single DF site can tell you the direction to a transmitter — a line of bearing (LOB) — but not how far. Take a bearing from a second location and the two lines cross. That crossing point is the transmitter. Add a third and you get a tight "cut" that pins it down.

Watch two bearings cross

—

Two sensors, two lines of bearing — where they intersect is the target. This is how a hidden emitter gets geolocated.

📐Geometry matters. If your sensors are almost in line with the target, the bearings cross at a shallow angle and the fix is fuzzy. Spread the sensors out and the intersection sharpens.

Why geometry quality matters so much

Two bearings crossing at close to 90° pin down a location tightly — a small bearing error only shifts the fix a little. But if both sensors sit almost on the same line from the target, the bearings cross at a very shallow angle, and that same small bearing error swings the fix wildly along the length of that shallow intersection. This is exactly why DF sensor placement is planned, not arbitrary — spreading sensors out to get closer to a 90° cut is worth more than adding a third sensor in a bad spot.

⏱️Modern DF adds timing. Systems compare the tiny differences in when and at what frequency a signal arrives at each sensor (TDOA/FDOA) to pin a location to a precise grid.

GPS attacks — jamming vs. spoofing

GPS is a receive-only signal, and it's extremely faint by the time it reaches the ground from space. That weakness makes it a prime target — in two very different ways.

🔇
Jamming
deny the signal

Blast noise on the GPS frequency so the receiver can't hear the faint satellites at all. Result: "no fix" — the device knows it's lost, but it can't navigate.

🎭
Spoofing
fake the signal

Transmit counterfeit GPS signals so the receiver computes a false position. The device thinks it knows where it is — but it's wrong. That's far sneakier than jamming.

🧭 The road-sign analogy: jamming is knocking down all the road signs so a driver is lost. Spoofing is putting up fake signs that quietly send the driver to the wrong town while they think they're on the right road. One is obvious; the other is deceptive.

JammingSpoofing
EffectNo position at allFalse position
Does the target know?Usually yes — "lost signal"Often no — it trusts the fake
DifficultyEasier (just noise)Harder (must mimic real signals)
🛰️Why GPS is so easy to overpower: the satellites are ~20,000 km away, so their signal arrives incredibly weak — below the background noise. A small transmitter nearby can easily drown it out or impersonate it.

Defending against both

Because jamming and spoofing exploit the same underlying weakness (a very faint, predictable signal), the countermeasures overlap too: military GPS receivers use encrypted signal formats that are far harder to convincingly fake, directional/null-steering antennas that can reject signals arriving from an unexpected direction (like a spoofer on the ground instead of satellites overhead), and cross-checking GPS position against inertial navigation or other sensors — so a receiver can flag "this doesn't match what my other instruments say" rather than blindly trusting a spoofed fix.

🛡️Protection (EP): anti-jam antennas, inertial navigation as a backup, and signal-authentication techniques help systems resist GPS attacks.
