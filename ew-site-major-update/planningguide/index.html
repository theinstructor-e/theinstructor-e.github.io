Planning & Tactics
How a mission comes together.

Before any operation, a leader plans, briefs, and rehearses. Learn the handful of frameworks the Army uses to turn a mission into action.

Troop Leading Procedures — the leader's checklist

TLP is the 8-step routine a small-unit leader runs to take a mission from "here's the task" to "we're ready to execute." It keeps planning from becoming chaos.

🍳 Like a chef before service: read the order, tell the kitchen to start prepping, plan the dishes, get ingredients moving, check the pantry, finalize the menu, brief the staff, then supervise and taste as you go. TLP is that same "get organized before the rush" discipline, for a mission.
Receive the mission
Understand the task and the commander's intent. Start a time plan (the ⅓–⅔ rule: keep a third, give two-thirds to your team).
Issue a warning order (WARNORD)
Give your team a heads-up right away so they can start prepping.
Make a tentative plan
Analyze the situation (METT-TC) and pick a course of action.
Initiate movement
Start any moving that has to happen while you keep planning.
Conduct reconnaissance
Confirm the terrain and your assumptions — on the ground if you can, on a map if you can't.
Complete the plan
Finalize it and check it against the intent.
Issue the order
Brief the full 5-paragraph OPORD to the team.
Supervise and refine
Rehearse, inspect, and adjust. This one never really stops.

METT-TC — the questions you ask

MMission — what must be done and why EEnemy — who's out there and what they'll likely do TTerrain & weather — the ground and conditions (OAKOC) TTroops & support — what you have to work with TTime available — how much clock you have CCivil considerations — the people and place

The OPORD — one order, five paragraphs

An Operation Order is how a leader hands off the plan. It always has the same five paragraphs, in the same order, so everyone knows where to find things.

📦 Like assembly instructions: a good manual always follows the same layout — what you're building, the parts, the steps, the extras, and who to call for help. The OPORD's five paragraphs are that reliable layout for a mission, so nothing gets missed.
Situation
What's going on — enemy forces, friendly forces, terrain, and any attachments.
Mission
One clear sentence: who, what, when, where, and why.
Execution
The commander's intent, the concept, and each unit's tasks — the "how."
Sustainment
Logistics, resupply, and medical/MEDEVAC — how you're kept in the fight.
Command & Signal
Who's in charge and where, plus radio nets, frequencies, and call signs.
🔤Remember it as SMESC: Situation, Mission, Execution, Sustainment, Command & Signal. Same five, every time.
📝Related orders: a WARNORD is the early heads-up, and a FRAGORD (frag order) is a quick update that changes only part of an existing order.

Battle drills — actions without orders

Some situations happen too fast to plan on the spot. A battle drill is a rehearsed reaction the whole team does automatically, on a simple cue.

🏈 Like a football team's set plays: when the ball is snapped, nobody stops to discuss — everyone runs their part instantly because they drilled it a thousand times. Battle drills are combat set plays: one cue, everyone reacts, no meeting required.
🎯
Warrior task vs. battle drill
individual vs. team

A warrior task is something one Soldier does (load a weapon, apply a tourniquet, call a 9-line). A battle drill is something the whole team does together on a cue (react to contact, react to ambush). Warrior tasks are grouped as Shoot, Move, Communicate, Survive, Adapt.

CueThe drill
"Contact front!"React to Contact — return fire, suppress, report, maneuver, assault through
"Ambush right!"React to Ambush — near: assault through the kill zone; far: take cover and maneuver
"Incoming!"React to Indirect Fire — get down, then move out of the impact area
"Man down!"Evacuate a Casualty — suppress first, then treat and move to cover
"Gas! Gas! Gas!"React to Chemical Attack — mask in 9 seconds, MOPP, move upwind
SPORTSweapon jam fix: Slap, Pull, Observe, Release, Tap, Shoot SALUTEspot report: Size, Activity, Location, Unit, Time, Equipment

Formations — how a team moves

FOOM stands for Formations and Order of Movement. The shape a team moves in trades off firepower, control, and security — and the right choice depends on the situation.

🐦 Like birds choosing a flight shape: a tight line, a V, or a single file — each shape is better for something. A team picks its formation the same way: spread out for security, single-file for speed through tight terrain, on-line to hit hard to the front.
FormationBest for
WedgeThe default — 360° security and fast reaction in any direction
File (column)Tight terrain and speed — but weakest security, avoid if contact is likely
LineMaximum firepower to the front — used to assault an objective
Vee / EchelonStrong fires to the front and a chosen flank

Movement techniques — how carefully you move

TravelingContact not likely — move fast, together Traveling OverwatchContact possible — lead moves, trail overwatches Bounding OverwatchContact expected — one element covers while the other bounds; slowest but safest
⚖️It's always a trade-off. Speed vs. security vs. control. There's no "best" formation — only the best one for the terrain and the threat right now (that's METT-TC again).
