What each status means

Taboo ⚠ Federal offense

Absolutely no unauthorized transmission. These bands are reserved for safety-of-life or government-exclusive use. Transmitting — even accidentally or at low power — can endanger lives, disrupt navigation, or jam emergency services. The FCC and federal law treat violations as serious criminal offenses, not just regulatory infractions. Jamming GPS, for example, carries fines up to $100,000 per violation and potential prison time.

Examples: Aviation nav 108–136 MHz GPS L1/L2 EPIRB 406 MHz Radio astronomy 1400 MHz Public safety 4.94 GHz
Protected Primary allocation

A primary service holds exclusive rights to this band and must be shielded from interference by all other users. You generally cannot transmit here without authorization, and even licensed secondary users must not cause harmful interference. The primary service — like FM radio, TV broadcast, or weather radar — was allocated this spectrum by the FCC and has first-in-right priority under ITU Radio Regulations.

Examples: FM broadcast 87.5–108 MHz DTV 470–698 MHz NEXRAD radar 2700–2900 MHz Federal radar 5.35–5.47 GHz
Guarded Secondary — must yield

You can operate here, but you are a guest. Secondary users must not cause harmful interference to any primary user, and must accept interference from them — no complaints, no recourse. If a primary user appears (e.g. military radar sweeps through a CBRS band), secondary devices must stop or move. Think of it as operating on borrowed time: legal, but always subordinate to whoever has primary rights.

Examples: 70 cm amateur 420–450 MHz CBRS 3.55–3.7 GHz 9 cm amateur 3.3–3.5 GHz V2X 5.85–5.925 GHz
Licensed FCC authorization required

You must apply for and hold an FCC license to transmit. Licensing gives you exclusive or coordinated rights to specific frequencies in specific areas, with defined power limits and technical parameters. It prevents two users from trampling each other. Licenses range from amateur radio tickets (free, earned by exam) to cellular spectrum licenses auctioned for billions of dollars. Operating without a license is a violation even if you cause no interference.

Examples: Amateur HF/VHF/UHF Cellular 700/850/1900 MHz Land mobile / public safety C-band 5G 3.7–4.2 GHz
Unlicensed Part 15 — no permit needed

Anyone can transmit here without a license — but only within strict power and technical limits set by FCC Part 15 rules. Devices must not cause harmful interference to licensed services, and must accept any interference they receive. This is the legal home of Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, baby monitors, and garage door openers. The catch: because it's open to everyone, congestion and interference between unlicensed devices is common and unprotected.

Examples: Wi-Fi 2.4 GHz (802.11b/g/n) Bluetooth Wi-Fi 5 GHz U-NII-1/2C Wi-Fi 6E 5.925–6 GHz

1227.6 / 1575.42 MHz GPS L2 / L1 GPS satellite navigation signals — civilian and military positioning. Jamming is a federal crime. GPS L1 (1575.42 MHz) carries the civilian C/A code used by every consumer GPS device. L2 (1227.6 MHz) carries a military P(Y) code and is also used by dual-frequency receivers for ionospheric error correction — enabling centimetre-level precision. GPS signals arrive at Earth at roughly −130 dBm (far weaker than a TV signal), making them trivially easy to jam or spoof. A cheap illegal jammer can disrupt GPS across several city blocks. 1400 – 1427 MHz L-band radio astronomy Passive radio astronomy and remote sensing — zero active transmissions permitted by ITU/FCC Hydrogen line passband — the 1420.405 MHz emission frequency of neutral hydrogen atoms is one of the most important in all of radio astronomy. Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe; mapping its Doppler-shifted emissions reveals galactic structure and the rotation curves that led astronomers to hypothesize dark matter. Even a single weak interfering signal from Earth can mask faint cosmic sources. SETI (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence) also monitors this band closely.
