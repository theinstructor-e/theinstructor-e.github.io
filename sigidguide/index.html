Signal Identification Explained

Every radio signal leaves a visual fingerprint. Learn the handful of traits that give it away — then go practice reading them for real.

The Four Traits That Matter

Almost any signal can be identified from just four characteristics. Learn these and you can reason through a signal you've never seen before.

📶
Frequency — where it sits on the dial. Some signals are pinned to one exact frequency (GPS, aviation nav beacons); others roam across a whole band.
↔️
Bandwidth — how wide it is. A voice channel is narrow; live video or a wideband jammer is wide. Width on the waterfall = bandwidth.
⏱️
Duty Cycle — how often it transmits. Always-on and continuous (a broadcast carrier) looks very different from brief, occasional bursts (a push-to-talk radio).
🦘
Hopping / Sweeping — does it stay put, or does it jump or slide around? Fast, regular jumps usually mean a deliberate anti-jam design, not an accident.

Reading a Waterfall

A waterfall shows frequency left-to-right and time scrolling top-to-bottom. A spectrum view shows the same instant differently: frequency left-to-right, but power (signal strength) as height.

👀
Don't judge from one frame. Duty cycle and hopping only become obvious after watching a few seconds scroll by.
🎯
A vertical streak that never moves = fixed frequency. A streak that jumps around between frames = hopping. A diagonal line = a swept/chirped signal.

Real Research Behind This

This isn't just an academic exercise — signal identification and anomaly detection are active, funded research areas right now, including close to home.

🐱
Georgia Tech Research Institute's "Angry Kitten" — a cognitive electronic warfare pod deployed on USAF F-16s (flown at Red Flag exercises) that uses machine learning to recognize and adapt to unfamiliar threat signals in real time, rather than relying on a fixed pre-programmed library. That's the same core idea behind this tool's anomaly quiz mode — noticing when something doesn't match a known baseline.
📡
GTRI's Electromagnetic Battle Management (EMBM) program builds tools like ATOM to help operators make sense of a crowded, contested spectrum picture across multiple distributed sensors — the same "what am I actually looking at" problem this trainer practices at a small scale.
📘
Want the real math? PySDR is a free, open-source textbook covering exactly what's happening under the hood of a real waterfall, written for people learning SDR from scratch. A few chapters that connect directly to this guide:
→ Ch. 2 — Frequency Domain (FFT & the spectrogram/waterfall, explained from scratch) → Ch. 3 — IQ Sampling (how a receiver actually captures a signal) → Ch. 4 — Digital Modulation (ASK/PSK/FSK/QAM — the "why" behind several signals in the Reference tab) → Ch. 24 — Detection Using Correlation (pulling a real signal out of the noise floor — the theory behind anomaly detection)
