1 · Why Identify Signals?

You will be able to
  • Say what signal identification is for in Electronic Support.
  • Name the four traits that identify almost any signal.
  • Describe the order of questions you ask about an unknown signal.

Every radio signal leaves a visual fingerprint. In Electronic Support (ES) you search, intercept, and identify emitters so a commander knows what is in the spectrum, who is using it, and what has changed. You rarely get a label with the signal. You get its traits.

The four traits

TraitQuestionWhere you see it
FrequencyWhere is it?Position left to right
BandwidthHow wide is it?Width on the display
Duty cycle / timingWhen and how often is it on?Pattern down a waterfall
MotionDoes it stay, hop, or sweep?Streak shape

Learn these four and you can reason through a signal you have never seen.

The order of questions

  1. Is something there? Is it above the noise floor?
  2. What are its traits? Frequency, width, timing, motion.
  3. What family does that fit? Broadcast, radar, land mobile, data, and so on.
  4. Is it where and when it should be? That is the baseline question, and it finds anomalies.
  5. What do I do with it? Log, report, locate (direction finding), or monitor.

Why a 17E caresIn a contested spectrum there are thousands of emitters. The skill is deciding which two or three need attention, and that starts with reading traits quickly.

2 · Frequency and Bandwidth

You will be able to
  • Read a bandwidth off a display.
  • Match a width to a likely family.
  • Explain why a signal’s bandwidth is not its data rate.

On a spectrum or waterfall, frequency runs across and a signal’s width is its bandwidth. To measure it, put a cursor on each edge. If the display spans S hertz over P pixels, each pixel is S ÷ P hertz.

bandwidth = pixels wide × (span ÷ pixels across)

Bandwidth is a strong clue to the family:

SignalTypical width
CW (Morse), FT8 and other narrow dataunder 1 kHz (FT8 about 50 Hz)
HF single-sideband voiceabout 3 kHz
AM broadcastabout 10 kHz
Narrow FM voice (land mobile)12.5 to 25 kHz
FM broadcastabout 200 kHz
Bluetooth1 MHz per channel
GPS C/Aabout 2 MHz
Wi-Fi (2.4 GHz)20 MHz (also 40 MHz)
Wideband jammer or video linkseveral MHz to tens of MHz

Bandwidth is not the same as data rate. Many signals carry little data and use a lot of room on purpose, such as spread spectrum (GPS). The relation depends on the modulation, as in the Modulation & Noise guide.

3 · Timing: Duty Cycle and Pulses

You will be able to
  • Calculate duty cycle and burst rate.
  • Work out radar PRF and its unambiguous range.
  • Say what continuous, bursty, and pulsed patterns usually mean.

Watch a waterfall for a few seconds and the timing pattern shows. A continuous carrier is a solid vertical line. A push-to-talk radio shows irregular bursts. A data link shows regular short bursts. A radar shows very short, regular pulses.

duty cycle = on time ÷ period rate = 1 ÷ period
radar: PRF = 1 ÷ pulse repetition interval max unambiguous range = c ÷ (2 × PRF)

Radar sends a pulse and waits for the echo. If the echo comes back after the next pulse has already gone out, the radar cannot tell which pulse it belongs to. So the longest range it can state without ambiguity is the distance light covers (there and back) in one interval. At a PRF of 1,000 Hz that is 150 km.

Try it: duty cycle, rate, and radar range
The last two readings treat the signal as a radar and use the period as its pulse interval.
Why a 17E caresTiming is hard to fake and easy to see. A transmitter that goes on at the same instant every time is telling you its schedule.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Duty cycle = on time ÷ period. Rate = 1 ÷ period.
  • Radar PRF sets unambiguous range: c ÷ (2 × PRF).
  • Continuous, bursty, and pulsed patterns point to different families.

4 · Hopping, Sweeping, and Spreading

You will be able to
  • Recognize fixed, hopping, swept, bursty, and wideband patterns on a waterfall.
  • Explain why hopping and spreading are used.
  • Name an example of each.
PatternOn a waterfallWhy and example
FixedVertical streak that holds stillBroadcast, GPS, beacons
HoppingShort dashes jumping between frequenciesAnti-jam and anti-intercept. Bluetooth (about 1,600 hops a second over 79 channels), SINCGARS (about 100 hops a second)
Swept (chirp)Diagonal lineFMCW radar, some jammers, sounders
Wideband / spreadBroad flat block, sometimes below the noiseSpread spectrum such as GPS, and noise jammers

Fast regular jumps usually mean a deliberate design, not an accident. A hopper spends only a moment on each frequency, so a narrowband jammer or listener catches only a fraction of it. A spread signal puts its energy over so wide a band that each part is faint.

Do not judge from one frame. Hopping and duty cycle only show after a few seconds scroll by.

5 · Reading the Display

You will be able to
  • Tell a spectrum display from a waterfall.
  • Explain the resolution and time trade-off in an FFT.
  • Use averaging, peak hold, and the noise floor.

A spectrum shows one instant: frequency across, power as height. A waterfall adds time: frequency across, time scrolling down, brightness for power. The waterfall shows timing and motion. The spectrum shows exact levels.

  • Noise floor: the fuzz everything sits on. A signal must rise above it to be seen. The floor rises with bandwidth: a wider setting shows more noise.
  • Averaging: smooths the noise so steady signals stand out. It hides short bursts.
  • Peak hold: keeps the highest value seen. It shows every burst that ever happened, but loses timing.
  • Dynamic range: a very strong signal can hide weak ones nearby or cause false signals (see the SDR guide).

The FFT trade-off

frequency resolution Δf = sample rate ÷ N time per line Δt = N ÷ sample rate Δf × Δt = 1

A big FFT can separate two close tones but misses brief bursts. A small FFT catches bursts but cannot separate neighbours.

Try it: can you resolve it? can you catch it?
Tones are resolved when they are at least one bin apart. A burst is caught cleanly when it lasts at least one line.
Why a 17E caresThe same signal looks different with different settings. Knowing what the settings do keeps you from missing a burst or calling two neighbours one signal.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Spectrum is one instant, waterfall adds time.
  • Averaging smooths steady signals and hides bursts. Peak hold shows bursts and loses timing.
  • Δf = fs ÷ N, Δt = N ÷ fs, and Δf × Δt = 1.

You will be able to
  • Name the broad signal families and their usual traits.

  • Spot what has changed from a baseline.

You do not have to memorize every signal. Know the broad families and what is normal where you are.

Baseline and anomaly

A baseline is what the spectrum normally looks like in a place and at a time. An anomaly is any difference that matters: a new signal, one that has gone missing, one that is wider, louder, or on a different schedule, or one that hops where it used to sit still. Finding a threat often means noticing it does not belong.

Worked example

An unknown signal

You see a signal at 433.9 MHz. It is about 100 kHz wide, appears in short bursts a few seconds apart, and stays on one frequency.

At 100 kHz it is a medium width (30 kHz to 1 MHz), bursty, and fixed. The 433 MHz band is an unlicensed band used by remote controls, weather stations, and sensors. Likely family: a consumer or IoT device. Check the baseline: is it new? Where is it strongest? A sensor beside a window is normal. The same burst from a place that should be empty is worth reporting.

Real research

Signal identification and anomaly detection are active, funded research areas. The Georgia Tech Research Institute’s “Angry Kitten” is a cognitive electromagnetic warfare pod flown on U.S. Air Force F-16s that uses machine learning to recognise and adapt to unfamiliar threat signals instead of relying only on a fixed library. GTRI’s electromagnetic battle management work builds tools to help operators make sense of a crowded spectrum picture across many sensors. PySDR (pysdr.org) is a free textbook that explains what is behind a real waterfall, including the frequency domain, IQ sampling, and digital modulation.
