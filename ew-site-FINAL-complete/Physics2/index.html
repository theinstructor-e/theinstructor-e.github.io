◆ Lesson Alignment — B14 (LSA 1-5)
An EM wave is an electric (E) field and magnetic (H) field oscillating together, mutually perpendicular and perpendicular to the direction of travel. The E-field (V/m) is created by charges and drives current in antennas; the H-field (A/m) comes from moving charges and is sensed by loop antennas for direction finding. Per Maxwell, each changing field regenerates the other — so the wave sustains itself through space with no medium needed.
EM Wave — Mutually Perpendicular Fields
AMBER = E-field (vertical)  |  CYAN = H-field (horizontal)  |  GREEN = propagation
FREE-SPACE IMPEDANCE (377 Ω): the impedance of free space is fundamentally different from the 50 Ω antenna system. The antenna acts as an impedance transformer bridging the 50 Ω feedline to 377 Ω free space. 50 Ω coax is a compromise between lowest loss and highest power handling.

◆ Lesson Alignment — B15 (LSA 1)
Radio waves interact with the environment four key ways. Reflection — bounce off objects larger than the wavelength (angle in = angle out). Refraction — bend when passing into a medium of different density (changing speed/direction). Diffraction — bend and spread around obstacles/openings, allowing reception with no line-of-sight. Knife-edge diffraction — a sharp ridge bends a signal over the horizon (at the cost of diffraction loss). These are why signals reach where straight lines can't — and why an emitter's apparent bearing can mislead.
17E: reflections and diffraction create multiple arrival paths and shift apparent emitter location — always be skeptical of single-site bearings in cluttered terrain.

◆ Lesson Alignment — B15 (LSA 2-6)
Ground waves hug the Earth via diffraction and induced currents — best at low frequencies, extended over seawater (high conductivity = low loss). Sky waves refract off the ionosphere's F2 layer for long-range HF (3-30 MHz). Space waves travel line-of-sight, limited by the radio horizon (~25-62 mi), used for VHF/UHF tactical links, radar, and satellite.

◆ Lesson Alignment — B16 (LSA 1-4)
Polarization is the orientation of the E-field. Vertical — whip/monopole antennas, mobile comms, AM/LMR; good ground-wave performance. Horizontal — rooftop TV antennas; more stable over terrain, immune to (mostly-vertical) man-made noise. Circular — E-field rotates (two 90°-out-of-phase waves); GPS/satcom use RHCP for immunity to antenna orientation and rain. For max signal transfer, RX polarization must MATCH TX.

◆ Lesson Alignment — B17 Multipath
Multipath = a signal arrives via multiple paths (direct + reflected). In-phase arrivals reinforce; out-of-phase cancel → fading. Flat fading affects the whole signal equally; selective fading notches specific frequencies (visible as drifting nulls in a waterfall). OFDM resists selective fading by splitting into many narrow subcarriers. EW fixes: antenna diversity (spatial/polarization), raise the antenna, use directional antennas, relocate, or frequency-hop.
EW: ionospheric multipath can mask an emitter's true position — be skeptical of single-site bearings; use TDOA / cross-fixes.
