Systems: Space & Drones
Orbits and counter-UAS, made simple.

The systems a 17E cares about live at the extremes — satellites hundreds of miles up, and small drones just overhead. Both depend on radio links you can exploit.

Why satellites don't fall down

You will be able to
  • Name LEO, MEO, and GEO and what each is used for.
  • Say why higher orbits are slower.

A satellite is constantly falling toward Earth — it just moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing. That balance is an orbit.

⚾ Newton's cannonball: throw a ball and it curves to the ground. Throw it harder and it lands farther. Throw it fast enough and the ground curves away beneath it just as fast as it falls — now it circles the planet forever. A satellite is a very fast cannonball.

Higher orbit = slower and farther

The three orbit "neighborhoods"

OrbitAltitudeUsed for
LEO (Low)~160–2,000 kmImaging, Starlink, ISS — fast, low latency, but zips overhead
MEO (Medium)~2,000–35,000 kmGPS and other navigation constellations
GEO (Geostationary)~35,786 kmWeather, comms, TV — appears to hover over one spot
🌐GEO satellites look motionless. At about 35,786 km they orbit exactly once per day — matching Earth's spin — so from the ground they appear to hang in one place. That's why a satellite dish can be bolted down and never moved.
⏱️Higher = slower. A low satellite races around in ~90 minutes; a GEO satellite takes 24 hours. The farther out, the weaker gravity's pull and the slower it needs to go.

Why this matters for tracking a satellite

A 90-minute LEO orbit means the satellite crosses your entire sky and disappears again in just minutes — useful for quick imaging passes, but it also means you only get a brief window of contact before it's gone and you're waiting for the next pass. A GEO satellite trades that fast pass for a fixed, always-available link — no tracking required at all, which is exactly why it's the right choice for things like broadcast TV and always-on communications relays, while LEO is the right choice when you need to physically image a location up close.

Talking to something in space

You will be able to
  • Explain uplink, downlink, and bent-pipe relays.
  • Calculate GEO delay.

Every satellite is really a radio relay in the sky. The physics is the same as any RF link — just over enormous distances.

🔦 A mirror on a mountaintop: a comms satellite is like a giant mirror you can bounce signals off to reach someone over the horizon. You send up (uplink), it relays back down (downlink) to a distant station you couldn't reach directly.
⬆️
Uplink & downlink
two directions, two frequencies

Ground-to-satellite is the uplink; satellite-to-ground is the downlink. They use different frequencies so they don't interfere. The satellite's "bent pipe" simply receives, amplifies, and re-transmits.

Satellite bands

BandRough rangeNotes
L1–2 GHzGPS, sat phones — robust, rain-resistant
Ku12–18 GHzTV, VSAT — high data, more rain fade
Ka26–40 GHzHigh-throughput broadband — most rain-sensitive
⏳GEO has a noticeable delay. A signal sent up to a GEO satellite and back down to a ground station travels about 72,000 km, which takes about a quarter-second (0.24 s). A reply makes the same trip, so a question and its answer take about half a second — which is why some satellite calls feel "walkie-talkie" slow.

Where that number actually comes from

Radio waves travel at the speed of light — the same c=fλ relationship from the RF physics tools. The distance up to a GEO satellite is about 35,786 km. Up and back down to a second ground station is roughly double that, close to 72,000 km:

delay = distance ÷ speed = 72,000 km ÷ 300,000 km/s ≈ 0.24 seconds

That is a single hop, one way. A reply makes the same trip, so a question and answer lag by about half a second. A phone call bounced satellite-to-satellite-to-ground can stack multiple hops of this same delay, which is exactly why LEO constellations (much closer, much less delay per hop) have become attractive for latency-sensitive links despite needing many more satellites to maintain constant coverage.

🌧️"Rain fade" is real. Higher bands (Ku, Ka) carry more data but are absorbed by heavy rain, so links can degrade in storms. Lower bands (L) are steadier.

How a small drone works

You will be able to
  • Name a drone’s three links and their weak points.
  • Say why pre-programmed missions break the “deny the links” answer.

A quadcopter is only as good as its radio links. Understand the three links and you understand its weak points.

🎮 A remote-control car with a camera: the drone needs a link to receive your steering (control), often a link to send video back (so you can see), and a way to know where it is (GPS). Cut any of those and the drone is in trouble — which is the whole idea behind counter-drone.
🎮
Control link (C2)
usually 2.4 / 5.8 GHz

Carries the pilot's commands up to the drone. Lose it and a manually-flown drone falls back to its failsafe — return home, hover, or land.

📹
Video downlink
usually 5.8 GHz

Streams the drone's camera back to the operator. Jam it and the pilot goes blind; intercept it and you see what they see.

🛰️
GPS / navigation
~1.5 GHz, receive-only

Lets the drone hold position and fly waypoints on its own. Deny it and an autonomous drone can't navigate.

🎯The airframe isn't the target — its links are. You rarely need to shoot a drone down. Attack the radio links it depends on and it fails on its own.

The harder case: pre-programmed missions

Everything above assumes a drone actively depends on its links in real time — but a drone flying a pre-programmed waypoint mission only needed GPS and control links to load that mission beforehand. Once airborne and following its stored waypoints, jamming the control link does nothing (there's no live pilot connection to interrupt), and even jamming GPS may only push it onto dead-reckoning navigation rather than stopping it outright. This is exactly why "deny the links" isn't a universal counter-drone answer — the drone's actual autonomy level determines which link, if any, is still the weak point.

Counter-UAS — the kill chain

You will be able to
  • List the six steps of the C-UAS kill chain.
  • Say why Decide is the hinge.

Stopping a hostile drone is a sequence, not a single action. Each step has to succeed for the next to matter.

Detect
Sense that something is in the air — by RF, radar, cameras (EO/IR), or acoustics.
Track
Keep a continuous fix on it so it can be handed to an effector.
Identify
Friend, foe, or a hobbyist? What model, and is it armed? Positive ID before acting.
Decide
Apply the rules of engagement and get clearance. This is the legal hinge — don't skip it.
Defeat
Apply the effect: jam the control link, jam or spoof GPS, or a kinetic option.
Assess
Did it work? If not, re-engage. Feeds straight back into tracking.
🥅 Like a goalkeeper: you can't stop the ball (defeat) until you see it (detect), follow it (track), read the shot (identify), and commit to a save (decide). Miss any read and the goal gets through.

How EW defeats a drone

EffectWhat the drone does
Jam control linkLoses the pilot → triggers failsafe (return home / hover / land)
Jam GPSCan't navigate → drifts, can't hold position or fly waypoints
Spoof GPSBelieves a false position → can be walked to a chosen spot
🤖Jamming the control link isn't always enough. A fully autonomous drone flying on GPS ignores a control-link jammer — so you have to deny its GPS too. That's a favorite teaching point.

Why "Decide" is really the hinge of the whole chain

Detect, Track, and Identify can all be done wrong without hurting anyone directly — a missed detection just means the chain restarts. Decide is different: acting on bad identification, or without proper clearance, is exactly how friendly drones get shot down (fratricide) or civilian property gets damaged. That's the entire reason positive ID and RoE clearance sit as their own explicit steps rather than being folded into "defeat" — the chain is deliberately built so the irreversible action can't happen until everything upstream of it has been verified.

Layered defense and passive measures

You will be able to
  • Explain why defense needs several layers.
  • Name passive measures.

No single sensor or effector stops every drone. Good defense stacks several layers and also makes the target harder to hit in the first place.

🏰 Like a castle: a moat, a wall, archers, and a keep. Any one can be beaten, but an attacker has to beat them in order. Overlap is the point.

Why layers

LayerGood atWeakness
RF detectionFinding drones that transmit, and often the pilotMisses radio-silent drones
RadarSeeing anything with a physical size, day or nightSmall, slow drones and ground clutter are hard
EO/IR camerasIdentifying what a drone isShort range, weather and light dependent
Acoustic sensorsCueing on drone noiseVery short range and noisy sites
RF effects (jamming, GPS denial or spoofing)Defeating drones that depend on their linksUseless against drones with no radio link
Kinetic and other effectsStopping any drone physicallyCost, collateral damage, and limited engagement time

Layers multiply. If each of two independent layers stops 70 percent of attackers, the chance that one gets through both is 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.09, so together they stop 91 percent. Three layers stop about 97 percent. Real layers are never fully independent, so treat that as the best case.

Passive measures

🌳Make the target harder to find and hurt. Camouflage and concealment, dispersion so one hit does not take everything, overhead cover and hardening, EMCON so you give the sensors nothing to detect, and early warning with a place to take shelter. Passive measures work against any drone, including ones jamming cannot stop.
💡The hard cases. Autonomous drones and drones controlled through a thin optical fibre have no control link to jam. That is why a defense that relies only on RF effects has a gap, and why radar, cameras, kinetic options, and passive measures remain part of the plan. See the Drone Flight Basics guide.
