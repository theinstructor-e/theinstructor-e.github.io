HOW IT WORKS AND WHAT IS LEFT OUT

HOW A FIX WORKS

Each satellite broadcasts its position and the time it sent the signal. The receiver measures how long the signal took, which gives a range. One range puts you on a sphere around that satellite. Several ranges cross at one point.

WHY FOUR SATELLITES

The receiver's clock is not accurate enough, so every range is off by the same unknown amount. That makes the clock error a fourth unknown on top of latitude, longitude, and height. The 2-D picture here works the same way: you need three ranges to solve for two position unknowns plus the clock error.

GEOMETRY AND DOP

Dilution of precision (DOP) says how much the satellite layout magnifies the range errors. Satellites spread widely across the sky give a low DOP. Satellites bunched together, or hidden by buildings or terrain, give a high DOP. Horizontal error is roughly HDOP times the range error.

WHY JAMMING IS EASY

GPS satellites are about 20,200 km up and broadcast with modest power. The minimum guaranteed signal at the ground for the civil L1 C/A signal is about -158.5 dBW, or -128.5 dBm. A small ground transmitter much closer to the receiver can easily overpower it. Typical civil receivers lose full tracking when the jammer is roughly 30 to 40 dB stronger than the satellite signal. Receivers built to resist jamming can tolerate far more.

LEFT OUT

Ionosphere and troposphere delay, multipath, satellite and receiver clock details, ephemeris error, multiple frequencies, and spoofing. The satellite positions come from an ideal circular 24-satellite constellation, not the real one. See the GPS Spoof tool for spoofing and meaconing.
