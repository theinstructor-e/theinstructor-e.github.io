1 · Where Am I? Position, Navigation and Timing

You will be able to
  • Say what PNT is and name the main satellite navigation systems.
  • Describe the three segments of GPS and how the satellites are arranged.
  • Calculate how orbit height sets period and speed.

Satellite navigation gives three things, known together as PNT: position (where am I), navigation (how do I get there, and how fast am I going), and timing (what time is it, exactly). Most people use the first two. Much of the modern world quietly depends on the third: cell towers, power grids, banks, and many military systems take their clock from satellites.

GNSS is the general name for satellite navigation systems. GPS is the American one. Others are GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (European Union), and BeiDou (China). Many receivers listen to several at once.

Three segments

SegmentWhat it isJob
SpaceThe satellitesBroadcast their orbit and the exact time, over and over.
ControlGround monitoring stations and control centersTrack the satellites, correct their clocks and orbits, upload fresh data.
UserYour receiver, such as a phone or a DAGRListen only. A GPS receiver does not transmit to anyone.

That last point matters in EW: using GPS does not give your position away. It is the receiver's weakness, not its signature, that an enemy goes after (Chapter 6).

The GPS constellation

  • The United States commits to keeping at least 24 operational satellites. In practice there are usually more, around 31.
  • They sit in six orbital planes, tilted about 55° to the equator.
  • Altitude is about 20,200 km. One orbit takes about 11 hours 58 minutes, close to half a day.
  • Each satellite can see about 38% of the Earth at once, so from open ground you normally have six or more in view.

Orbit height and speed are tied together by gravity. A higher orbit is slower and takes longer. The widget below uses the real formula.

period T = 2π × √( a³ ÷ μ ) speed v = √( μ ÷ a )
a = Earth radius (6,371 km) + altitude μ = 398,600 km³/s²

The green circle is the part of Earth the satellite can see. Try GEO: its period is one day, so it hovers over one spot. At 20,200 km, GPS sees 38% of the Earth.

2 · How a Fix Works

You will be able to
  • Explain how a travel time becomes a distance.
  • Say why a receiver needs four satellites, not three.
  • Convert a clock error into a position error.

Time is distance

Every satellite broadcasts its position and the exact moment it sent the signal. Radio travels at the speed of light, about 299,792 km per second. The receiver notes when the signal arrived, subtracts, and multiplies.

distance = speed of light × travel time

From overhead, a GPS signal takes about 67 milliseconds to arrive. From a satellite near the horizon it takes about 86 ms. Now look at the other end of the scale: one microsecond of timing error is about 300 metres. One nanosecond is 30 centimetres. That is why satellites carry atomic clocks.

Try it: a clock error is a distance error
The bar runs from 1 nanosecond to 1 millisecond. Each step along the bar is ten times bigger. Notice how soon the error stops being a few metres and becomes a wrong place on the map.

Why the fourth satellite

Your receiver does not carry an atomic clock. Its cheap clock is off by an unknown amount, so every measured distance is wrong by the same amount. These measurements are called pseudoranges. “Pseudo” because they include the receiver's clock error.

That leaves four unknowns: latitude, longitude, height, and the clock error. Four unknowns need four equations, so the receiver needs four satellites. With a fifth it can check that its answer is consistent, which is a basic form of integrity checking.

The 2-D picture below shows it with circles. Three satellites solve for two position unknowns plus the clock error. With the clock wrong, the circles do not meet at one point. Solving for position and clock together finds the one point where all three circles would meet.

Try it: circles, clock error and the solve
0 units
Drag the green dot to move the receiver. Add clock error and the circles stop meeting. The red X shows where a receiver would think it was if it ignored the clock. Press Solve to recover the true position.

The message is slow

The orbit data that tells the receiver where each satellite is arrives at only 50 bits per second. A receiver that has just been switched on, with no stored orbit data, needs roughly half a minute just to collect it. That delay is why a cold start takes longer than a hot one.

  • Four unknowns (three position and one clock) need four satellites.

3 · Geometry and Error Budget

You will be able to
  • Explain dilution of precision (DOP) and tell good geometry from bad.
  • List the main sources of GPS error.
  • Estimate position error as DOP times range error.

Geometry: DOP

Imagine locating a point by sighting along two lines. If the lines cross at right angles you get a sharp fix. If they are almost parallel, a small error swings the crossing point a long way. Satellites work the same way.

Dilution of precision is the factor that says how much the satellite layout magnifies ranging errors. A low number is good.

NameMeaning
HDOPHorizontal (position on the ground)
VDOPVertical (height). Usually worse than HDOP, because every satellite is above you.
PDOPPosition in three dimensions
TDOPTime
GDOPEverything combined

Good geometry spreads satellites widely around the sky, with some high and some low. Bad geometry clusters them, or hides some behind terrain or buildings. A rough guide: under 2 is excellent, 2 to 5 is fair, above 5 is poor.

Where the errors come from

Each range measurement has errors. They add up, as a root sum of squares, to the user equivalent range error (UERE).

SourceWhat happens
Satellite clock and orbitSmall errors in where the satellite is and what time it says.
IonosphereCharged layers slow the signal. The delay depends on frequency, so a receiver listening on two frequencies can remove most of it.
TroposphereWeather and humidity slow the signal a little.
MultipathSignals bounce off buildings and ground and arrive late. Worst in cities and canyons.
ReceiverNoise inside the receiver.
position error (DRMS) ≈ HDOP × UERE
UERE = √( sum of each source squared ) 95% of fixes ≈ 2 × DRMS
Worked example

Sources of 4, 5, 1, 1 and 0.5 m combine to √(16 + 25 + 1 + 1 + 0.25) = √43.25 ≈ 6.6 m.

With HDOP 1.2 the horizontal error is 1.2 × 6.6 ≈ 7.9 m (DRMS). About 95% of fixes would fall within roughly 16 m.

With HDOP 4 in a bad canyon, the same receiver is off by about 26 m.

These numbers are for teaching. Real errors vary with receiver, weather, and place. For a sense of scale, GPS.gov says a typical smartphone is accurate to within about 5 metres under open sky, and a well-designed receiver does better. Techniques such as differential GPS and real-time kinematic (RTK) use a nearby reference station and the signal's carrier wave to get down to centimetres.

Defaults are illustrative teaching values, not a specification. Troposphere is fixed at 0.7 m and receiver noise at 0.5 m. Switching to two frequencies removes most of the ionosphere error. Compare the sky presets: same errors, very different results.

Summary
  • DOP measures how much the satellite layout magnifies range errors. Lower is better.
  • Error sources add as a root sum of squares into the UERE.
  • Position error ≈ DOP × UERE. A second frequency removes most ionosphere delay.

4 · The Signal: Faint, Shared, and Coded

You will be able to
  • Name the main GPS frequencies and signals.
  • Explain how all the satellites share one frequency.
  • Explain how a receiver pulls a signal out from below the noise.

Frequencies and signals

SignalFrequencyNotes
L1 C/A1575.42 MHzThe original civil signal. Open to everyone.
L21227.60 MHzLets dual-frequency receivers remove ionosphere delay.
L51176.45 MHzNewer civil signal for safety-of-life use.
P(Y)L1 and L2Encrypted military code. Hard to fake or read without keys.
M-codeMilitary bandsNewer military signal with added protection.

GPS signals use right-hand circular polarization, so the receiving antenna does not need to be turned to line up with them. Other systems broadcast nearby: Galileo and BeiDou have signals at or near the GPS frequencies, and GLONASS is around 1.6 GHz.

How the satellites share a frequency

Every GPS satellite transmits on the same frequency at the same time. They stay separable because each one multiplies its data by its own fast pseudo-random pattern, called a PRN code. This is code division multiple access (CDMA). To the receiver, a satellite's code looks like noise unless it holds a copy of that same code.

  • The civil C/A code is 1,023 chips long, sent at 1.023 million chips per second, so it repeats every 1 ms.
  • The navigation data is only 50 bits per second, so each data bit lasts 20 ms: 20 code repeats per bit.

That is a spread spectrum signal, just like Chapter 6 of the Modulation & Noise guide.

A signal below the noise

The minimum signal power the GPS specification promises at the ground is −158.5 dBW, or about −128.5 dBm. In the roughly 2 MHz the signal occupies, receiver noise is about −174 + 63 = −111 dBm. So the signal sits about 18 dB below the noise. You cannot see it on a spectrum analyzer.

The receiver recovers it by correlating: sliding its own copy of the code against what it hears and adding up how well they match. At the right delay the matches add up. At any other delay they cancel. The gain is the same processing gain you already know:

processing gain = 10 × log10(chips per data bit) = 10 × log10(1,023 × 20) ≈ 43 dB

The delay at which the match peaks is the travel time of the signal. That is how the receiver gets its pseudorange.

Try it: dig a signal out of the noise
Top: what the receiver hears, mostly noise. Bottom: the correlation across every possible delay of a 1,023-chip code. Add more code repeats and the spike rises out of the noise. The code here is a single 10-stage shift-register sequence, a simplified stand-in for the real C/A Gold codes.
Why a 17E caresThis is both GPS's strength and its weakness. Processing gain lets it work when the signal is far below noise. But that gain has a limit. A jammer that is about 30 to 40 dB stronger than the signal still overwhelms a typical receiver, and a jammer a few kilometres away is billions of times stronger than a signal from 20,200 km up.
Check yourself
Summary
  • GPS L1 is 1575.42 MHz, L2 is 1227.60 MHz, and L5 is 1176.45 MHz. Military signals are encrypted.
  • All satellites share a frequency using different PRN codes (CDMA). The C/A code repeats every 1 ms and the data runs at 50 b/s.
  • The signal is about 18 dB below noise. Correlation gives about 43 dB of processing gain and also measures the travel time.

5 · Using a Receiver: Datums, Grids, Waypoints

You will be able to
  • Say why the datum and coordinate system must match.
  • Read an MGRS grid reference and truncate it to the precision you need.
  • Use waypoints and a navigation pointer to move to a point.

The datum: a model of the Earth

GPS gives a position relative to a mathematical model of the Earth, called a datum. GPS itself uses WGS 84. Old paper maps may use a different datum. Plot the same numbers on two datums and the points can be tens or even hundreds of metres apart. So the first rule of using a receiver with a map is: set the receiver to the same datum as the map. For modern military maps that is WGS 84.

Coordinate systems

The same spot can be written several ways: latitude and longitude (degrees), UTM (metres east and north within a zone), or MGRS, the Military Grid Reference System. MGRS is UTM written compactly with letters, and it is what Soldiers use on the ground.

An MGRS reference has three parts:

  • Grid zone designation, such as 17S: a 6°-wide zone and an 8°-tall latitude band.
  • 100,000 m square identifier, two letters such as LS.
  • Numerical location: digits for easting first, then northing. Read right, then up.
DigitsPrecisionExample
4-digit1,000 m17S LS 94 98
6-digit100 m17S LS 940 984
8-digit10 m17S LS 9401 9843
10-digit1 m17S LS 94010 98430

Shorten by truncating. Never round. A point at 94,012.7 m east is still in the 100 m square that starts at 94,000, so its 6-digit easting is 940, not 941. Rounding puts you in the wrong square.

Try it: latitude and longitude to MGRS
The boxes show how big a square each precision describes, around your point. The 10-digit grid is 1 m wide. Tap Fort Gordon, then change the digits and watch the easting and northing get cut short, not rounded.

Grid, true and magnetic north

A grid bearing on the map is measured from grid north. A compass points to magnetic north. A receiver can show bearings against true, magnetic, or grid north. Check which one it is set to, and use the declination diagram on your map (see Land Navigation Explained) to convert.

Waypoints and the navigation pointer

A waypoint is a stored position. A DAGR can store a number of them and string them into a route. The usual sequence is: set datum and grid, mark your position as a waypoint before you move, then choose a waypoint and GOTO it. The navigation page shows an arrow and a bearing and distance to the waypoint. Walk the way the arrow points, and the distance counts down.

Remember what the receiver is doing: it is telling you where you are, not where to go. Check it against the ground and the map.

Try it: bearing and distance between two points
This is the bearing and distance a navigation pointer shows. Grid azimuth is measured from the map grid. True bearing is measured from the North Pole. They differ by a small angle called grid convergence, about 0.7° for the default points, and it grows with distance from the middle of the zone. Ground distance on the sphere is within about 0.1% of the true figure.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Match the receiver's datum and grid to the map: WGS 84 and MGRS.
  • MGRS is zone, 100 km square, then easting and northing. Truncate, never round.
  • Mark a waypoint before you move. GOTO a waypoint and follow the pointer.

6 · When GPS Fails or Lies

You will be able to
  • Tell jamming, spoofing, and meaconing apart.
  • Compute how much stronger a jammer is than the satellite signal.
  • Estimate how far dead reckoning drifts when GPS is gone.

Three attacks

AttackWhat the enemy doesWhat you see
JammingOverpowers the faint signal with noiseSatellites vanish and the receiver says no fix. Obvious.
SpoofingSends counterfeit satellite signalsA confident but wrong position or time. Subtle.
MeaconingRecords real signals and rebroadcasts them laterA position that looks valid but belongs to another time and place.

Jamming works because the signal is so weak. How much stronger than the satellite is the jammer where it reaches you? That ratio is the jammer-to-signal ratio, J/S. Free-space path loss at GPS L1 follows the same formula as before:

FSPL (dB) = 32.44 + 20·log10(1575.42) + 20·log10(distance in km)
J/S (dB) = jammer power at the receiver (dBm) − (−128.5 dBm)

A typical civil receiver loses full tracking when the jammer is roughly 30 to 40 dB above the signal. Receivers built to resist jamming can ride through far more. Terrain and distance help: the jammer needs a path to your antenna, and the radio horizon limits how far a low-mounted jammer can reach.

Try it: how much jammer does it take?
1 W is 30 dBm and 10 mW is 10 dBm. The line-of-sight limit is not included here. Use the GNSS Fundamentals Lab to add antenna height and the radio horizon.

Spotting a lie

  • Sudden jumps: your position leaps by more than normal error explains, or your time jumps.
  • Too good to be true: every satellite arrives at the same strength, or you suddenly see more satellites than the sky should allow.
  • Disagreement: GPS says one thing while the map, terrain, or an inertial system says another.
  • Loss of satellites all at once, or lost fixes in the same place every time.

Defences

MeasureHelps against
Encrypted military signalsSpoofing (the enemy cannot easily copy them)
Anti-jam antennas that null out a directionJamming from the ground
Inertial navigation (INS) and dead reckoningBoth: they do not depend on satellites
Map, compass, pace count, terrain associationEverything. It is your last line.
Reporting itWarns others. Jamming and interference are reported through your chain (MIJI reports).

Notice that using several constellations helps if one system fails, but a jammer on or near their shared frequencies can hit all of them at once.

Dead reckoning: how fast it drifts

If GPS goes, you fall back on direction and distance, which is the land navigation you already know. The trouble is that every small error piles up with distance. A heading error of 1° puts you about 17 m off to one side for every 1,000 m you walk. A pace count that is 5% off puts you 5% short or long.

Why a 17E caresYou may be the person who recognizes it first. Knowing a jammed receiver from a spoofed one, and being able to fall back on land navigation without panic, is part of the job. EMCON and EW planning also depend on timing that comes from GPS.

Summary

  • The signal is so weak that a small nearby jammer can be tens of dB stronger.
  • Dead reckoning drifts about 17 m per 1,000 m for each degree of heading error, plus pace-count error.

7 · Put It Together: A Navigation Plan

You will be able to
  • Build a primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency (PACE) plan for navigation.
  • Turn two grid references into a bearing, a distance, and a pace count.
  • Fall back from GPS to a compass leg correctly.

A navigation plan should never rest on one system. The same PACE idea used for radios works for navigation: a primary method, an alternate that is ready now, a contingency that works without any equipment, and an emergency that gets you home safely.

Try it: build a PACE plan

Scenario: a squad moves 6 km through wooded hills. The enemy is known to use GPS jammers. Fill in each slot.

Worked scenario: GPS goes down at the checkpoint

Step by step

Situation. You are at CP 2, 17S LS 9401 9843. GPS has just been jammed. The next point is OP 3 at 17S LS 9512 9902. You carry a map, a compass, and you know your pace count is 64 paces per 100 m. The map's declination diagram shows a G-M angle of 6° with magnetic north to the left of grid north.

1. Leg size. Easting changes by 9512 − 9401 = 111 tens of metres, which is 1,110 m. Northing changes by 9902 − 9843 = 59 tens, 590 m.

2. Distance. √(1,110² + 590²) = √(1,232,100 + 348,100) ≈ 1,257 m.

3. Grid azimuth. tan&supmin;¹(1,110 ÷ 590) ≈ 62.0°. East of north, so it is an azimuth of about 62°.

4. Magnetic azimuth. MN is left of GN and you are going grid to magnetic, so add the G-M angle (LARS: Left Add, Right Subtract): 62 + 6 = 68°.

5. Paces. 1,257 ÷ 100 × 64 ≈ 805 paces.

6. Move. Hold 68°, count 805 paces, and watch for the handrails and checkpoints you picked from the map. If the ground does not match the map, stop and find out why before you go on. The dead reckoning widget shows how much you can drift: with a 2° heading error and a 5% pace-count error, 1,257 m leaves you about 77 m off: roughly 44 m to one side and 63 m long or short.

Check yourself
Summary
  • Plan navigation with PACE: GPS first, map and compass ready, terrain association needing nothing, and a safe fall-back.
  • Grid references give easting and northing changes. Distance and azimuth follow from them.
  • Convert grid to magnetic with the G-M angle. Check the ground at every checkpoint.
