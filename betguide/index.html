What is Electricity?
The basics, made simple.

Electricity is just tiny charged particles pushed through a wire. Master four ideas — voltage, current, resistance, and power — and every circuit starts to make sense.

The four things that explain everything

You will be able to
  • Name voltage, current, resistance, and power and their units.
  • Find current and power for a simple circuit.

Before any gadget or circuit, there are four quantities. Learn what each one is and you can reason about almost any electrical problem.

💧 The water pipe analogy: imagine water flowing through a pipe. The pressure pushing the water is voltage. The amount of water flowing is current. Anything that narrows the pipe and slows the flow is resistance. And the work the water can do (spin a wheel) is power. This one picture carries you a long way.
V
Voltage
measured in Volts (V)
The push. How hard the electricity is being shoved through the wire. A 9 V battery pushes three times harder than a 3 V one.
I
Current
measured in Amps (A)
The flow. How much electric charge actually moves past a point each second. (The symbol is "I", from the French intensité.)
R
Resistance
measured in Ohms (Ω)
The opposition. How much the material fights the flow. More resistance = less current for the same push, and heat is given off.
P
Power
measured in Watts (W)
The work rate. How fast energy is used — a bright bulb or a hot resistor uses more watts than a dim one.
🔢One amp is a LOT of electrons. A current of 1 amp means about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons flow past a point every second — that's 6.24 billion-billion.
🌡️Resistance makes heat. When current fights through resistance, energy turns into heat. That's exactly how a toaster, a hair dryer, and an old light bulb work on purpose.
⚠️It's the current that hurts you, not the voltage alone. Voltage provides the push, but it's the amount of current through the body that's dangerous — which is why even low voltages can matter in the wrong conditions.

Worked example: all four quantities in one circuit

A 12V battery pushes current through a 4Ω resistor. Using Ohm's Law (covered fully in the next tab) to find current, then the power formula to find power:

I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 4 = 3A      P = V × I = 12 × 3 = 36W

Every one of the "Big 4" shows up in that single circuit: 12V pushing, 3A flowing, 4Ω resisting, 36W of work being done (as heat, in a plain resistor). This is the exact calculation the Circuit Bench tool runs live every time you place a battery and resistor.

Ohm's Law — the one equation to know

You will be able to
  • Use Ohm’s Law in any of its three forms.
  • Pick the power formula that fits what you know.

Voltage, current, and resistance are locked together by a single, famous relationship. If you know any two, you can always find the third.

V = I × R

"Voltage equals current times resistance." Rearranged: I = V ÷ R  and  R = V ÷ I.

👆 The magic triangle trick: write V on top, with I and R underneath. Cover the letter you want to find. Cover V → you see I × R. Cover I → you see V over R (divide). Cover R → you see V over I. The triangle hands you the formula every time.

See it move

Notice: turn the voltage up and current rises. Turn the resistance up and current falls. That push-and-fight balance is the whole idea.

Power ties in too

P = V × I

Power (watts) is voltage times current. Because of Ohm's Law you can also write it as P = I²R or P = V²÷R — three doors to the same room. This is how you figure out if a resistor will run cool or cook itself.

🎓Who was Ohm? Georg Ohm published this relationship in 1827. At first his work was dismissed — today his name is a unit of measurement used billions of times a day.

Worked example: picking the right power formula

Say you only know a resistor's value (220Ω) and the current through it (0.1A) — you don't have voltage directly. Rather than find voltage first, jump straight to the power formula that only needs the two values you already have:

P = I²R = (0.1)² × 220 = 0.01 × 220 = 2.2W

This is exactly why P = I²R and P = V²÷R exist alongside P = V × I — pick whichever formula matches the two quantities you actually have, instead of calculating an extra step you don't need. This also matters practically: it's how you check whether a resistor's wattage rating is high enough before it overheats.

AC vs DC — two ways electricity flows

You will be able to
  • Tell AC from DC.
  • Say what a frequency of 60 Hz means.

Electricity can flow steadily in one direction, or rapidly back and forth. Both are everywhere, and each is good at different jobs.

🔋
DC — Direct Current
flows one direction, steady

Direct current flows steadily from minus to plus, always the same direction. It's what batteries give you, and what almost all electronics run on internally — phones, radios, circuit boards.

Where you see it

Batteries, USB power, solar panels, every chip and sensor in your gear.

🔄
AC — Alternating Current
reverses direction many times a second

Alternating current constantly switches direction — in the US wall outlets it completes 60 cycles a second (60 Hz), reversing direction twice in every cycle. AC is easy to push over long distances and to step up or down with a transformer, which is why the power grid uses it.

Where you see it

Wall outlets, the power grid, large motors, and the RF signals that carry radio and Wi-Fi (very fast AC!).

What they look like on a scope

DC is a flat, steady line. AC is a smooth wave rising and falling above and below zero.

⚡The "War of Currents." In the 1880s–90s, Edison pushed DC while Tesla and Westinghouse pushed AC. AC won for power delivery because it travels long distances with far less loss.
📻Radio is just really fast AC. A 100 MHz FM signal is alternating current completing 100 million cycles a second. That's why RF theory and basic AC are the same family.

Series & parallel — two ways to wire things up

You will be able to
  • Compare series and parallel circuits.
  • Calculate total resistance both ways.

How you connect components decides how current and voltage split up. There are two basic ways, and every complex circuit is a mix of them.

➡️
Series — one single path
components in a line

In a series circuit everything sits on one loop, so the same current flows through every part. The resistances add up, and the voltage is shared among them (the drops add back up to the battery).

🎄 Old Christmas lights: wired in series, if one bulb burned out it broke the single path and the whole string went dark. That's series in a nutshell — one break stops everything.
🔀
Parallel — multiple paths
components side by side

In a parallel circuit each component gets its own branch, so every branch sees the same voltage (the full battery). The currents in the branches add up, and the total resistance actually drops below the smallest branch.

🏠 Your house wiring: outlets are in parallel. Each device gets the full 120 V, and unplugging one doesn't affect the others. One break only kills its own branch.
SeriesParallel
CurrentSame everywhereSplits between branches (adds up)
VoltageShared (drops add up)Same across each branch
Total resistanceAdds up (bigger)Less than the smallest branch
One part failsWhole circuit stopsOnly that branch stops

Worked example: adding up resistance both ways

Two 100Ω resistors in series: just add them directly.

R_total = 100 + 100 = 200Ω

The same two 100Ω resistors in parallel: use the parallel resistance formula instead, which is why parallel combinations always come out lower than either individual resistor.

R_total = (100 × 100) ÷ (100 + 100) = 10,000 ÷ 200 = 50Ω

Same two parts, same values — but wiring them differently gives 200Ω one way and 50Ω the other. That's the entire practical payoff of understanding series vs. parallel: it's a design choice that directly controls how much current a circuit draws.

The parts in the box

You will be able to
  • Name the main components and what each does.
  • Read a resistor color code.

Circuits are built from a small cast of characters. Here's what each one does, in plain terms.

🟫
Resistor
limits current

A deliberate "narrow spot" in the pipe. It limits how much current flows and drops some voltage. The colored bands on it are a code for its value.

🔵
Capacitor
stores charge briefly

A tiny rechargeable bucket. It charges up and releases energy quickly — great for smoothing out bumps in power and for timing. It blocks steady DC but lets AC "through."

🌀
Inductor
resists changes in current

A coil of wire that stores energy in a magnetic field and dislikes sudden changes in current. It passes DC easily but resists fast AC — the opposite personality to a capacitor.

▶️
Diode
one-way valve

Lets current flow one direction and blocks the other. This is what turns AC into DC (rectification). An LED is a diode that also gives off light.

🎛️
Transistor
switch & amplifier

A tiny electronic tap where a small signal controls a large one. It can switch things on/off or amplify a weak signal. Billions of them make up every computer chip.

🎨Resistor color code: the bands read as digits and a multiplier. A handy mnemonic for 0–9 is Black Brown Red Orange Yellow Green Blue Violet Gray White.

Worked example: reading a real resistor

A resistor with bands Brown, Black, Red: the first two bands are digits (Brown=1, Black=0 → "10"), and the third band is the multiplier (Red = ×100).

10 × 100 = 1,000Ω = 1kΩ

That's it — the same three-step read (digit, digit, multiplier) works for every standard resistor, which is exactly why the Circuit Bench tool can show you a resistor's color bands and value side by side.

💡Why LEDs need a resistor: an LED will grab as much current as it can and burn out. A small series resistor limits the current to a safe level — a classic first circuit to build.
