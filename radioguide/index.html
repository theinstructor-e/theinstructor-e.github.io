Tactical Radio Basics Explained

How tactical radios reach, hop, and stay secure; how to set one up and talk on it; and what to do when it will not work or the enemy interferes. Seven chapters with hands-on widgets.

1 · The Tactical Radio Picture

You will be able to
  • Name the main families of tactical radio and what each is good for.
  • Explain why VHF FM is the workhorse of the ground net.
  • Estimate line-of-sight range from antenna heights.

A unit moves, shoots, and survives on its ability to talk. The tactical radio picture is a set of tools, each with a job. No single radio does everything, so planners combine them.

FamilyTypical bandHow it travelsGood for
HFabout 3–30 MHzGround wave, or skywave bounced off the ionosphereLong range with no infrastructure. Slower data, antenna needs room.
VHF FM30–87.975 MHz (SINCGARS)Line of sightThe everyday squad, platoon, and company net. Voice and data.
UHFabout 225–400 MHz (military band)Line of sight, and up to satellitesAir-ground radios and tactical satellite links.
SATCOMSeveral bands, many in UHF and aboveVia a satelliteBeyond line of sight anywhere, at the cost of delay and a visible terminal.

Why VHF FM carries the ground net

  • Small antennas. At 40 MHz the wavelength is 7.5 m, so a quarter-wave whip is under 2 m. A radio that fits on a pack can carry it.
  • Clean voice. FM resists noise and fading better than AM (see Modulation & Noise Explained).
  • Predictable reach. Range is mostly line of sight, which suits a unit that already needs to see its neighbours.
  • Frequency hopping. SINCGARS can hop across its band to resist jamming and make direction finding harder (Chapter 3).

Half duplex, nets, and the NCS

Tactical radios are half duplex: you press the push-to-talk (PTT) key to send, release it to listen. You cannot talk and hear at once. Because of that, radio users form a net: a group of radios sharing a frequency or hopping pattern. One station acts as the net control station (NCS) and keeps order. Each station has a callsign so calls are short and clear.

PACE for communications

No radio is reliable enough to stand alone. Units plan with PACE: a primary way to communicate, an alternate, a contingency, and an emergency. A common set might be VHF voice, then a data or HF link, then satellite, then runner or visual signals. Your unit's plan decides, and everyone has to know it.

How far can I talk?

Above VHF, radio waves run in straight lines and the Earth curves away. The horizon limits line of sight. A standard rule of thumb, which already allows for a little bending of the signal, is:

line-of-sight range (km) ≈ 4.12 × ( √h1 + √h2 ) heights in metres
Try it: line-of-sight range
The shaded curve is the horizon. Raising either antenna helps, but the square root means height pays off slowly: 4 times the height gives only 2 times the reach. Hills, buildings, and trees make the real range shorter.
Why a 17E caresThe same facts that set your range also set the enemy's: a radio whose signal reaches your net also reaches any sensor with a line to it. Knowing your own horizon helps you plan both coverage and EMCON.

2 · Antennas, Power, and Range

You will be able to
  • Work out antenna length for a given frequency.
  • Explain why doubling power does not double range.
  • Use direction, height, and terrain to your advantage.

When a radio link is weak, people reach for power. But the antenna and the terrain usually matter more. A good operator fixes those first.

Antenna length follows wavelength

An antenna works best when its length matches the wavelength. The usual building blocks are:

wavelength λ (m) = 300 ÷ frequency (MHz)
quarter-wave whip ≈ λ ÷ 4 (practical length a few percent shorter)
half-wave dipole ≈ 142.5 ÷ frequency (MHz) metres

A SINCGARS radio covers 30 to 87.975 MHz, so antennas have to cope with wavelengths from about 10 m down to 3.4 m. Different antennas suit different parts of the band, and a field-expedient antenna is cut for the frequency you are using.

Also keep the polarization the same. Tactical VHF antennas are normally vertical, so hold the whip upright. A vertical antenna talking to a horizontal one loses on the order of 20 dB.

Try it: antenna length for any frequency
The bar shows the antenna lengths side by side, drawn to scale. A longer wavelength at the low end of the band needs a longer antenna.

Power: expensive and not as useful as you think

In free space, signal weakens by 6 dB each time distance doubles. Radio power is measured the same way: 6 dB is 4 times the power. So to double the range you need four times the power. Going from 5 W to 50 W is ten times the power but only about 3.2 times the range, and it costs battery life and makes you easier to detect.

Try it: how much does power buy?
Free space, equal antennas, same receiver. Terrain and obstacles will cut these ranges. Relative to the 5 W setting.

Better ways to gain range

  • Get the antenna up and clear. Height extends the horizon. Move the antenna away from vehicles, wires, and trees.
  • Aim a directional antenna. It sends and hears in one direction, which lengthens the link and, as a bonus, shrinks what an enemy off to the side can hear.
  • Use terrain. A ridge can block your signal from an enemy position. It can also block your friend. Plan where both are.
  • Use a remote antenna. Moving the antenna away from the radio and the command post improves coverage and keeps the vehicle itself from being the thing that gets located. Army guidance suggests offset antennas more than 1 km away where possible for command post radios.
Why a 17E caresThese are the EMCON techniques from the other guide in practical form: use the least power that works, direct the signal, and use terrain to hide it. A radio check at lower power tells you whether you really needed the extra watts.

3 · Frequency Hopping and COMSEC

You will be able to
  • Describe single-channel and frequency-hopping modes.
  • List what two radios must share to hop together.
  • Tell COMSEC from TRANSEC.

Two modes

A SINCGARS radio can work two ways.

  • Single channel (SC). It sits on one fixed frequency, which you type in. It also works with older radios. But a fixed frequency is easy to find, listen to, and jam.
  • Frequency hopping (FH). The radio jumps among many frequencies, about 100 times a second, in a pattern only the net knows. The band runs 30 to 87.975 MHz in 25 kHz steps, which is 2,320 possible channels. The hopset is a smaller selection from them. FH is the electronic protection built into the radio.

What two radios must share

To hop together, every radio in the net needs the same set of data, and a clock that agrees.

ItemWhat it is
HopsetThe set of frequencies the radio hops among.
Lockout setFrequencies to leave out, for example ones in use by friendly systems.
Net IDA three-digit number that sets where in the pattern the net starts.
TRANSEC key (TSK)The key that sets the order in which the radio hops within the hopset.
TimeThe radios must agree on the time. SINCGARS can tolerate roughly four seconds of difference and still stay in sync.

Opening a hopping net uses a cold start: the net first talks on a single-channel frequency while members load the hopping data. A radio that has been out of the net can rejoin with late net entry, passively by listening for traffic, or by being cued. The next chapter shows how the data gets into the radio.

COMSEC and TRANSEC are different

  • COMSEC (communications security) protects the content. In cipher text (CT) mode the voice or data is encrypted. In plain text (PT) mode it is not. Both radios must agree, or the audio will be unintelligible.
  • TRANSEC (transmission security) protects the fact that you are transmitting and where. Hopping is TRANSEC. It resists jamming and makes the signal harder to find.

You can have either without the other. A hopping radio in plain text hides where it is but not what it says. A single-channel radio in cipher text hides what it says but not that it is there.

Try it: will these radios talk?
Each dot is one hop of radio A (green) and radio B (cyan). When all four hopping items match, every cyan ring sits around a green dot. Teaching model: real radios resynchronize and use late net entry.
Why a 17E caresHopping resists jamming and finding, but it is only as good as the data and the clock. A radio with the wrong net ID looks like a dead radio and a radio in the wrong COMSEC mode looks like a jammed one. Knowing what to check first saves time and keeps you off the air.
Check yourself
Summary
  • SC uses a typed frequency. FH hops about 100 times a second over a hopset in the 2,320-channel band.
  • To hop together: same hopset, lockouts, net ID, TRANSEC key, and the time within a few seconds.
  • COMSEC protects content. TRANSEC protects the fact and place of transmission. They are independent.

4 · Operating the Radio

You will be able to
  • Put a SINCGARS radio on the net in the right order.
  • Say what the function switch positions do.
  • Convert a typed frequency to MHz and check it is valid.

This chapter follows the same steps as the SINCGARS Radio Simulator, using the RT-1523 family (the AN/PRC-119 series). Your radio, your signal operating instructions (SOI), and your unit's SOP decide the exact settings. The order of the steps is what you learn here.

The function (FCTN) switch

PositionWhat it does
OFFTurns primary power off. A backup keeps memory.
TSTRuns the radio’s self-test. Wait for GOOD.
LDLoad mode. You enter or receive frequencies and data here.
SQ ONRadio on, squelch on. The normal operating position. The speaker is silent until a signal arrives.
SQ OFFRadio on, squelch off. You hear noise between signals. Useful when you must hear a very weak signal.
RXMTRetransmit. The radio relays traffic between two nets.
Zeroize positionsClear the loaded data. Several positions are guarded so they cannot be reached by accident.

For listening silence, use SQ ON rather than turning the radio off. It keeps the radio ready and loaded while it transmits nothing. This is an EMCON habit.

The sequence

  1. Mate the antenna base with ANT and hand-tighten.
  2. Plug the handset into AUD/DATA, lining up the keys.
  3. Turn FCTN to TST and wait for GOOD.
  4. Turn FCTN to LD.
  5. Set CHAN, PWR, MODE, and COMSEC from your card (for a single-channel radio check that is MODE SC and COMSEC PT).
  6. Press FREQ, then CLR, key in the frequency, and press STO within about 7 seconds.
  7. Turn FCTN to SQ ON.
  8. Make the radio check: callsigns, what you want, over.
  9. Acknowledge, out.

Frequencies are typed as five digits with no decimal. 40500 means 40.500 MHz. Standard channels fall on 25 kHz steps, so the last digits are 00, 25, 50, or 75.

Why a 17E caresMost failed radio checks are a skipped step or a wrong number, not a broken radio. The order also protects you: TST first proves the radio is healthy, and SQ ON last means you are not transmitting or hearing noise while you load data.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Antenna and handset first, then TST, then LD, then load, then SQ ON, then radio check.
  • Type frequencies as five digits. 40500 is 40.500 MHz. Standard channels are 25 kHz apart.
  • Use SQ ON, not OFF, for listening silence.

5 · Voice Procedure

You will be able to
  • Structure a call so it is short and clear.
  • Use prowords correctly, and avoid the common wrong ones.
  • Give and answer a radio check.

Voice procedure is a shared set of habits so that strangers on a noisy net understand each other the first time. The allied standard is ACP 125, and Army guidance follows it. Short, standard calls also mean less time on the air, which is good EMCON.

The shape of a call

[who you are calling], this is [who you are], [message], [OVER or OUT]

Example: “Alpha 6, this is Alpha 1, radio check, over.” Say the station you are calling first so they know it is for them. Then say who you are. Then the message. End with a proword.

Think before you key the handset. Know what you will say. Pause a second after pressing PTT so the first word is not cut off. Speak clearly, at a steady pace, not louder.

Prowords

Prowords are short words with fixed meanings. Tap any card.

Try it: proword cards
Tap a proword to see what it means and an example.

Words not to use

  • “Over and out.” They contradict each other. OVER means I expect a reply. OUT means I do not.
  • “Roger wilco.” WILCO already includes ROGER. Use one.
  • “Repeat.” In some contexts it means “fire again.” Say SAY AGAIN instead.

The radio check

A radio check asks how well you are heard. It reports signal strength (loud, weak, very weak) and readability (clear, readable, distorted, with interference, unreadable). If the answer is loud and clear, the shortcut is just ROGER. Anything else must be described.

Try it: answer the radio check
Words like AND and BUT are chosen by whether the two descriptions agree or contrast. A numeric 1 to 5 readability scale is also common in some nets: 1 unreadable, 2 readable now and then, 3 readable with difficulty, 4 readable, 5 perfectly readable.

Numbers, names, and spelling

Use the phonetic alphabet for letters and say numbers one digit at a time. For nine, say “niner.” Use “I SPELL” before a word you are spelling out. Follow your SOP for exact pronunciation. For anything sensitive, use the unit's authentication and brevity codes, and do not say it plainly on an open net.

Why a 17E caresEvery second you transmit gives an enemy sensor a chance to hear you and take a bearing. Standard calls shorten transmissions and cut repeats. Doing it right is the cheapest EMCON you have.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Call shape: who you call, this is who you are, message, over or out.
  • OVER and OUT are opposites. ROGER and WILCO are not used together. Say SAY AGAIN, not repeat.
  • ROGER answers a radio check when you are loud and clear. Otherwise describe strength and readability.

6 · Interference and Jamming

You will be able to
  • Tell enemy jamming from interference and from your own equipment trouble.
  • Describe actions to keep communicating and to report it.
  • Say what not to do over the radio when you suspect jamming.

Radios fail for ordinary reasons far more often than because of the enemy. The skill is working out which one you are facing, quickly, without making things worse.

CauseTypical sign
Your own equipmentOnly your radio is affected. Other stations hear each other fine.
Interference from friendly sourcesNoise that starts when a generator, vehicle, or another radio near you is running. It changes when you move.
Natural or unintended interferenceDistance, terrain, weather, or another net on a nearby frequency.
Enemy jammingNoise, tones, or odd signals on your net that affect many radios, often starting at tactically meaningful moments.
Try it: what could it be?

Tick what you notice, then see what it points to. This is a thinking aid, not a diagnosis.

Keep communicating

Army doctrine treats overcoming jamming as a skill set: recognize it, react, and report. The specific procedures are in your radio manual, your SOP, and ATP 6-02.53, Chapter 10. In general terms:

  • Check yourself first. Rule out your own equipment: antenna, battery, cables, settings.
  • Reposition. Move the antenna or radio, or put terrain between you and the interference source. Turn a directional antenna toward your friendly station.
  • Keep transmissions short. Use brevity codes and standard procedure.
  • Fall back on your PACE plan. Move to the alternate: another net, a data link, a runner, or visual signals.
  • Stay in frequency-hopping mode when you can. A hopper is harder to jam than a fixed channel.
  • Report it. Meaconing, intrusion, jamming, and interference reports (MIJI) go through the channel your SOP names, so the staff can see the pattern.

What not to do: do not announce on an open, unsecured net that you are being jammed or how badly. That tells the enemy the jamming works. Use the reporting channel your SOP specifies.

Why a 17E caresYou are often the one who is asked, “Is that jamming?” Your answer depends on the same checks used here. If you can show it is not your equipment, and that it follows a pattern, you have given the commander something real.
Check yourself
Summary
  • Rule out your own equipment first, then local interference, then think jamming.
  • Reposition, shorten transmissions, stay in FH mode, and fall back on PACE.
  • Report MIJI through the proper channel. Never discuss the effects of jamming on an open net.

7 · Put It Together: No Comms

You will be able to
  • Work a no-communications problem in a sensible order.
  • Do the checks without needlessly transmitting.
  • Tie together antennas, hopping, COMSEC, procedure, and EMCON.

“Alpha 6, this is Alpha 1, radio check, over.” Silence. Now what? Work the problem from simplest to most complex, and from your end outward. A good order is:

  1. Power: battery installed and charged, function switch in an operating position.
  2. Antenna and cables: connected, tight, undamaged, and upright.
  3. Handset and audio: plugged in, volume up.
  4. Settings: function switch, squelch, channel, mode.
  5. Frequency or hopping data: the right frequency; the right net ID, hopset, and time.
  6. COMSEC: both radios in the same mode, with the right key.
  7. Range and terrain: are you beyond the horizon or behind a hill?
  8. Then think interference or jamming (Chapter 6).

Do the checks that need no transmission first. You can look at a connector, read a display, and hear a squelch break without sending anything. Each transmission you avoid is one less chance for an enemy sensor to hear you.

Putting it all together

  • Range: set by antenna height, aiming, terrain, and frequency (Chapters 1 and 2).
  • Resistance: hopping, COMSEC, and short calls (Chapters 3 and 5).
  • Discipline: standard order of operations and standard procedure (Chapters 4 and 5).
  • Resilience: PACE and calm troubleshooting (Chapters 6 and 7).
  • Signature: everything here that shortens or directs a transmission also makes you harder to find. See EMCON Explained.

Summary
  • Troubleshoot in order: power, antenna, handset, settings, frequency or hopping data, COMSEC, range, then interference.
  • Do non-transmitting checks first.
