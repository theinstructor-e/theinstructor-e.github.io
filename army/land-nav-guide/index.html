Simple Tech

Land Navigation
Know where you are and where you are going.

Read the map, measure a direction, walk a distance, and check the ground. Six short tabs take you from the grid to a full route.

Maps and grids — the picture and the address system

A military map is a scaled picture of the ground with a grid laid over it. The grid gives every spot an address, so you can tell someone exactly where something is without describing it.

🏙️ Like a city street map, but for everywhere: instead of "Main Street and 5th," you give a short number that points to one 100-meter square.

What the margin tells you

Before you use any map, read the margin. It is where the map explains itself.

ScaleHow much ground one map unit covers (the representative fraction, like 1:50,000). Bar scales in the lower margin are rulers in different units.Contour noteThe contour interval: the elevation change between neighboring contour lines. It sits below the bar scales.Declination diagramShows how true north, grid north, and magnetic north relate. It is in the lower margin.LegendWhat the symbols and colors mean on this sheet.

What the colors mean

ColorWhat it shows
BlackMan-made (cultural) features like buildings and roads, spot elevations, and all labels
BlueWater: lakes, swamps, rivers, and drainage
GreenVegetation that matters militarily, such as woods and orchards
BrownRelief and elevation, such as contour lines on standard maps
Red-brownSome cultural features and relief features, and contours on red-light readable maps

Scale: turning map distance into ground distance

At 1:50,000, 1 cm on the map is 500 m on the ground. At 1:25,000, 1 cm is 250 m. At 1:100,000, 1 cm is 1 km. On 1:50,000 and 1:25,000 maps the grid squares are 1,000 m (1 km) on a side.

Reading a grid coordinate

Grid lines are numbered along the edges. To give a location, read right, then up: first the vertical line to the left of the point (the easting), then the horizontal line below it (the northing). Then estimate how many tenths of the square you have moved past each line.

DigitsPrecisionExample
4-digit1,000 m (a whole grid square)4122
6-digit100 m413 227
8-digit10 m4135 2274
10-digit1 m41352 22741
🧮Worked example. A point sits past vertical line 41 and above horizontal line 22. It is 3 tenths of the way across the square and 7 tenths of the way up. Easting = 41 + 3 tenths = 413. Northing = 22 + 7 tenths = 227. The 6-digit grid is 413 227.
🌐MGRS. A full Military Grid Reference System reference also names the grid zone and the 100,000 m square, for example 18S UJ 413 227 (an example only). Inside one map sheet you can usually drop the zone and square.

Reading the relief

Contour lines connect points of equal elevation. Lines close together mean a steep slope. Lines far apart mean gentle ground. The heavy index contours carry elevation numbers, the lighter intermediate contours fill in between, and dashed supplementary contours show extra detail on very flat ground.

Direction and azimuths

An azimuth is a horizontal angle measured clockwise from a north base line. It is the standard way Soldiers give direction.

🕛 Like a clock face you read in degrees: straight ahead north is 0°, east is 90°, south is 180°, west is 270°, and you always count clockwise.

Degrees and mils

A circle is 360 degrees or 6,400 mils. One degree is about 17.8 mils. Your compass shows both. Always check which unit you are working in before you read or set anything.

Back azimuth

A back azimuth is the opposite direction. If the azimuth is less than 180°, add 180°. If it is 180° or more, subtract 180°. In mils, use 3,200.

AzimuthBack azimuth
90°270°

Three norths

TNTrue north: the direction to the North Pole.GNGrid north: the direction the vertical grid lines on your map point.MNMagnetic north: where your compass needle points.

Your map uses grid north. Your compass uses magnetic north. The angle between them is the G-M angle, and the declination diagram in the map margin shows it. If you ignore it, your map azimuth and your compass azimuth will not match.

Converting between grid and magnetic

Look at the declination diagram. Is magnetic north to the left (west) or the right (east) of grid north?

ConvertingMN is left of GNMN is right of GN
Grid → magneticAdd the G-M angleSubtract the G-M angle
Magnetic → gridSubtract the G-M angleAdd the G-M angle
🔤Memory aid: LARS. Left Add, Right Subtract when you go from grid to magnetic. Going the other way, do the opposite. Recent maps also print a conversion note beside the declination diagram. Trust the note on your own map.
🧮Worked example. G-M angle is 8° with magnetic north to the left of grid north. Grid azimuth 90° → magnetic azimuth 90° + 8° = 98°. Going back: compass reads 98° → grid azimuth 98° − 8° = 90°.
📏Why it matters. A 1° error is about 17 m off target for every 1,000 m you walk. An 8° miss over 1 km puts you about 140 m away.

Compass and protractor

The map gives you a grid azimuth. The compass turns it into a direction you can walk. The protractor is how you measure that grid azimuth on the map in the first place.

The lensatic compass

🎯
Sighting wire and lens
Line up

The wire in the cover and the slot in the lens work like the front and rear sight on a rifle. Sight on an object to get an exact azimuth.

🧭
Floating dial
The scales

The dial floats free when the lens is open past 45°. It has two scales: the outer scale is in mils and the inner (usually red) scale is in degrees.

⚙️
Bezel ring
Presets a direction

It clicks as you turn it: 120 clicks per full turn, 3° per click. The short luminous line lines up with the north-seeking arrow for night navigation.

📍
Black index line
Reference

When you hold the compass correctly, the number under the fixed black index line is the direction the compass is pointing.

Two ways to hold it

TechniqueHow it worksBest for
Center-holdOpen the compass flat, hold it at chest level with elbows tight to your sides, and turn your whole body toward the object. Read the dial by looking down.Moving. Keeping a general direction. Works in any visibility.
Compass-to-cheekHold the compass up to your cheek and sight through the slot and the wire at a distant object, reading the azimuth through the lens.A more precise azimuth to a specific point.
⚠️Keep it clean. Metal, vehicles, power lines, and electronics can pull the needle. Take readings away from them, and check your azimuth again if the needle looks off.

The protractor: measuring a grid azimuth on the map

1Draw a straight line from your start point to your objective.2Find where that line crosses a vertical (north-south) grid line.3Put the protractor’s index mark on that crossing, with its base line along the grid line and its 0° end pointing to grid north.4Read the scale where your line crosses it. That is the grid azimuth.

Then convert it to a magnetic azimuth (see the Direction tab) before you set it on your compass.

Distance and pace count

The map tells you how far it is. Your pace count tells you when you have walked that far.

Distance on the map

Measure the straight-line distance with the bar scale or a ruler, then convert with the scale. For a winding road or trail, lay a strip of paper along it, mark the bends, and measure the strip. Remember that the map shows distance on a flat plane. On steep ground you walk farther than the map says.

Pace count

A pace is two steps, counted each time the same foot hits the ground. Your pace count is how many paces it takes you to cover 100 m. It is personal, so measure yours on a marked 100 m course while walking normally.

🧮Paces to meters. meters = (paces counted ÷ your pace count per 100 m) × 100. If your count is 64 per 100 m and you counted 192 paces: 192 ÷ 64 × 100 = 300 m.
🧮Meters to paces. paces = (meters ÷ 100) × your pace count. To walk 450 m at 64 per 100 m: 4.5 × 64 = 288 paces.

Ranger beads

Ranger beads keep count for you. Each of the nine lower beads stands for 100 m: slide one down every time you finish a pace count of 100 m. At 1,000 m, reset the lower beads and slide one of the four upper beads, which stand for 1,000 m each.

What changes your pace count

SlopeUphill and downhill both change your stride.SurfaceMud, sand, snow, loose rock, and thick brush shorten it.LoadA heavy ruck and body armor change it.YouFatigue, injury, and speed change it.WeatherWind, rain, ice, and heat change it.

If you can, measure your pace count on terrain like your route, and re-check it during a long movement.

Terrain association

Terrain association is matching what you see on the ground to what the map shows. It keeps you from trusting a number when the ground is telling you something different.

👀 Like matching a photo to a place: you look at the hill in front of you, find the same hill on the map, and know where you are.

The ten terrain features

FeatureOn the groundOn the map
Hill (major)A point or small area of high groundClosed contour circles, the smallest one at the top
Ridge (major)A line of high ground with minor crestsContour U or V shapes pointing away from higher ground
Valley (major)Fairly level ground with higher ground on the sides, usually with a streamContour U or V shapes pointing toward higher ground
Saddle (major)A low point between two areas of higher groundAn hourglass shape between two hills
Depression (major)A low point surrounded by higher ground on all sidesClosed contour lines with tick marks pointing downhill
Draw (minor)A small stream course with little or no level groundContour V shapes pointing toward higher ground
Spur (minor)A short line of higher ground jutting out from a ridgeContour shapes pointing away from higher ground
Cliff (minor)A very steep or vertical faceContour lines pressed together, sometimes merged
Cut (supplementary)Ground removed for a road or railroadA man-made notch shown with special symbols
Fill (supplementary)An embankment built up for a road or railroadA man-made ridge shown with special symbols
💧Quick tell. Water runs down valleys and draws, so their contour V’s point uphill. Ridges and spurs shed water, so their V’s point downhill, away from the high ground.

Tools for staying on line

HandrailA long feature you can follow: a stream, a road, a ridge line.Catching featureSomething big beyond your objective that tells you that you have gone too far.Attack pointAn easy-to-find spot near your objective. Navigate to it first, then make the last short leg carefully.CheckpointA point along the way where you confirm that map and ground still agree.

The military view: OAKOC

Leaders also read terrain for its tactical value: Observation and fields of fire, Avenues of approach, Key terrain, Obstacles, and Cover and concealment. This is the "T" in METT-TC when planning.

Putting it together

Real land navigation combines everything: the map, the grid, the azimuth, the distance, and the terrain.

Moving from A to B (dead reckoning)

Orient and plot
Orient the map to north. Plot your start point and your objective using their grid coordinates.
Measure the leg
Use the protractor for the grid azimuth and the scale for the distance.
Convert
Change the grid azimuth to a magnetic azimuth with the G-M angle. Do not skip this.
Pick your aids
Choose handrails, checkpoints, an attack point, and a catching feature. Break a long leg into short ones.
Move
Hold your azimuth, count your paces, and keep looking for your checkpoints.
Check
At each checkpoint, does the ground match the map? If not, stop and find out why before you go on.

Where am I? Resection

Resection finds your own position on the map. Shoot magnetic azimuths to at least two well-defined points you can find on the map, convert them to grid, take the back azimuths, and draw those lines from each point. Where the lines cross is where you are.

Intersection is the opposite: you find an unknown point (say, a target) by shooting azimuths to it from at least two known positions.

Common mistakes

SkippedForgetting to convert grid to magnetic.FlippedAdding when you should subtract because you misread which side magnetic north is on.MixedMixing degrees and mils.ReversedReading the grid "up, then right" instead of "right, then up."StaleUsing an old pace count on new terrain, or never re-checking it.BlindTrusting the compass when the ground clearly disagrees.
