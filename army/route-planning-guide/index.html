Simple Tech

Route Planning
Picking a path that doesn't pick a fight.

Legs, control measures, danger areas, and the crossing techniques that keep a patrol moving without getting caught in the open.

Route planning — the leader's job before anyone moves

A route is more than a line on a map. It's a plan for exactly how a unit gets from here to there without walking into an ambush, wasting the mission clock, or losing itself along the way.

🗺️ Think of it like a road trip with checkpoints: you don't just point at the destination — you break the drive into legs, mark the rest stops, and already know which intersections need extra caution before you get there.

Break the route into legs

The best way to manage a route is to divide it into segments called legs. Breaking the overall route into smaller pieces lets the leader plan pace count, azimuth, and terrain association in manageable chunks instead of trying to hold the whole route in their head at once.

Control measures that keep everyone on the same page

📍
Checkpoints
Point features

A specific point used for reference or control — also called a waypoint.

📏
Phase Lines
Linear features

A line used to control and coordinate movement, often tied to an identifiable terrain feature.

🚩
Rally Points
Regroup locations

Easily identifiable points where a unit can reassemble if it gets separated or makes contact.

Other measures a leader may put on the route include the Objective Rally Point (ORP) — the last stop before the objective — the Probable Line of Deployment (PLD), the Limit of Advance (LOA), and the location of a patrol base or tactical assembly area (TAA).

Navigation aid techniques

Three techniques help a leader stay on track between control measures:

Catching featuresA terrain feature beyond your objective that tells you you've gone too far, so you can stop and reorient before getting lost.HandrailsA linear feature — a road, ridge, or stream — that you navigate alongside without walking directly on it.Attack pointsA well-defined feature close to the objective, used as a final "jump-off" point for precise navigation over the last stretch.

Danger areas — the fatal funnel

A danger area is any place on the route where the terrain could expose the patrol to enemy observation, fire, or both. The best plan avoids them. When that's not possible, the unit crosses quickly, carefully, and with security in place.

🎯 Think of a linear danger area as a fatal funnel: a long, open lane — a road, trail, or stream — where the enemy can concentrate fire on you the entire time you're crossing.

Three general types

➖
Linear
Long axis

A road, trail, stream, or similar feature with a long axis running across your route. Cannot usually be bypassed.

🔲
Small Open Area
Can bypass

A clearing small enough that going around it costs little time.

⬛
Large Open Area
May not bypass

A field or clearing big enough that bypassing it could blow the mission timeline.

Whenever the lead element signals "danger area," the whole unit halts. The leader moves forward, confirms the danger area, and decides how the unit will treat it based on METT-TC. If it can't be bypassed — as with most linear danger areas — the leader determines the crossing site, near- and far-side rally points, the crossing method, and where near-side security goes.

🔀Near-side and far-side rally points. These are designated before the crossing so that if the unit makes contact mid-crossing, everyone already knows where to reassemble — no time wasted deciding under fire.

Crossing techniques

Which technique you use depends on the type of danger area and how much time the mission gives you.

Bypass (detour) method — for open areas you can go around

The unit turns 90 degrees around the open area and moves in the direction of travel. Once past the danger area, it completes the box with another 90-degree turn and arrives at the far-side rally point. The pace count of the offset and return legs isn't added to the planned route distance.

Contour method — hugging the edge

The patrol "hugs" the perimeter of the danger area until it resumes its original azimuth. This works well when the terrain has identifiable features to follow, but it's less precise than the bypass method and keeps the unit closer to the danger area longer.

Linear danger area crossing — when you can't go around it

Designate near- and far-side rally points
Emplace near-side security
Left and right security elements overwatch the crossing site.
Clear the far side
A team crosses first and confirms it's clear before the rest follows.
Continue the unit crossing
Retrieve near-side security and complete the crossing
⚡Speed versus security. A squad moving on its own can also cross a linear danger area using the bounding overwatch movement technique, trading some security for a much faster crossing. Every technique is a trade-off — quick and quiet is the goal either way.

Large open area — when you can't bypass it either

If the open area is too large to bypass without blowing the time available, the unit crosses using a combination of traveling overwatch and bounding overwatch — traveling overwatch to save time, transitioning to bounding overwatch as the risk increases.

Worked example — a squad route with two danger areas

This is a sample scenario for illustration. Real route planning always uses the actual map and terrain in front of you.

📋 The route: a squad patrol moves 1,200 meters from its patrol base to an observation post, crossing a dirt road at the halfway point and a 150-meter-wide field near the objective.
LegFeatureLeader's plan
1Patrol base to dirt road (600m)Handrail along a tree line paralleling the road for most of the leg.
Danger area 1Dirt road (linear)Cannot bypass — full linear crossing: near/far rally points, near-side security left and right, clear far side, cross, retrieve security.
2Road to field edge (450m)Catching feature: a streambed 500m out tells the squad if it has drifted off azimuth.
Danger area 2150m field (small/medium open area)Bypass method — 90° turn, skirt the tree line around the field, 90° turn back onto azimuth at the far-side rally point.
3Field to OP (150m)Attack point: a lone dead tree 50m from the OP used as the final jump-off point.
💡What to notice. The squad leader picked a different technique for each danger area based on its type — a full linear crossing for the road, a quicker bypass for the field — and used navigation aids to stay on track between them.
