Troop Leading Procedures — the leader's plan in 8 steps

You will be able to
  • List the eight steps of TLP in order.
  • Apply the one-third, two-thirds rule.

TLP is the routine a small-unit leader runs to take a mission from "here's the task" to "we're ready to go." It helps you use your time well and keeps your team from finding out about the plan at the last minute.

✈️ Like a pilot’s pre-flight routine: the same steps every time, in a sensible order, so nothing important gets skipped when you are busy.

Who uses it

TLP is for leaders who plan without a dedicated staff, typically at company level and below. Battalion and higher headquarters use the MDMP. Step 3 of TLP is where the MDMP way of thinking shows up, done quickly by one leader.

The eight steps

Receive the mission
Figure out what you have been told to do, and why.
Issue a warning order
Get your team moving early with a heads-up.
Make a tentative plan
Analyze the situation and pick a course of action.
Initiate movement
Start any movement that cannot wait for the finished plan.
Conduct reconnaissance
Check your assumptions against the ground and the enemy.
Complete the plan
Finalize it in light of what you learned.
Issue the order
Brief the operation order.
Supervise and refine
Rehearse, inspect, and adjust until the moment you execute.

The one-third, two-thirds rule

Use no more than one third of the time you have for your own planning. That leaves at least two thirds for your subordinates to plan, rehearse, and prepare. If you have 12 hours before you move, your plan and your order should be done in about 4.

It is a loop, not a straight line

The steps are numbered, but they overlap. You will often start step 4 before you finish step 3. A recon in step 5 can send you back to change the plan from step 3. Step 8 never really ends. Leaders keep supervising until the mission starts.

Steps 1–4 — Getting started

You will be able to
  • Say what happens in steps 1 to 4.
  • Say what a warning order covers.

The first four steps get you oriented, get your team moving, and give you a draft plan. Speed matters here, because everything your team does later depends on the time you give them.

Step 1: Receive the mission

You may get the mission as a warning order, an operation order, or a fragmentary order. Read it for the task and purpose of your unit, the higher commander's intent, and any times or limits you have been given.

Find your task and purpose
What must your unit do, and what is it for?
Work the clock backward
Start from the time you must execute and list what has to happen before then. This is your time plan.
Note what is fixed
Times, locations, and limits from higher headquarters are not yours to change.

Step 2: Issue a warning order

Send a warning order (WARNORD) as soon as you have something useful to say. Do not wait for a perfect plan. A short early WARNORD lets your team start preparing while you keep planning. It usually covers the situation as you know it, the mission or the type of operation, the time schedule (when the order, rehearsals, and movement are), and any instructions to get moving. Issue updates as you learn more.

Step 3: Make a tentative plan

Analyze the situation with METT-TC, then build a course of action and check whether it works.

MMission: your task, purpose, and higher intentEEnemy: what they are, where they are, what they can doTTerrain and weather: use OAKOC to read the groundTTroops and support: what you have and what shape it is inTTime available: how much clock you haveCCivil considerations: people, places, and infrastructure
ℹ️Newer doctrine adds an "I". You may see METT-TC(I), with informational considerations added to the list.

Small-unit leaders often work with one main course of action rather than several. Think it through as a "what if": what do we do, what might the enemy do, and how do we react? Check for risk and write down the plan in outline.

Step 4: Initiate movement

If something needs to move before the plan is final, start it now. That might be getting to an assembly area, sending a recon element ahead, or pulling ammunition and gear. Moving early buys time for later steps.

Steps 5–8 — Finishing and preparing

You will be able to
  • Say what happens in steps 5 to 8.
  • Name the parts of supervise and refine.

The last four steps turn a tentative plan into a rehearsed, understood plan.

Step 5: Conduct reconnaissance

Confirm what you assumed. A ground recon is best, when the situation allows. If it does not, do a map recon. Look at the route, the objective, and any place that could surprise you. Ask: is the ground the way the map says, and is the enemy where I expect?

Step 6: Complete the plan

Update the tentative plan with what you learned. Prepare what you need to brief it: a sketch, an overlay, or a terrain model. Check the plan against the higher commander's intent. Coordinate with the units next to you and with supporting elements.

Step 7: Issue the order

Brief the operation order in its five-paragraph format: Situation, Mission, Execution, Sustainment, Command and Signal. Give it where your team can see the terrain or a model of it, if you can. Check that your subordinates understand it by having them repeat back key parts.

Step 8: Supervise and refine

Do not assume the plan will be carried out as briefed. Go and look. This step has three main parts.

🎭
Rehearse
Practice the plan

A rehearsal finds gaps and builds shared understanding. Common types: confirmation brief, back-brief, support rehearsal, combined arms rehearsal, and battle drill or SOP rehearsal.

🔎
Inspect
Check readiness

Leaders run pre-combat checks (PCC) and pre-combat inspections (PCI): weapons, radios, gear, water, ammunition, and whether every Soldier knows the plan.

🔁
Refine
Adjust

Change the plan if the situation changes, and tell everyone. Keep supervising until you execute.

📝Rehearsal techniques. You can rehearse full-dress (the whole unit with equipment), reduced-force (key leaders only), or on a terrain model, sketch map, map, or radio network. Use the most realistic one your time allows.

Worked example — a squad recon patrol

You will be able to
  • Walk through a worked timeline.
  • Apply the rule to a real clock.

This is a sample timeline for illustration. Your times, mission, and details will be different.

📋 The mission: "1st Squad conducts a reconnaissance patrol NLT 0600 at OBJ RAVEN in order to confirm enemy strength prior to the platoon's assault." The squad must leave at 0400 to get there in time. The squad leader gets the platoon order at 1800.

The time plan

From 1800 to 0400 is 10 hours. One third is about 3 hours 20 minutes. The squad leader aims to issue the order by 2100, which uses 3 hours, and leaves 7 hours for the squad.

TimeStepWhat happens
18001. Receive the missionReads the platoon order. Task: recon OBJ RAVEN. Purpose: confirm enemy strength. Works the clock backward from 0400.
18152. Warning orderTells the squad the mission, the OPORD time (2100), rehearsal time, and departure (0400). Team leaders start checking gear.
18303. Tentative planRuns METT-TC. Picks a route and an observation position. Notes the risks and how to react.
19004. Initiate movementSends a team to pull batteries, radios, and ammunition, and to move the squad to the assembly area.
19305. ReconnaissanceDoes a map recon of the route and the approach. Studies the terrain features around the objective.
20306. Complete the planAdjusts the route after the map recon. Prepares a sketch for the brief.
21007. Issue the orderBriefs the five-paragraph OPORD at a sand table. Has team leaders back-brief their tasks.
2130–03308. Supervise and refineRuns a rehearsal, then PCCs and PCIs. Makes updates. Squad rests.
0400ExecuteThe squad departs.
💡What to notice. The warning order goes out 15 minutes after the mission arrives. Movement starts before the plan is final. The order is issued with 7 hours left, so the squad has time to rehearse and rest.

TLP and MDMP — how they compare

You will be able to
  • Say who uses TLP and who uses MDMP.
  • Say where the MDMP steps live inside TLP.

Both help a leader plan. TLP is built for a small unit with no staff. MDMP is built for a commander with a staff.

TLPMDMP
Who uses itSmall-unit leaders, typically company and belowCommanders with a staff, typically battalion and above
Who does the thinkingThe leader, with input from key subordinatesThe commander directs; the staff runs the process
Number of steps87
SpeedFast and flexibleMore detailed and formal
Main outputA rehearsed unit that understands the orderA plan or order for subordinate units

Where the MDMP steps live inside TLP

MDMP stepWhere it appears in TLP
1. Receipt of missionTLP 1 (Receive the mission) and TLP 2 (Warning order)
2. Mission analysisTLP 3, especially METT-TC
3–6. COA development, analysis, comparison, approvalTLP 3, done by the leader in a shorter form
7. Orders productionTLP 6 (Complete the plan) and TLP 7 (Issue the order)
🔗Why this matters. If you understand the MDMP, TLP feels familiar. If you know TLP, you already do a small version of the MDMP whenever you plan.
