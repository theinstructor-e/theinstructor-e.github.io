OAKOC — the five military aspects of terrain

Before you can plan a route, place a weapon, or pick a patrol base, you have to read the ground the same way every time. OAKOC is the checklist that makes sure you don't miss anything.

🏠 Think of it like house-hunting with a tactical eye: where can I see out from (Observation), what's the driveway in (Avenues of Approach), which room matters most (Key Terrain), what's blocking the door (Obstacles), and where would I duck if someone shot at the house (Cover and Concealment)?

Where it fits

OAKOC is the terrain half of the "T" in METT-TC — the mission analysis framework used in both TLP and MDMP. Whenever a leader says "let's talk terrain and weather," this is the tool they reach for.

OObservation and Fields of Fire — what you can see, what you can hitAAvenues of Approach — routes in, for you and the enemyKKey Terrain — ground worth fighting forOObstacles — anything that stops or slows movementCCover and Concealment — protection from fire, protection from being seen

Some older references order it OCOKA (Observation and Fields of Fire, Cover and Concealment, Obstacles, Key Terrain, Avenues of Approach) — same five factors, different order. Either way, analyze all five before you decide anything.

🔁Use it both ways. Every one of these five factors gets evaluated twice — once for what it means for the enemy, once for what it means for you. Terrain that helps you observe an avenue of approach might also expose you to enemy observation from the same spot.

Observation and Fields of Fire, Avenues of Approach

These two go together: observation tells you what you can see, avenues of approach tell you where the fight is likely to come from.

Observation and Fields of Fire

👁️
Observation
Can you see the threat?

The ability to see the enemy, either visually or through surveillance devices (optics, sensors, radar). Anything that offers cover or concealment to the enemy denies or limits your observation of them.

🎯
Fields of Fire
Can you hit what you see?

The area that a weapon, or a group of weapons, can cover effectively with fire from a given position. Terrain that offers cover also tends to limit fields of fire — you can't shoot through what you can't see through.

When you evaluate observation and fields of fire, you're looking for places that give clear sight lines and engagement ranges over key terrain, obstacles, and — most importantly — avenues of approach.

Avenues of Approach

An avenue of approach (AA) is an air or ground route that leads an attacking force of a given size to its objective, or to key terrain in its path. Every avenue of approach gets evaluated twice:

🔵
Friendly AA
Blue arrow

The routes your own element can use to reach the objective or key terrain.

🔴
Enemy AA
Red arrow

The routes the enemy is most likely to use against you or the objective.

🚦Canalizing terrain. Avenues of approach are often shaped by natural lines of drift — valleys, roads, ridgelines, waterways. Terrain that funnels movement into a narrow path is called canalizing terrain, and it's exactly where you'd expect an ambush or a choke-point obstacle.

Key Terrain, Obstacles, Cover and Concealment

These three decide who controls the ground, what slows people down on it, and where you're safe once you're on it.

Key Terrain

Any locality, or area, that — if seized, retained, or controlled — affords a marked advantage to either combatant. Common examples: high ground overlooking an avenue of approach, bridges, road junctions, and towns that sit astride a route. A hilltop with great observation isn't automatically key terrain — it only matters if controlling it actually changes the fight. Decisive terrain is the rarer case where controlling it is essential to mission success.

Obstacles

Any natural or man-made terrain feature that stops, slows, impedes, or diverts movement. Natural obstacles include rivers, steep slopes, and dense vegetation; man-made obstacles include wire, minefields, and craters. Consider obstacles to air mobility as well as ground movement.

Cover and Concealment

🛡️
Cover
Protection from fire

Shelter or protection from the effects of direct and indirect fire — natural (a berm, a ditch) or artificial (a wall, a fighting position).

🌳
Concealment
Protection from observation

Protection from observation or surveillance, including from the air. Woods, underbrush, tall grass, and cultivated vegetation are classic concealment.

⚠️Cover and concealment aren't the same thing. Something that hides you doesn't always stop a bullet (tall grass), and something that stops a bullet doesn't always hide you (a lone boulder). The best positions have both — but don't assume one buys you the other.

Weather — the sixth factor

Terrain and weather get analyzed together, because weather changes how the terrain behaves. Doctrine lists these military aspects of weather alongside OAKOC.

FactorWhy it matters
VisibilityFog, haze, smoke, and darkness change observation ranges and engagement distances for everyone.
WindAffects aircraft, smoke and CBRN hazards, sound carry, and indirect-fire ballistics.
PrecipitationRain and snow affect mobility, visibility, and equipment.
Cloud cover / ceilingDrives whether air support and resupply are even possible.
TemperatureAffects personnel endurance, equipment performance, and casualty risk (heat/cold injury).
HumidityCompounds heat-injury risk and affects some equipment and optics.
Atmospheric pressureConsidered as required — relevant to some aircraft and munitions calculations.

Worked example — a squad chooses a defensive position

This is a sample scenario for illustration. Real terrain analysis always uses the actual ground and map in front of you.

📋 The scenario: a squad must set up a hasty defense overlooking a river crossing site, expecting the enemy to approach from the tree line on the far bank.
FactorWhat the squad leader notes
Observation & Fields of FireThe near-side high ground gives clear sight and engagement range across the entire crossing site.
Avenues of ApproachThe tree line on the far bank is the likely enemy AA; the gravel road behind the squad is the friendly AA for resupply or withdrawal.
Key TerrainThe high ground itself — whoever holds it controls the crossing site.
ObstaclesThe river is a natural obstacle that canalizes the enemy toward the crossing site — a natural choke point to cover with fire.
Cover & ConcealmentA tree line just behind the high ground offers concealment for the squad's positions; a rock outcrop nearby offers cover for the machine gun team.
💡What to notice. The same river that's an obstacle to the enemy is also the reason the high ground is key terrain — the two factors reinforce each other. That's normal: OAKOC factors are rarely independent of one another.
