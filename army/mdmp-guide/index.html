MDMP — how a headquarters plans

You will be able to
  • List the seven MDMP steps in order.
  • Say who does what in the process.

The Military Decision Making Process is the Army's step-by-step method for a commander and staff to understand a mission, build options, pick the best one, and turn it into an order.

🏗️ Like a design-and-build project: you study the problem, sketch several designs, test them, choose one, and then write the blueprints everyone will build from.

The seven steps

Receipt of mission
The staff is alerted, gathers tools, and the commander gives initial guidance.
Mission analysis
Understand the problem: tasks, constraints, facts, assumptions, and risks. End with a restated mission and the commander’s intent.
COA development
Build several workable courses of action.
COA analysis
Wargame each course of action to see how it plays out.
COA comparison
Compare the courses of action against the evaluation criteria.
COA approval
The commander picks one and gives final guidance.
Orders production, dissemination, and transition
Write the order, share it, and hand off to execution.
🎯COA stands for course of action: a broad possible way to accomplish the mission.

Who does what

👤
Commander
The decision maker

The most important participant. The commander frames the problem, gives guidance and intent, and chooses the course of action.

📋
Executive officer
Runs the staff

Coordinates the staff, builds the planning timeline, and keeps the process on track.

👥
Staff sections
The experts

Each section keeps a running estimate for its area and contributes to every step. The intelligence, operations, logistics, and other sections all take part.

📡Where EW fits. Electromagnetic warfare staff add the spectrum picture to planning: what the enemy emits, what friendly units depend on, and how to attack or protect it. Your course instructors will show the exact products.

It bends, not breaks

Staffs usually work the steps in order, but they go back when they learn something new. The process is also meant to be shortened when time is tight. See the Time & Tools tab.

Steps 1–2 — Start and understand the problem

You will be able to
  • Say what happens in receipt of mission.
  • Name the products of mission analysis.

Step 1: Receipt of mission

Planning begins when the commander gets a mission or sees one coming. Quick, focused actions set everything else up.

Alert the staff and key people
Tell them planning is starting, and how much time there is.
Gather tools
Higher headquarters’ orders, maps, running estimates, unit SOPs, and other references.
Make an initial assessment
A first look at the time and resources available and the situation.
Issue initial guidance
The commander gives early direction, including how to use the time.
Publish the planning timeline
The staff sets who does what and when.
Issue a warning order
Tell subordinate units so they can begin their own preparation.

Step 2: Mission analysis

This is the step where the staff figures out what the problem really is. It is the base for every later step.

QuestionWhat the staff produces
What were we told to do?Analysis of the higher headquarters’ order: task, purpose, and intent
What is the situation?Intelligence preparation of the battlefield (the enemy, terrain, and weather)
What must we do?A list of specified, implied, and essential tasks
What limits us?Constraints: what we must do and must not do
What do we know and assume?Critical facts and assumptions
What could go wrong?A first risk assessment
What does the commander need to know?Commander’s critical information requirements (CCIR) and essential elements of friendly information (EEFI)
What is our mission?A restated mission statement (who, what, when, where, why)
SpecifiedA task the higher order tells you to do.ImpliedA task you must do to accomplish a specified task, though nobody wrote it down.EssentialA task you must do to accomplish the mission. Essential tasks go into the mission statement.

Step 2 ends with a mission analysis briefing to the commander. The commander approves the restated mission and gives the commander’s intent and planning guidance. The staff also sets the evaluation criteria it will use to compare courses of action later, and a second warning order goes out.

Steps 3–4 — Build options and test them

You will be able to
  • Apply the five screening tests to a COA.
  • Name the three wargaming methods.

Step 3: COA development

The staff builds several possible courses of action. A COA is a broad possible solution, described well enough to be tested. Typical work includes weighing relative combat power, generating options, arranging forces, developing a broad concept, and writing a COA statement and sketch.

Each COA is screened against five tests:

SSuitable: it accomplishes the mission and follows the commander’s guidance.FFeasible: it can be done with the time, space, and resources available.AAcceptable: the advantage is worth the risk and cost.DDistinguishable: it is meaningfully different from the other COAs.CComplete: it covers the operation from start to finish.

Step 4: COA analysis (wargaming)

Wargaming plays each COA against the likely enemy actions to find out what works. It goes in a rhythm of action, reaction, counteraction: we do something, the enemy responds, we respond to that.

MethodHow it works
BeltDivides the battlefield into areas (belts) across its width and plays each in turn
Avenue-in-depthFollows one avenue of approach from start to finish, then the next
BoxFocuses in detail on a specific area, such as the objective

Wargaming turns up weak points, decision points, and sequencing problems. The staff refines each COA and updates its plans and estimates.

🎲Tip. A good wargame needs someone to play the enemy honestly. If the "enemy" only does what is convenient for us, the results are worthless.

Steps 5–7 — Choose and write the plan

You will be able to
  • Say what a decision matrix does and its limits.
  • Say what the commander does at COA approval.

Step 5: COA comparison

The staff compares the COAs against the evaluation criteria set in mission analysis. A decision matrix is a common tool: score each COA on each criterion, weight the criteria, and total the scores. The staff then recommends a COA.

⚠️A matrix supports judgment. It does not replace it. Scores and weights are opinions. Look at whether the answer changes if a weight changes, and ask why.

Step 6: COA approval

The staff briefs the commander on the recommended COA. The commander picks one, changes it, or asks for more work. Then the commander gives final planning guidance, refines the intent, and updates the commander's critical information requirements. Another warning order goes to subordinates.

Step 7: Orders production, dissemination, and transition

The staff turns the approved COA into an operation order or plan, the commander approves it, and it goes out to the units. Rehearsals help everyone understand it. The plan then transitions from the planners to the people who run current operations.

📄Orders follow a standard format. The five-paragraph OPORD (Situation, Mission, Execution, Sustainment, Command and Signal) is the same one you use for small-unit orders. See the OPORD Builder.

Products and terms

You will be able to
  • Define the key products and terms.
  • Tell the CCIR from the EEFI.

These are the words and documents you will hear most in planning.

TermWhat it means
Warning order (WARNORD)An early notice of an upcoming operation, so units can start preparing. Sent at several points in the MDMP.
Running estimateA staff section’s continuous assessment of its area. It is updated throughout planning and execution.
Commander’s intentA clear, short statement of the purpose of the operation and the desired end state. Subordinates use it when the plan no longer fits.
Planning guidanceThe commander’s direction to the staff about how to plan, given in mission analysis and again at COA approval.
Restated missionThe mission in who, what, when, where, and why form, developed in mission analysis.
Essential taskA task that must be done to accomplish the mission.
CCIRCommander’s critical information requirements: information the commander needs to make timely decisions. It includes priority intelligence requirements (PIR) and friendly force information requirements (FFIR).
EEFIEssential elements of friendly information: things the enemy must not learn about our operations.
Evaluation criteriaThe standards used to compare COAs, chosen during mission analysis.
Decision pointA time or place where the commander expects to make a key decision.
WargameA step-by-step test of a COA against the enemy’s likely actions.
OPORD / OPLANThe order or plan that results from the MDMP.
FRAGORDA fragmentary order: a quick change to an existing order.

Time and tools

You will be able to
  • Apply the one-third, two-thirds rule.
  • Name ways to shorten the process and the steps of RDSP.

The full MDMP takes time. Real headquarters often have less, so they need to shorten it without losing the point.

The one-third, two-thirds rule

A commander should use no more than about one third of the available time for the headquarters' own planning and leave at least two thirds for subordinates. Late plans starve the units that have to execute them.

🧮Example. Available time: 24 hours. Headquarters planning: no more than 8 hours. Subordinate units: at least 16 hours.

When time is short

Commanders can direct the staff to abbreviate the MDMP. Common methods include:

More guidanceThe commander gives clearer, more detailed guidance early, so the staff wastes less time.Fewer COAsDevelop one COA, or even just refine the commander’s own concept.Parallel planningSubordinates start planning as soon as warning orders arrive instead of waiting.Fewer briefsCombine or skip some briefings.
📘Learn the full version first. Doctrine says a staff should master the full MDMP before trying to abbreviate it. You cannot safely skip what you do not understand.

Changing the plan during execution

When the situation changes after the order is out, commanders and staffs often use the Rapid Decision-making and Synchronization Process (RDSP):

Compare the current situation to the order
Is what is happening what we expected?
Determine whether a decision is needed, and what type
Some changes need a new decision; others do not.
Develop a course of action
Build a workable option quickly.
Refine and validate it
Check it against the mission and intent.
Issue and implement the order
Get the change to the units.
