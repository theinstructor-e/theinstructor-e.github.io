Battle drills — standardized action, not a debate

You will be able to
  • Say what a battle drill is and why there is no debate.
  • Say what a cue is and name the one judgment call most drills turn on.

A battle drill is a standardized collective action a unit takes in response to a common battle occurrence. The whole point is speed: no meeting, no debate — everyone already knows their job the instant the cue happens.

🚨 Think of it like a fire drill: nobody stops to plan when the alarm sounds. Everyone already knows the route, so the whole building empties in the time it takes to think about it once.

Fourteen essential drills

ATP 3-21.8 lists battle drills every Infantry platoon and squad trains on. This guide focuses on the ones a Soldier is most likely to need on short notice: React to Contact, Break Contact, React to an Ambush (near and far), and React to Indirect Fire. Others — Knock Out a Bunker, Enter and Clear a Room, breach a wire obstacle, and more — build on the same fire-and-movement principles.

Every drill starts with a cue

A drill has a specific trigger, called a cue. React to Contact begins the instant the enemy initiates direct fire. React to an Ambush begins the instant the enemy initiates the ambush. There's no "if" about whether to execute — the cue itself is the order.

🔑The one judgment call that matters most. Almost every drill on this page branches on one question: is the enemy inside hand-grenade range or not? Get that distance call right, and the rest of the drill follows almost automatically.

React to Contact and Break Contact

You will be able to
  • Give the steps of React to Contact.
  • Say what Break Contact is and name the peel technique.

React to Contact (Battle Drill 1) is the base drill nearly every other drill builds on. Break Contact is what you do when the fight isn't one you want to keep having.

React to Contact

Cue: the enemy initiates direct fire (this drill also covers visual contact and IED contact).

Return fire and seek cover
The element in contact returns fire immediately and gets into covered positions.
Locate the enemy
Identify the enemy's position(s) so fire can be aimed, not just outgoing.
Suppress
Place well-aimed suppressive fire to gain fire superiority.
Leader decides the next action
Assault, support by fire for another element, or break contact — based on the situation.
Report
The leader reports the contact to higher headquarters.

Break Contact

Cue: the leader gives the order to break contact — usually because continuing the fight isn't worth it (outmatched, off the objective, or the mission doesn't call for decisive engagement here).

The unit returns fire, then moves away from the enemy by a covered and concealed route, using a movement technique like bounding overwatch so part of the element is always providing fire while the rest moves. One common technique for a small element is the peel: the rear-most Soldier fires, then moves back past the others to the new rear position, and the process repeats down the line.

React to an Ambush — near versus far

You will be able to
  • Tell a near ambush from a far ambush and give the response to each.
  • Say why the distance call matters.

An ambush is a surprise attack by fire from a concealed position on a moving or halted target. The single most important call a Soldier makes is distance: is the enemy within hand-grenade range?

💥
Near Ambush
Within hand-grenade range (~35m or closer)

Staying in the kill zone means staying in the enemy's most effective fire. Assault through the kill zone — it is the only way out. Soldiers in the kill zone return fire immediately and assault; Soldiers not in the kill zone place suppressive fire on the enemy.

🎯
Far Ambush
Beyond hand-grenade range

Too far to close quickly. Soldiers in the kill zone return fire and seek cover, then suppress the enemy. The element then breaks contact or maneuvers, with support, rather than assaulting across open distance.

⚠️Why this distinction matters so much. Assaulting a far ambush means crossing open ground under fire for no reason — the enemy planned for that. Staying put in a near ambush means dying in the kill zone the enemy built to kill you in. The "right" answer flips completely depending on which one it is.

The sequence for a near ambush

Return fire immediately
On known or suspected enemy positions — no order needed, this is automatic on contact.
Assault through the kill zone
Soldiers not already moving link up and continue the assault; smoke can help mask movement.
Consolidate and reorganize
Account for personnel and equipment on the far side.
Report
The leader reports the situation to higher headquarters.

React to Indirect Fire

You will be able to
  • Give the steps of React to Indirect Fire.
  • Say why moving matters more than cover against indirect fire.

Indirect fire — mortars or artillery — can't be suppressed by returning fire the way direct fire can. The only good response is to get out of the impact area fast.

Announce "incoming"
Alert everyone the instant rounds are detected.
Move out of the impact area
Rapidly move in the direction specified by unit SOP — do not simply hit the ground and stay put.
Leader accounts for personnel
Checks for casualties and equipment once clear of the impact area.
Report and continue the mission
Or reorganize if casualties or damage require it.
🏃Why moving matters more than cover here. Unlike direct fire, indirect fire isn't aimed at a visible target in real time — staying in one spot doesn't protect you the way cover does against a rifleman. Distance from the original impact point is what saves you.

CallWhy
Ambush, not just contactSudden fire from a concealed position on a moving target fits the definition of an ambush, not a chance meeting engagement.
