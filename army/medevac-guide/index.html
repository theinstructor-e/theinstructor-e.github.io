9-Line MEDEVAC & TCCC
Treat first, then call it in right.

The MARCH sequence that keeps a casualty alive, and the nine-line format that gets help to them.

MARCH — treat in this order, every time

You will be able to
  • Give the MARCH steps in order and say why bleeding comes first.
  • Say what each letter covers, including the extended MARCH-PAWS.

MARCH is the Tactical Combat Casualty Care sequence for what to fix first. It exists because the things that kill fastest aren't always the things that look the worst.

🩸 Why bleeding comes before airway: massive hemorrhage kills faster than almost anything else on a battlefield — faster than most airway problems. MARCH flips the civilian "ABC" order on purpose.
M — Massive Hemorrhage
Stop life-threatening bleeding first — tourniquet or pressure dressing before anything else.
A — Airway
Open and protect the airway. A conscious, speaking casualty already has a patent airway.
R — Respiration
Check for and seal a chest wound; watch for tension pneumothorax.
C — Circulation
Reassess bleeding control, watch for shock, support circulation.
H — Hypothermia / Head Injury
Keep the casualty warm; hypothermia worsens bleeding and secondary brain injury.
📋MARCH-PAWS. Some units extend the sequence for prolonged field care: Pain management, Antibiotics, Wounds (dressing non-life-threatening injuries), and Splints — always after the core MARCH steps.

The nine lines

You will be able to
  • Say what each of the nine lines asks for.
  • Read a precedence line and the time it sets.

Once the casualty is stable enough to move, the 9-line MEDEVAC request gets an aircraft moving. Most of the information for it comes straight off the TCCC card.

LineWhat it saysCodes
1Location of pickup siteGrid coordinates (MGRS)
2Radio frequency and callsign—
3Patients by precedenceA-Urgent, B-Urgent Surgical, C-Priority, D-Routine, E-Convenience
4Special equipment requiredA-None, B-Hoist, C-Extraction equipment, D-Ventilator
5Patients by typeL-Litter, A-Ambulatory
6Security at pickup site (wartime)N-No enemy, P-Possible enemy, E-Enemy in area, X-Armed escort required
7Method of marking pickup siteA-Panels, B-Pyrotechnic, C-Smoke, D-None, E-Other
8Patient nationality and statusA-US Military, B-US Civilian, C-Non-US Military, D-Non-US Civilian, E-EPW
9NBC contamination (wartime)N-Nuclear, B-Biological, C-Chemical
🔀Lines 6 and 9 change in peacetime. Line 6 becomes the number and type of wounds, injuries, or illnesses; Line 9 becomes a terrain description of the pickup site.
⏱️Precedence sets the clock. Urgent and Urgent Surgical both mean evacuation within 2 hours; Priority means within 4; Routine means within 24.

Sending the request

You will be able to
  • Say which lines go first and on which frequency.
  • Say when the MIST report is sent.

The 9-line isn't just information — it's a specific transmission format, split across two radio frequencies.

1️⃣
Lines 1–5 first
MEDEVAC frequency

Sent immediately — this is what the aircraft needs to launch. Target: within 25 seconds of initial contact.

2️⃣
Lines 6–9 after
Operational frequency

Sent once you've switched frequencies — can go out while the aircraft is already inbound.

Say "BREAK" between categories within a line (for example, between the urgent count and the priority count on Line 3), and "OVER" at the end of each transmission. In training, 100% accuracy is the standard — a wrong brevity code is treated as a failed attempt.

🗣️Example, Line 3: "Line 3: Two Alpha, BREAK, one Charlie, over" means two Urgent patients and one Priority patient.
📝MIST comes after, not instead of. The MIST report (Mechanism, Injuries, Signs/symptoms, Treatment given) is sent after the 9-line, not in place of it — never delay the MEDEVAC request waiting to gather MIST details.
▶ Practice it: the 9-Line MEDEVAC Simulator puts you through real casualty scenarios — you make the MARCH call, then build the actual report yourself.
