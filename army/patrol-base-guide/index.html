Patrol base — a security perimeter, not a campsite

You will be able to
  • Say what a patrol base is and its two fixed rules.
  • Say how it is entered.

A patrol base is a security perimeter a squad or platoon sets up when it halts for an extended period during a patrol. It is not a place to relax — it's a temporary defensive position built the same way every time, so the unit stays hidden and ready.

🏕️ Think of it like a rest stop you build yourself: you don't just pull over — you pick a spot nobody would look for you, cover your tracks getting there, and post watch before anyone closes their eyes.

The two rules that never change

⏱️
24-hour limit
Except in an emergency

A patrol base should not be occupied for more than 24 hours. The longer you sit still, the more likely you are to be found.

🚫
Never reuse one
No exceptions

A patrol never uses the same patrol base twice. If the enemy finds an old one, they'll watch it — or worse, prep it as an ambush.

What a patrol base is used for

Avoiding detection by eliminating movement, hiding during a long or detailed reconnaissance, performing maintenance on weapons and equipment, eating and resting, planning and issuing orders, reorganizing after infiltrating enemy territory, or serving as a base for several consecutive or concurrent operations.

🔗Same family as the ORP. A patrol base is reconnoitered and occupied the same way as an Objective Rally Point — with one difference: the patrol typically plans to enter at a 90-degree turn off its original line of march, to throw off anyone trying to backtrack the unit's trail.

Site selection and occupation

You will be able to
  • Pick a good site and name the perimeter shapes.
  • Say what the leader does before the unit occupies.

Where you put a patrol base matters as much as how you defend it. The site should work against the enemy before a single fighting position is dug.

What makes a good site

Terrain the enemy would consider of little value
Nobody defends — or looks for you in — ground they don't care about.
Away from main lines of drift
Avoid trails, roads, and other paths people naturally walk.
Away from built-up areas and known enemy positions
Terrain that offers cover and concealment
Same OAKOC thinking used for any other position.

Perimeter shapes

🥬
Squad-sized patrol
Cigar-shaped perimeter

A smaller element typically forms an elongated, cigar-shaped security perimeter.

🔺
Platoon-sized patrol
Triangle-shaped perimeter

A platoon typically forms a triangle, giving each squad a side to secure.

The leader conducts a leader's reconnaissance before the unit occupies the site, then brings the rest of the element in — entering with that 90-degree turn — and inspects and adjusts the perimeter as needed once everyone is in place.

Priorities of work

You will be able to
  • Say what comes first in priorities of work.
  • Name the common priorities of work.

Priorities of work are not just a checklist. Doctrine is specific: each one is a task, a given time, and a measurable performance standard — set according to METT-TC, not run the same way on autopilot every time.

Security comes first — always

The absolute first priority upon occupying a patrol base is establishing security. That typically means posting a listening post / observation post (LP/OP) forward of the perimeter along likely avenues of approach, so the unit has early warning before it commits to anything else — cooking, cleaning weapons, or resting.

Common priorities of work

🛡️
Continuous security

Passive and active security measures cover the perimeter at all times — this never stops for the other priorities.

🚪
Withdrawal plan

The leader designates the signal for withdrawal, the order units withdraw in, and the rendezvous point or alternate patrol base.

📻
Communications

Radio watch and maintenance continue throughout the halt.

📋
Mission preparation and planning

The patrol base is often where the leader plans and issues orders for what comes next.

🕳️Fighting positions. When time allows, hasty fighting positions are prepared at least 18 inches deep at the front, sloping gently front to rear, with a grenade sump if possible.
🧼Personal tasks happen with a buddy on watch. Hygiene, eating, and equipment maintenance are done individually while a Soldier's buddy maintains security — the perimeter is never fully unmanned for these tasks.

Worked example — a squad occupies a patrol base

You will be able to
  • Follow the worked example step by step.
  • Say what happens before first light.

This is a sample scenario for illustration. Real occupation always follows unit SOP and the leader's actual reconnaissance.

📋 The scenario: a squad needs to halt for the night after a long movement, with a resupply and follow-on mission planned for the next day.
StepWhat the squad leader does
Site selectionPicks a tentative site off the map — thick vegetation well away from the trail the squad has been following, terrain the enemy has no reason to care about.
ApproachLeads the squad past the site, then executes a 90-degree turn back into it to break the trail.
OccupationLeader's recon confirms the site; squad forms a cigar-shaped perimeter and posts an LP/OP forward before anyone does anything else.
Priorities of workContinuous security first, then the withdrawal plan is briefed, then radio watch is set, then the squad eats and performs weapons maintenance in shifts with buddies on watch.
Before first light100% security during stand-to, then the squad breaks down the site completely before departing — leaving nothing behind that shows they were ever there.
💡What to notice. Security is established before anything else happens, and it never actually stops — every other task is layered on top of it, not instead of it.
