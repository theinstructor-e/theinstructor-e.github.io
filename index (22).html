Simple Tech

How does Cellular work?
The basics, made simple.

From 1G to 5G, every cellular network does the same three jobs: get your phone talking to a tower, know exactly who and where you are, and hand your call off as you move. Here's how.

1G to 5G — what actually changed each time

Each cellular "generation" wasn't just a speed bump — each one changed what the network was fundamentally built to carry.

📻 Think of it as an evolving delivery service: 1G could only carry your voice, like an analog walkie-talkie. Every generation since has added a new kind of cargo — first digital voice, then data, then all-IP data, and now ultra-fast, ultra-low-latency data for things far beyond phones.

📡Voice used to be special. In 2G and 3G, voice calls ran on dedicated circuit-switched channels, separate from data. By 4G, voice itself became just another IP data stream (VoLTE) — the network stopped treating voice as a unique case.
⚡5G's real headline isn't just speed. Its biggest structural change is ultra-low latency and the ability to serve enormous numbers of connected devices per cell — the design target that makes things like large-scale IoT deployments realistic.

Two halves: the Radio side and the Core

Every cellular generation splits into the same two halves: a Radio Access Network (RAN) that talks to your phone over the air, and a Core Network that handles identity, routing, and connecting out to other networks — including the internet.

🏢 Like a hotel: the RAN is the front desk and hallways — it's what you actually interact with to get to your room. The Core is everything behind the scenes — reservations, billing, security — that makes the whole hotel actually function, even though guests never see it directly.

How the network knows who and where you are

Cellular networks run on a stack of IDs — some identify the network itself, some identify your device, and some identify the specific cell tower you're talking to.

🪪 Like a layered ID system: your IMSI is like your permanent national ID number — it never changes and identifies you specifically. Your TMSI is a temporary badge the network hands you for a session, so your permanent ID isn't broadcast over the air constantly. The PLMN is which country/carrier's "building" you're in, and the Cell ID is which specific room.

Network identifiers

🌍
PLMN
Public Land Mobile Network

Identifies a specific carrier in a specific country. It's built from two smaller codes: the MCC (Mobile Country Code — which country) and the MNC (Mobile Network Code — which carrier within that country).

📍
TAC / LAC
Tracking Area Code / Location Area Code

Groups of cells that the network manages together for tracking your rough location — so it doesn't have to page every single tower to find you, just the ones in your current tracking/location area.

Device identifiers

🪪
IMSI
International Mobile Subscriber Identity

A permanent number tied to your SIM card, uniquely identifying you as a subscriber worldwide. It rarely changes and is sensitive — networks try to avoid broadcasting it directly over the air whenever possible.

🏷️
TMSI
Temporary Mobile Subscriber Identity

A temporary, reassignable ID the network gives your device instead of using your IMSI on every over-the-air exchange — a privacy measure so your permanent identity isn't constantly exposed.

🏗️Cell-level identifiers scale with the network. Things like the Cell Global Identity (CGI) in older networks and the Physical Cell Identity (PCI) or NR Cell Identity (NCI) in newer ones all serve the same basic purpose: uniquely naming a specific cell/tower/sector so the network and your phone both know exactly which piece of radio coverage you're using.

Logical channels — the radio's "conversation types"

Even though it's all radio waves, a cellular connection isn't one single conversation — it's split into several logical channels, each carrying a different kind of information at a different time.

📻 Think of separate radio stations sharing one tower: one "station" just broadcasts network info to everyone listening. Another handles the brief back-and-forth of a phone trying to connect. A third carries your actual call or data once you're connected. They're logically separate even though they may share the same physical radio.

Three broad families

📢
Broadcast Channels
one-way, to everyone in range

Carry system information every device in range needs — network identity, timing, configuration — before a device even tries to connect. Example: BCCH (Broadcast Control Channel).

🤝
Control Channels
setting up and managing a connection

Handle the handshake of getting a device connected and keeping the link managed: paging a device, letting it request access, and carrying signaling during a call. Examples: PCH (paging), RACH (random access — a device's first "hello"), SDCCH (stand-alone dedicated control), SACCH/FACCH (associated control, running alongside a call).

🗣️
Traffic Channels
your actual call or data

Carry the payload — your actual voice or data — once a connection is established. Example: TCH (Traffic Channel).

The basic sequence

A device generally: (1) listens to the broadcast channel to learn about the network, (2) uses a random access channel to say "I'd like to connect," (3) gets assigned a dedicated control channel to complete setup, then (4) moves to a traffic channel for the actual call or data session.

🔄The names change across generations, the concept doesn't. GSM's BCCH/PCH/RACH/SDCCH/TCH model has direct conceptual cousins in LTE (PRACH for random access, RRC signaling for control) and 5G NR (SSB for broadcast/synchronization, PDSCH for the actual data) — the channel types persist even as the specific channel names evolve.
