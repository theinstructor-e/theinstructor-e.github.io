AC, Transformers & Machines Explained

Alternating current waveforms, transformers, generators, and motors — the fundamentals behind how power actually gets made, changed, and turned into motion.

The Sine Wave

Picture a bicycle pedal going around in a circle at a steady speed, and someone recording only its height above the ground over time. That height traces out exactly the shape of an AC sine wave — up to a peak, back through zero, down to a trough, and back to zero again. One full trip around is one cycle.

One cycle: zero → peak → zero → trough → zero

Frequency & Period

Frequency is how many complete cycles happen every second, measured in Hertz (Hz). U.S. household power runs at 60 Hz — 60 full cycles every second. Period is the flip side: how long one single cycle takes. They're mathematical opposites of each other.

Period (seconds) = 1 ÷ Frequency (Hz)

So 60 Hz power completes one cycle every 1/60th of a second — about 16.7 milliseconds.

Peak, Peak-to-Peak & RMS

A sine wave doesn't sit at one voltage — it's constantly changing, which raises an obvious question: what number do you even call "the voltage" of an AC source?

TermWhat it means
PeakThe single highest point the wave reaches above zero
Peak-to-PeakThe full swing, from the top peak down to the bottom trough
RMS (Root Mean Square)The "effective" value — the DC-equivalent that would deliver the same real power
Why RMS Exists
A wave that's at its peak only for an instant obviously doesn't deliver the same continuous power as a steady DC source at that same peak value. RMS is the honest answer to "what's it really equivalent to" — it's why standard 120V household power actually peaks at about 170V, but nobody calls it a 170V outlet.
RMS ≈ Peak × 0.707

Phase

Phase describes the timing relationship between two waveforms of the same frequency — whether they rise and fall together, or one is shifted ahead of or behind the other, measured in degrees (a full cycle = 360°). Two waves that are perfectly aligned are in phase; if one is shifted by 180° it's exactly upside-down relative to the other, called out of phase.

This is the general wave concept. For how phase actually shifts between voltage and current inside a real circuit with capacitors and inductors — the "ELI the ICE man" relationship — see AC Circuit Theory & Impedance.

How It Works

A transformer is two coils of wire wound around a shared iron core, with no direct electrical connection between them. AC current in the first coil (the primary) creates a constantly changing magnetic field. That changing field induces a voltage in the second coil (the secondary) purely through that shared magnetism — a principle called mutual induction. This is exactly why transformers only work on AC: DC current creates a magnetic field that stops changing once it's established, so nothing new gets induced in the secondary.

Step-down transformer — more turns on the primary than the secondary

Turns Ratio

The ratio of primary turns to secondary turns sets exactly how much the voltage changes — and current changes by the exact opposite ratio, since a transformer can't create or destroy power out of nowhere.

Vprimary / Vsecondary = Nprimary / Nsecondary

Fewer turns on the secondary than the primary makes a step-down transformer (lower voltage out); more turns on the secondary makes a step-up transformer (higher voltage out). Either way, whatever voltage is gained, current is lost in the same proportion — and vice versa.

The Lever Analogy
Think of a see-saw: push down hard with a small motion on the long end, and the short end goes up with a small force but a huge motion. A transformer trades voltage and current against each other the exact same way — you can't get more of both out than you put in.

Real transformers aren't perfectly ideal

The turns-ratio math above assumes a perfect transformer — no losses at all. Real transformers lose a small amount of energy as heat, from two main sources: core losses (the iron core itself heats up slightly from the constantly-reversing magnetic field) and copper losses (ordinary resistive heating in the winding wire itself, following P=I²R from the electricity fundamentals). Well-designed transformers are still highly efficient — typically well above 90% — but "100% efficient" is always the simplified teaching model, not the literal real-world result.

Electromagnetic Induction

This principle — first identified in the 1830s — is the foundation of essentially all commercial electrical power generation. Move a conductor through a magnetic field (or move the field past a stationary conductor), and a voltage is induced in that conductor. No physical contact between them is required — only relative motion and a magnetic field.

The Rotating-Coil Generator

Spin a loop of wire inside a magnetic field, and as it rotates, the angle between the wire and the field lines constantly changes — which changes how much voltage gets induced at each instant. The result is naturally a sine wave: maximum voltage when the coil cuts straight across the field lines, zero voltage when it's momentarily aligned with them.

A coil spinning between magnetic poles — this is the physical source of AC

This is why utility-scale generation is fundamentally AC: it's simply the natural output of a spinning coil in a magnetic field. Getting DC out of a rotating generator requires additional hardware (a commutator, or a separate rectifier) to convert that naturally-occurring AC afterward.

What controls how much voltage gets induced

Three things directly increase the voltage a generator produces: a stronger magnetic field, more turns of wire in the coil, and faster rotation. This is exactly why a generator's output voltage sags under heavy mechanical load (the rotation slows slightly) and why generator design is a constant trade-off between magnet strength, coil size, and how fast the prime mover can realistically spin the shaft.

What Actually Spins the Coil

The generator itself doesn't care what turns the shaft — a steam turbine, a diesel engine, falling water, or a gas turbine are all just different ways of supplying the mechanical rotation electromagnetic induction needs. The generator converts mechanical motion into electrical power; whatever spins it is a separate engineering problem.

The Basic Principle

Run current through a wire sitting inside a magnetic field, and that wire experiences a physical force pushing it — this is the same electromagnetic relationship as a generator, just used in the opposite direction. Arrange the wire as a loop able to rotate, and that force becomes continuous rotation: a motor.

Same Machine, Opposite Job
Spin a generator's shaft and you get electricity out. Feed electricity into that exact same kind of machine and its shaft spins instead. Many real devices — like the alternator/starter systems in some vehicles — are built to do literally both jobs.

Back-EMF: the motor generating against itself

Here's where the "same machine, opposite job" idea becomes measurable: a spinning motor is also a generator the whole time it's running, since its coil is moving through a magnetic field exactly like the Generators tab describes. That self-generated voltage — called back-EMF — opposes the voltage being fed into the motor. A motor at rest has no back-EMF and draws its highest current (this is exactly why motors draw a large inrush current at startup); as it spins faster, back-EMF rises and opposes more of the supply voltage, which is why running current settles much lower than starting current.

Torque

Torque is the twisting/rotational force a motor produces — the thing that actually gets a load moving and keeps it moving against resistance. It depends on both the strength of the magnetic field and the amount of current flowing through the coil; more of either generally means more torque, up to the physical limits of the motor's design.

Stalled = maximum torque, maximum risk

Connect the back-EMF idea from earlier: if a motor's shaft is physically prevented from turning — jammed, overloaded, or stalled — back-EMF can never develop, since back-EMF only exists while the coil is actually moving through the field. With nothing opposing the supply voltage, current (and therefore torque) hits its absolute maximum. That's exactly why a stalled motor is a real hazard: it's producing its highest possible torque and drawing its highest possible current at the same moment, which is exactly the condition motor overload protection is designed to catch before windings overheat and burn out.

Reactance

Reactance is opposition to AC from an inductor or a capacitor. Unlike resistance, it changes with frequency, and it does not turn power into heat. It is measured in ohms.

XL = 2π × f × L     XC = 1 ÷ (2π × f × C)

An inductor resists change in current, so its reactance rises with frequency. A capacitor passes fast changes more easily, so its reactance falls with frequency. At 60 Hz, a 1 H inductor has XL of about 377 Ω, and a 100 µF capacitor has XC of about 26.5 Ω.

🚽 Inductor and capacitor, side by side: an inductor is like a heavy flywheel in the pipe: it fights any sudden change, so fast changes get through poorly. A capacitor is like a rubber diaphragm in the pipe: fast back-and-forth motion passes, slow steady push does not.

Impedance

Impedance (Z) is the total opposition: resistance and reactance together. They are 90 degrees apart, so they add like the sides of a right triangle, not as plain numbers.

Z = √( R² + (XL − XC)² )

With R = 30 Ω and a net reactance of 40 Ω, Z = √(900 + 1,600) = 50 Ω. Remember the voltage and current timing from the ELI-the-ICE-man rule: in an inductor (L) voltage leads current, in a capacitor (C) current leads voltage.

Resonance

Resonance happens at the one frequency where XL equals XC. They cancel, so the circuit's impedance drops to just R in a series circuit. This is how a radio picks one station out of thousands: tune L or C until the resonant frequency matches the station.

f0 = 1 ÷ (2π × √(L × C))

A 10 µH coil with a 100 pF capacitor resonates at about 5.03 MHz. The narrower the circuit’s response around f0, the higher its Q, roughly f0 divided by the bandwidth.
