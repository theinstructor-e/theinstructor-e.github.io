Modulation & Noise Explained

How a message rides a radio wave, how much room it needs, and what noise and jamming do to it. Seven short chapters, hands-on widgets, and questions after every chapter.

1 · Why We Modulate

You will be able to
  • Explain why a message cannot just be sent straight from an antenna.
  • Name the three things about a carrier that can carry information.
  • Tell analog from digital modulation.

Your voice is a slow vibration, mostly between about 300 Hz and 3.4 kHz. If you wired a microphone to an antenna and hoped for the best, two things would go wrong.

Problem 1: the antenna would be huge

An antenna works well when it is a sizeable fraction of the wavelength. Wavelength is the speed of light divided by frequency.

wavelength (m) = 300,000,000 ÷ frequency (Hz)

At 3 kHz the wavelength is 100 km. A quarter-wave antenna would be 25 km tall. At 150 MHz the wavelength is 2 m, and a quarter-wave antenna is a 50 cm whip that fits on a radio.

Problem 2: everyone would talk on top of everyone

Every voice lives in the same few kilohertz. Send them all out directly and they pile up into noise. If each transmitter moves its signal to its own place on the dial, many signals can share the air without colliding.

The fix: a carrier

A carrier is a steady, high-frequency wave that radiates well from a small antenna. To send a message, you change something about the carrier in step with the message. That is modulation. The receiver undoes it, which is demodulation.

Three knobs

Change the carrier's…Analog nameDigital name
Amplitude (height)AMASK, OOK
FrequencyFMFSK
Phase (timing)PMPSK
Amplitude and phase together—QAM

Analog means the message is a smoothly varying signal, such as a voice waveform. Digital means the message is a stream of separate symbols, usually bits. Most modern links are digital, even when they carry voice.

2 · Analog: AM and FM

You will be able to
  • Describe AM and FM and where their energy sits in the spectrum.
  • Calculate AM bandwidth and FM bandwidth (Carson's rule).
  • Explain the capture effect and the FM threshold.

AM: change the height

In amplitude modulation the carrier's height follows the message. The outline of the waveform is called the envelope, and it copies the message. The modulation depth m says how strongly: m = 1 means the envelope swings from zero to double the carrier height.

Modulating a carrier at fc with a tone at fm creates new frequencies, called sidebands, at fc − fm and fc + fm. So the signal needs room on both sides of the carrier.

AM bandwidth = 2 × highest message frequency

A catch: the message lives only in the sidebands. At m = 1, two thirds of the transmitted power is the plain carrier, which carries nothing. AM is simple but wasteful. Single sideband (SSB) drops the carrier and one sideband, so it needs about half the bandwidth and wastes nothing. That is why HF voice uses SSB.

Push m above 1 and the envelope tries to go negative. That is overmodulation: the waveform distorts and splatters across extra bandwidth.

Try it: AM depth and sidebands
Drag depth past 1.0 and watch the envelope lines cross. The bars below the waveform show the carrier and the two sidebands.

FM: change the speed

In frequency modulation the carrier's height never changes. Its frequency swings up and down in step with the message. How far it swings is the deviation Δf. The modulation index compares it to the message tone:

β = Δf ÷ fm
FM bandwidth ≈ 2 × (Δf + fm) (Carson's rule)

FM makes a whole comb of sidebands spaced fm apart. Carson's rule tells you where nearly all the energy sits. FM broadcast uses Δf = 75 kHz and audio up to 15 kHz, so its bandwidth is about 180 kHz, which fits the 200 kHz channel spacing. Narrowband FM voice radios on 25 kHz channels typically use about 5 kHz deviation with 3 kHz audio, about 16 kHz, which fits the channel.

Why accept the extra bandwidth? Noise mostly changes a signal's height, not its frequency, and an FM receiver ignores height. A wider swing also spreads the message over more bandwidth, which improves the output signal-to-noise ratio. It is a direct trade of bandwidth for quality.

Try it: FM deviation and Carson's rule
Bars are the sideband strengths (Bessel functions). The shaded band is Carson's rule. Try β ≈ 2.4: the carrier itself disappears while the sidebands carry everything.

Two effects worth knowing

  • Capture effect. If two FM signals land on one channel, the receiver locks onto the stronger one and the weaker one disappears, even if it is only a few dB weaker. AM lets you hear both.
  • Threshold effect. FM stays clean as the signal gets weaker, until about 10 dB signal-to-noise. Below that, clicks flood in and quality collapses fast. AM fades gradually instead.

3 · Digital: Bits on a Carrier

You will be able to
  • Tell bit rate from symbol rate and convert between them.
  • Describe ASK/OOK, FSK, PSK and QAM and read a constellation.
  • Explain why packing in more points costs noise tolerance.

Digital modulation sends bits by choosing among a fixed set of carrier states. Each state is a symbol. If there are M possible symbols, each one carries log2M bits.

bit rate = symbol rate × log2(M)

The symbol rate (symbols per second, also called baud) sets the bandwidth. The number of bits per symbol sets how fast you actually send data.

The basic families

  • ASK / OOK. Amplitude shift keying changes the height. On-off keying is the extreme case: carrier on means 1, carrier off means 0. Morse code and many garage remotes work this way.
  • FSK. Frequency shift keying jumps between two (or more) frequencies. Simple and rugged. DMR radios use four-level FSK.
  • PSK. Phase shift keying changes the timing of the wave. Binary PSK (BPSK) flips between 0° and 180°. The GPS civil signal uses BPSK. Quadrature PSK (QPSK) uses four phases, so each symbol carries 2 bits.
  • QAM. Quadrature amplitude modulation changes amplitude and phase together. 16-QAM carries 4 bits per symbol, 64-QAM carries 6. Wi-Fi and LTE step up and down through these as signal conditions change.
Try it: bits to waveform
Watch where each bit starts. For ASK the height changes, for FSK the speed changes, and for BPSK the wave flips upside down.

The constellation diagram

A constellation plots every allowed symbol as a dot. Distance from the center is amplitude, and angle is phase. The horizontal axis is called I (in-phase) and the vertical axis Q (quadrature). Each dot gets a bit label. A receiver sees a noisy dot and picks the nearest allowed one.

Labels are assigned with Gray coding: neighboring dots differ by exactly one bit. The most common mistake is landing on a neighbor, and with Gray coding that wrecks only one bit.

4 · Bandwidth and Data Rate

You will be able to
  • Estimate the bandwidth a digital signal needs.
  • Calculate spectral efficiency.
  • Use the Shannon limit to see what a channel can carry at best.

Spectrum is limited and shared, so how much room a signal needs matters as much as how fast it sends.

Bandwidth of a digital signal

A well-shaped digital signal takes up a little more than its symbol rate. How much more is set by the roll-off factor α, a filter setting between 0 and 1. Smaller α saves bandwidth but is harder to build.

bandwidth ≈ symbol rate × (1 + α)
spectral efficiency = bit rate ÷ bandwidth (bits/s per Hz)
Worked example: QPSK at 1 Mb/s

QPSK carries 2 bits per symbol, so symbol rate = 1 Mb/s ÷ 2 = 0.5 Msymbols/s.

With α = 0.35, bandwidth = 0.5 × 1.35 = 0.675 MHz.

Spectral efficiency = 1 ÷ 0.675 ≈ 1.5 bits/s per Hz.

Try it: bandwidth and the Shannon limit
The bar compares your bit rate with the absolute Shannon limit for the bandwidth you just computed. Real, uncoded links sit well below it.

The Shannon limit

In 1948 Claude Shannon showed that every channel has a maximum error-free data rate. It depends on only two things.

C = B × log2(1 + SNR)

Here B is bandwidth in hertz, and SNR is the signal-to-noise power ratio as a plain number, not in dB. Convert first: 20 dB is a ratio of 100.

Worked example: a 1 MHz channel at 20 dB

SNR = 100, so C = 1,000,000 × log2(101) ≈ 1,000,000 × 6.66 = 6.66 Mb/s.

Add 10 dB more (ratio 1,000) and C is only about 9.97 Mb/s. Each extra bit per second per Hz costs about 3 dB more signal. Doubling bandwidth, by contrast, doubles the limit.

This shows the central trade of the whole field: you can swap bandwidth for power, or power for bandwidth. FM does it, and so does spread spectrum (Chapter 6). Real systems approach the limit with error-correcting codes. Plain uncoded modulation like the examples in this guide stays several dB short.

Check yourself
Summary
  • Bandwidth ≈ symbol rate × (1 + α). Efficiency = bit rate ÷ bandwidth.
  • Higher-order modulation packs more bits per hertz.
  • Shannon: C = B log2(1 + SNR), with SNR as a ratio. Bandwidth and power trade against each other.

5 · Noise and Errors

You will be able to
  • Calculate a receiver's noise floor from bandwidth and noise figure.
  • Compute SNR and judge whether a modulation will work.
  • Explain why higher-order modulation needs more SNR.

Thermal noise: the floor you cannot beat

Any warm object makes random radio noise. Its power depends on temperature and on how wide a slice of spectrum you listen to. At room temperature (290 K) the noise power density is −174 dBm per hertz. Add the receiver's own noise figure (NF) and you get the noise floor.

noise floor (dBm) = −174 + 10 × log10(bandwidth in Hz) + NF (dB)
SNR (dB) = received power (dBm) − noise floor (dBm)
Worked example: 25 kHz voice channel, NF = 8 dB

10 × log10(25,000) = 44 dB.

Noise floor = −174 + 44 + 8 = −122 dBm.

If the signal arrives at −100 dBm, SNR = −100 − (−122) = 22 dB.

Two habits follow. Wider bandwidth lets in more noise: 10× wider costs 10 dB. And a better (lower NF) receiver gives you dB for free.

From SNR to bit errors

A receiver decides which dot it was sent. Noise scatters the received dots into little clouds. If a cloud spills across the boundary to a neighbor, you get a bit error. The bit error rate (BER) is the fraction of bits wrong. It falls steeply as SNR rises, so each modulation has a fairly sharp “needs at least this much” level.

ModulationSNR per symbol for 0.1% BER
BPSKabout 6.8 dB
QPSKabout 9.8 dB
8-PSKabout 14.8 dB
16-QAMabout 16.6 dB
64-QAMabout 22.6 dB

Uncoded, ideal receiver, steady noise only. Standard textbook approximations, so treat them as teaching values. Real radios differ by a dB or two and use error-correcting codes. Here “SNR per symbol” is the SNR when the receiver bandwidth equals the symbol rate.

Why a 17E caresA jammer works by raising the noise at the victim's receiver. It does not matter whether it is noise, a tone, or a deliberate signal: the question is how far it pushes SNR below the modulation's requirement.

Buying SNR back with coding

Forward error correction adds extra bits that let the receiver fix some mistakes. It costs bandwidth or data rate, and in return the link works at lower SNR for the same error rate. Coding is how modern radios work close to the Shannon limit.

6 · Interference, Jamming and Spread Spectrum

You will be able to
  • Combine noise and a jammer into an effective SNR.
  • Explain how spread spectrum and frequency hopping resist jamming.
  • Calculate processing gain and jamming margin.

What a jammer does to a link

A receiver cannot tell noise from interference. Whatever lands in its band adds to the noise. The ratio of jammer power to signal power at the receiver is the jammer-to-signal ratio (J/S). The effective quality of the link is the signal against everything else:

SINR = S ÷ (N + J) (powers, not dB)

Do the addition in watts or milliwatts, then convert back to dB. If the jammer is far above the noise, the noise floor no longer matters and SINR is about 1 ÷ (J/S).

Different jammers break different modulations in different ways. Steady noise degrades everything gradually, and then sharply for FM past its threshold. A single tone does nothing until it is strong enough to push a symbol across a boundary, and then does a lot, which hits dense constellations first. Short pulses can ruin a link at the same average power as steady noise, because bits hit during the burst are destroyed. The Modulation & Jamming Lab lets you see all of these.

Fighting back: spread the signal

If the problem is that a jammer can concentrate its power on your narrow signal, make your signal wide.

  • Direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS). Multiply each data bit by a fast pseudo-random pattern of chips. The signal spreads across a bandwidth many times wider, at proportionally lower power density. The receiver multiplies by the same code. That collapses your signal back to narrow while a narrowband jammer, which does not match the code, gets spread out and diluted. GPS works this way.
  • Frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS). Both radios jump together among many channels in an order only they know. A jammer on one channel hits only the hops that land there.
processing gain = 10 × log10(spread bandwidth ÷ data rate) (dB)
jamming margin = processing gain − required Eb/N0 − system losses (dB)
Worked example: GPS civil signal

The C/A code runs at 1.023 million chips per second. The data rate is 50 bits per second.

Processing gain = 10 × log10(1,023,000 ÷ 50) = 10 × log10(20,460) ≈ 43 dB.

That gain is what lets a receiver dig a signal out from below the noise. It also sets how much jamming a receiver can ride through, which is why the signal is still easy to jam: it starts out so weak.

Hopping in the field. SINCGARS tactical radios hop across 30–87.975 MHz in 25 kHz steps, which is 2,320 channels, at roughly 100 hops per second. For two radios to stay in step they must share the hopset, the net ID, the time, and the TRANSEC key. Classic Bluetooth hops across 79 channels at 1,600 hops per second.

Hopping is not magic. A “follower” jammer that can detect where you landed and retune faster than your dwell time can still hit the rest of each hop. At 100 hops per second the dwell is 10 ms, which is why that rate is considered slow. Hopping also makes direction finding harder, not impossible.

7 · Put It Together: Design a Link

You will be able to
  • Walk a link from transmitter to receiver in dB.
  • Pick the fastest modulation the link can support.
  • Predict how a jammer forces a link to step down.

Everything in this guide comes together in one problem. A link has a transmitter, antennas, a path, a receiver, and maybe a jammer. Work it in five steps:

  1. Path loss for the distance and frequency.
  2. Received power = transmit power + both antenna gains − path loss.
  3. Noise floor from bandwidth and noise figure.
  4. SNR (or SINR with a jammer) = received power − noise (plus jammer).
  5. Highest modulation whose required SNR is below yours.

Free-space path loss (the same formula as the Radio Waves guide) is:

FSPL (dB) = 32.44 + 20·log10(f in MHz) + 20·log10(d in km)
Worked scenario: a 5.8 GHz data link over 5 km

Given: 27 dBm transmitter (0.5 W), 10 dBi antennas at each end, 5 km, 5,800 MHz, 20 MHz channel, receiver noise figure 6 dB.

1. Path loss: 32.44 + 75.27 + 13.98 = 121.7 dB.

2. Received power: 27 + 10 + 10 − 121.7 = −74.7 dBm.

3. Noise floor: −174 + 73 + 6 = −95.0 dBm.

4. SNR: −74.7 − (−95.0) = 20.3 dB.

5. Modulation: 16-QAM needs about 16.6 dB, so it works with 3.7 dB to spare. 64-QAM needs 22.6 dB, so it does not. With α = 0.25 the 20 MHz channel carries about 4 bits × 16 Msymbols/s = 64 Mb/s.

Now a jammer arrives at −90 dBm. Noise plus jammer in milliwatts: 10−9.5 + 10−9.0 → −88.8 dBm. SINR = −74.7 − (−88.8) = 14.1 dB. 16-QAM (16.6) fails, and 8-PSK (14.8) just misses. QPSK (9.8) holds, so the link steps down to about 32 Mb/s. The jammer's J/S is only −15 dB, so it did real damage while weaker than the signal.

Try it: link designer
Free-space path only. Real links add terrain, foliage, fading, and margin. Set the jammer to its lowest value for “off”. The defaults are the worked scenario above.
