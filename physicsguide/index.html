What are Radio Waves?
RF physics, made simple.

Every signal a 17E touches is a radio wave. Understand how waves travel, spread, and fade, and the whole spectrum stops being mysterious.

A wave is just a wiggle that travels

You will be able to
  • Link frequency and wavelength.
  • Say why higher frequencies need smaller antennas.

Radio waves are invisible ripples of electric and magnetic energy moving at the speed of light. Two numbers describe any wave: how fast it wiggles and how long each wiggle is.

🌊 Ocean waves at the beach: frequency is how many waves hit the shore each second. Wavelength is the distance from one wave crest to the next. Fast little ripples = high frequency, short wavelength. Slow rolling swells = low frequency, long wavelength.

Frequency and wavelength are linked

100 MHz
WAVELENGTH
3.00 m

Turn the frequency up and the wavelength gets shorter — they're two sides of the same coin.

wavelength (m) = 300 ÷ frequency (MHz)

Because all radio waves travel at the same speed (the speed of light, about 300 million meters per second), frequency and wavelength are locked together. Know one, you can always find the other.

⚡They all move at light speed. A signal crosses a football field, or reaches a satellite, at 300,000 km per second. Frequency only changes the size of the wiggle, not its speed.
📏Wavelength sets antenna size. A good antenna is a fraction of the wavelength long. That's why an FM antenna is a meter or so, but a Wi-Fi antenna is tiny — higher frequency, shorter wave, smaller antenna.

The radio spectrum — one big dial

You will be able to
  • Name the main bands and their uses.
  • Say what the ionosphere does for HF.

The radio spectrum runs from very low frequencies up to microwaves and beyond. Each band behaves differently and is used for different jobs.

🎹 Think of a giant piano. Low notes (low frequencies) are the deep, booming keys — they travel far and bend around obstacles. High notes (high frequencies) are the bright, sharp keys — they carry lots of data but don't travel as far or bend as well. The spectrum is that keyboard, and every service gets its own keys.

📡Lower = farther, higher = faster. Low frequencies travel long distances and bend around terrain but carry less data. High frequencies carry huge data rates but need near line-of-sight.
🌍HF can bounce off the sky. High-frequency signals can reflect off a layer of the upper atmosphere (the ionosphere) and skip thousands of miles — which is how shortwave reaches across oceans without satellites.

How waves get from A to B

You will be able to
  • Describe line of sight, ground wave, and sky wave.
  • Tell reflection, refraction, and diffraction apart.

Radio waves don't just fly straight. Depending on frequency and terrain, they travel by several different paths.

📶
Line of sight
the straight shot

Most tactical radio and all high frequencies travel in a straight line, like light from a flashlight. If a hill or building is in the way, the signal is blocked. This is the dominant mode for VHF/UHF.

🌍
Ground wave
hugs the earth

Low frequencies can follow the curve of the earth for a good distance, especially over water. That's how AM radio reaches beyond the horizon.

☁️
Sky wave
bounces off the atmosphere

HF signals can refract off the ionosphere and skip back down far away — enabling long-range HF comms without any infrastructure.

Waves also bend and bounce

BehaviorWhat happens
ReflectionBounces off a large flat surface (a building, a hill, the ground)
RefractionBends when passing through changing conditions (like the atmosphere)
DiffractionBends around the edge of an obstacle, filling in a bit of the "shadow"
🔦 Light does all of this too. Reflection is a mirror. Refraction is a straw looking "bent" in a glass of water. Diffraction is light spilling around a doorway. Radio waves obey the same rules — just at frequencies your eyes can't see.

Why signals fade

You will be able to
  • Calculate free-space path loss.
  • Explain fade margin.

A signal gets weaker the farther it travels — and a few other things can make it worse. Understanding loss is the key to whether a link works.

🔊 Like sound from a speaker: stand next to it and it's loud; walk away and it fades fast. Radio does the same — and the drop-off is steep. Every time you double the distance, the signal loses about 6 dB (drops to a quarter of the power). Distance is expensive.

Other things that hurt a signal

🌧️Weather & obstacles: rain, foliage, walls, and terrain all absorb or block energy — worse at higher frequencies.
🔀Multipath: a signal can reach you by several bounced paths at once. When they arrive out of step they can partly cancel, causing the signal to flutter or drop — that's fading.
📊What matters is signal-to-noise. A link works when the wanted signal is comfortably above the background noise floor. It's not about raw power — it's about the ratio.

The actual formula

The "6 dB per doubling" rule of thumb comes from the Free-Space Path Loss formula — the same idea, just precise enough to actually calculate with:

FSPL (dB) = 20·log₁₀(d) + 20·log₁₀(f) + 32.44

where d is distance in kilometers and f is frequency in MHz. Both distance and frequency show up with a factor of 20 — meaning doubling either one costs the same ~6 dB, which is exactly where that shortcut rule came from.

Worked example

A 100 MHz link at 10 km:

FSPL = 20·log₁₀(10) + 20·log₁₀(100) + 32.44 = 20(1) + 20(2) + 32.44 = 92.44 dB

That 92.44 dB is how much weaker the signal arrives compared to how strong it left — this is the number a link budget (from the Decibels math guide) has to overcome with transmit power and antenna gain.

Fade margin: the safety cushion

A link "just barely" closing on paper is a bad plan — weather, terrain, and multipath (below) all eat into that margin in practice. Fade margin is the extra dB of headroom built in above the minimum needed, specifically to absorb those real-world losses without the link dropping. More margin means a more reliable link, at the cost of needing more transmit power, gain, or a shorter range.

Antennas — the doorway to the air

You will be able to
  • Compare omnidirectional and directional antennas.
  • Say what gain really is.

An antenna turns electricity in a wire into radio waves in the air (and back again). Its shape decides where the energy goes.

💡 Bare bulb vs. flashlight: a plain antenna is like a bare light bulb — it glows in every direction (good coverage, low reach). A directional antenna is like a flashlight — it focuses the same energy into a beam, reaching much farther in one direction while ignoring the rest. That focusing is called gain.
📍
Omnidirectional
radiates all around

A whip or dipole sends energy out in all directions — great when you don't know where the other station is, or need to talk to many at once. Lower gain, wider coverage.

🎯
Directional
focuses a beam

A Yagi (the classic "TV antenna" shape) or dish concentrates energy toward one direction. Much longer reach and better rejection of interference — but you have to aim it.

➕Antennas don't make power — they focus it. "Gain" (measured in dBi) means concentrating energy into a direction, not creating more of it. A high-gain antenna is just a very good flashlight.
↕️Polarization matters. If one antenna is vertical and the other horizontal, they hear each other poorly. Matching polarization keeps the link strong — a mismatch can cost 20 dB.

Beamwidth: the tradeoff for gain

Gain isn't free — it's traded directly against coverage angle. A high-gain directional antenna squeezes energy into a narrow cone (its beamwidth), which is exactly why it reaches farther in that direction but hears nothing outside it. Roughly speaking, the higher the gain, the narrower the beamwidth: a wide-coverage antenna might have a 60° beamwidth and modest gain, while a dish antenna might narrow that down to a few degrees for very high gain.

Worked example: gain vs. range

Doubling an antenna's gain (a +3 dB improvement) has the same effect on link budget as doubling transmit power — both add 3 dB to the signal arriving at the far end. The difference: transmit power costs more battery and heat; gain costs beamwidth (aiming precision) instead. That's the real decision behind choosing a directional antenna over an omni: you're trading "must aim it" for "reaches farther without more power."

Beyond vertical and horizontal: circular polarization

Some systems (GPS and satellite links, notably) use circular polarization instead — the wave's orientation rotates continuously as it travels, rather than staying fixed vertical or horizontal. This makes the link far less sensitive to the receiving antenna's exact rotation or to reflections that would otherwise scramble a linear signal's orientation — a major reason GPS satellites transmit circularly polarized signals despite the added antenna complexity.

Noise, SNR, and interference

You will be able to
  • Calculate a thermal noise floor.
  • Work out SNR and say what harmonics and EMI are.

A link works when the wanted signal is comfortably above the noise. Noise is always there, and interference makes it worse.

🎤 A conversation at a party: you can follow your friend if their voice is louder than the room. Turn the music up and you have to shout. Radio is the same: what matters is not how loud you are, but how far above the background you sit.

The thermal noise floor

Every receiver sits on a floor of random noise from the heat of its own parts. It rises with the bandwidth you listen to, because a wider window lets in more noise. A good rule for room temperature:

noise floor (dBm) = −174 + 10·log10(bandwidth in Hz) + noise figure (dB)

A 1 MHz bandwidth gives −114 dBm before the receiver adds its own noise. A 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel gives about −101 dBm, and a typical receiver noise figure of 5 dB makes that about −96 dBm.

BandwidthNoise floor (noise figure 0 dB)
200 kHz (FM broadcast)about −121 dBm
1 MHz−114 dBm
20 MHz (Wi-Fi channel)about −101 dBm
SNR (dB) = signal level (dBm) − noise floor (dBm)

A signal at −85 dBm over a −96 dBm floor has an SNR of 11 dB. More SNR means a cleaner link and a faster data rate.

Harmonics

🎻Harmonics are echoes at multiples. An overdriven or non-linear amplifier makes unwanted copies of a signal at 2, 3, 4 times its frequency. A 100 MHz transmitter can leak energy at 200 MHz and 300 MHz, which can interfere with other services. Filters at the output remove them.

Interference and EMI

⚡EMI is unwanted energy. Switching power supplies, motors, and digital electronics leak radio noise. It can come in through the air or along power and signal cables. The fixes are shielding, filtering, good grounding, and distance between the source and the receiver.
💡Interference is not always an attack. A noisy generator, a nearby transmitter on an adjacent channel, or a bad cable can all raise the noise floor. Rule these out before you suspect jamming.
