Section 1: The Basics of RF Power

Why Standard Math Fails Us

In the world of Radio Frequency (RF), Radar, and Electronic Warfare (EW), we deal with extreme extremes.

A ground-based tactical radar might push out 1,000,000 Watts of energy to scan the horizon. But the returning echo bouncing off an incoming artillery shell or a small enemy drone miles away might only hit the receiving antenna with 0.0000000001 Watts.

If we tried to use standard decimal math to calculate the difference, the massive strings of zeros would cause constant human errors. To solve this, RF engineers abandoned linear math in favor of a Logarithmic scale.

Section 1: The Basics of RF Power

Linear vs. Logarithmic Math

Understanding the shift in how we count.

Linear Scale

This is standard math. You add and subtract quantities. The distance between 1 and 2 is the exact same as the distance between 99 and 100.

If you have 10 Watts and you add a 50 Watt amplifier, you have 60 Watts. (10 + 50 = 60).

Logarithmic Scale

This is multiplication based. You don't add amounts; you add ratios (exponents).

In log math, when you "add", you are actually multiplying the total power. When you "subtract", you are dividing the total power.

Section 1: The Basics of RF Power

The Decibel (dB)

The fundamental unit of measurement in Electronic Warfare.

The "Bel" was originally invented by Bell Telephone Labs to measure signal loss over miles of telegraph wire. It was too large of a unit, so we use the Deci-bel (one tenth of a Bel).

A Decibel (dB) is simply a ratio between two power levels. It describes how much a signal grew (Gain) or how much a signal shrank (Loss).

⚠️ Crucial Point: A decibel is NOT an absolute amount of power. Saying "+3 dB" is exactly like saying "Double it." You cannot know the final power unless you know what you started with!

Section 2: The Rules of 3 and 10

Ditching the Calculator

In the field, you don't have time to use complex logarithmic formulas (e.g., dB = 10 * log10(P2/P1)). You only need to memorize two rules.

By memorizing the Rule of 3 and the Rule of 10, you can calculate almost any RF power budget entirely in your head.

Rule Addition (+) Subtraction (-)
The 3 dB Rule Multiply power by 2 Divide power by 2
The 10 dB Rule Multiply power by 10 Divide power by 10
Section 2: The Rules of 3 and 10

Applying the 3 dB Rule

Every 3 decibels equates to a factor of two.

Positive Gain (+3 dB)

If you have a 10 Watt transmitter and you run it through an amplifier rated at +3 dB, what is the output?

Rule: +3 dB means multiply by 2.

10W × 2 = 20 Watts.

Negative Loss (-3 dB)

If you push 10 Watts down a damaged coaxial cable that is rated at -3 dB of loss, what hits the antenna?

Rule: -3 dB means divide by 2.

10W ÷ 2 = 5 Watts.

Section 2: The Rules of 3 and 10

Applying the 10 dB Rule

Every 10 decibels equates to a factor of ten.

Positive Gain (+10 dB)

If you have a 5 Watt radio and attach a high-gain directional antenna rated at +10 dB, what is the effective output?

Rule: +10 dB means multiply by 10.

5W × 10 = 50 Watts.

Negative Loss (-10 dB)

If an enemy jammer hits your receiver with an attenuator that causes -10 dB of loss to a 50 Watt signal, what remains?

Rule: -10 dB means divide by 10.

50W ÷ 10 = 5 Watts.

Section 2: The Rules of 3 and 10

Combining the Rules

What if the gain is +13 dB? Or +20 dB? Just stack the rules.

Because dB is logarithmic, you ADD the decibels together to MULTIPLY the physical power.

Example: You have an amplifier that provides +13 dB of gain. You input 1 Watt.

  1. Break 13 dB into pieces you know: 10 dB + 3 dB = 13 dB.
  2. Apply the first piece (+10 dB): 1 Watt × 10 = 10 Watts.
  3. Apply the second piece (+3 dB) to the new total: 10 Watts × 2 = 20 Watts.
A +13 dB gain will always multiply your starting power by 20.

Section 3: Absolute Power

The Problem with dB

If I tell you I gained +3 dB today, do I have a lot of power?

You don't know. If I started with 1 Watt, I now have 2 Watts. If I started with 1,000 Watts, I now have 2,000 Watts.

Because dB is just a ratio of change, we need a way to express an Absolute Power Level using the logarithmic scale so we can do math easily without losing track of our baseline.

Section 3: Absolute Power

Introducing dBm

The "m" anchors the math to reality.

To measure absolute power, engineers created dBm. The "m" stands for 1 milliWatt (0.001 Watts).

0 dBm = Exactly 1 milliWatt

Now, instead of saying "I have 1 milliWatt of power," you can say "I am transmitting at 0 dBm." Once you are anchored in dBm, you can simply add and subtract standard dB gains and losses!

Section 3: Absolute Power

Converting dBm to Watts

Let's scale up from 0 dBm using our Rules of 3 and 10.

Absolute Level (dBm) Actual Power (Watts) The Math applied to 1 mW
0 dBm 1 milliWatt (0.001 W) The Baseline
+10 dBm 10 mW (1 mW × 10)
+20 dBm 100 mW (10 mW × 10)
+30 dBm 1 Watt (100 mW × 10)
Memorize this: 30 dBm is exactly 1 Watt. You will use this constantly.
Section 3: Absolute Power

The Power Budget

Calculating what actually leaves the antenna.

In a real system, power is created, lost in cables, and then amplified by the antenna. This calculation is called the Effective Isotropic Radiated Power (EIRP).

EIRP = TX Power (dBm) - Cable Loss (dB) + Antenna Gain (dB)

Section 3: Absolute Power

Interactive Power Budget

Step through the calculation to find the final radiated power.

1. Transmitter Output: The radio outputs 1 Watt.
(Convert to dBm. 1 Watt = +30 dBm)
2. Coaxial Cable Loss: The long cable run to the roof loses -3 dB.
(30 dBm - 3 dB = +27 dBm at the antenna base)
3. Antenna Gain: A parabolic dish focuses the signal, providing +10 dB of gain.
(27 dBm + 10 dB = +37 dBm total EIRP)
4. Final Conversion: What is +37 dBm in actual Watts?
Start at 30 dBm (1W). Add 10 dB (1W x10 = 10W). Subtract 3 dB (10W / 2 = 5W).
Final Radiated Power: 5 Watts

Section 4: Radar Ranging

The Speed of Light

How does a radar know exactly how far away an enemy jet is?

Radar relies entirely on the fact that RF electromagnetic energy travels through space at a constant, unyielding velocity: The Speed of Light (c).

  • In Meters: ~300,000,000 meters per second.
  • In Statute Miles: ~186,000 miles per second.
  • In Nautical Miles (NM): ~162,000 NM per second.

Because the speed never changes, we can determine the exact distance to a target simply by starting a stopwatch when the pulse leaves the antenna, and stopping the watch when the echo returns.

Section 4: Radar Ranging

The Radar Range Equation

The fundamental math of all pulse radar systems.

The total time measured by the radar's stopwatch is for a round trip (out to the target and back again). If we used that total time, the calculated distance would be exactly twice as far as the actual target.

Therefore, we must divide the time by 2.

Range = Speed × (Time / 2)
Section 4: Radar Ranging

Radar Ranging in Meters & Kilometers

Converting microseconds into physical distance on the battlefield.

In Army tactical radar applications, we measure range in meters and kilometers instead of nautical miles.

Because light travels at approximately 300 meters per microsecond (µs), and the pulse must make a round trip, we use two simple constants for mental math:

1 µs delay = 150 Meters of Range
6.67 µs delay = 1 Kilometer (The Radar Kilometer)
  • If the echo returns in 10 µs, the target is 1,500 meters away (1.5 km).
  • If the echo returns in 66.7 µs, the target is exactly 10 kilometers away.
Section 4: Radar Ranging

Maximum Unambiguous Range

How far can the radar see before it gets confused?

A pulse radar transmits a massive burst of energy, then shuts off its transmitter and turns on its receiver to "listen" for the echo. The time it spends listening before firing the next pulse is called the Pulse Repetition Time (PRT).

The Problem: If a target is so far away that its echo returns after the radar has fired its second pulse, the radar will think the echo belongs to the second pulse, placing a false target extremely close to the transmitting radar.

The longest distance a pulse can travel and return before the next pulse fires is the system's Maximum Unambiguous Range.

Module 8 Review

Summary of Key Concepts

  • Linear vs Logarithmic: RF math uses Log scales (dB) to compress massive numbers.
  • The 3 dB Rule: +3 dB = Double Power (x2). -3 dB = Half Power (/2).
  • The 10 dB Rule: +10 dB = Multiply by 10. -10 dB = Divide by 10.
  • dB vs dBm: dB is a ratio of change. dBm is absolute power (0 dBm = 1 milliWatt. 30 dBm = 1 Watt).
  • Power Budget (EIRP): TX dBm - Cable Loss + Antenna Gain.
  • Radar Ranging: Range = c × (Time/2). The Radar Mile constant is 12.36 µs.
  • Max Range: Limited by the PRT (the listening time before the next pulse fires).
