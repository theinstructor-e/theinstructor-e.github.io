Section 1: Active vs Passive

Entering the World of Electronics

Up until now, we have dealt with basic electrical circuits. Now we transition to true electronics.

The distinction between "electrical" and "electronic" comes down to how we control the electrons.

  • Passive Components: Resistors, Capacitors, and Inductors. These simply react to voltage and current. They cannot amplify a signal or make logical decisions.
  • Active Components: Diodes, Transistors, and Integrated Circuits (ICs). These components can actively control, switch, or amplify electron flow. They are the brains of modern systems.
Section 1: Passive Components

Resistors: The Foundation

Before we can control active components, we must set their operating conditions using resistors.

As discussed in Module 1, resistance opposes current flow. In electronic circuits, we use physical components called Resistors to deliberately drop voltage or limit current to protect sensitive chips.

Because these components are often too tiny to print numbers on, their resistance value in Ohms (Ω) is painted onto them using a universal Color Code.

Section 1: Passive Components

Decoding the Resistor Color Code

Most common resistors use a 4-band color code system.

  1. Band 1 (1st Digit): The first number.
  2. Band 2 (2nd Digit): The second number.
  3. Band 3 (Multiplier): How many zeros to add to the end.
  4. Band 4 (Tolerance): How accurate the resistor is (e.g., Gold = ±5%, Silver = ±10%).

Section 2: Semiconductors

The Magic of Silicon

To create active components, we use Semiconductor materials, primarily Silicon (Si).

Pure silicon is actually a terrible conductor. Its atoms lock together in a rigid crystal lattice, leaving no "free electrons" to carry current.

To make it useful, engineers use a process called Doping. This involves injecting impurities into the pure silicon to alter its electrical properties.

Section 2: Semiconductors

N-Type and P-Type Material

Doping creates two entirely different types of materials.

N-Type (Negative)

Doped with elements like Phosphorus.

This creates an excess of Free Electrons. The primary carriers of current in this material are negative electrons.

P-Type (Positive)

Doped with elements like Boron.

This creates empty spaces in the atomic structure called "Holes." A hole acts like a positive charge that attracts electrons.

Section 3: Diodes

The P-N Junction

The magic happens when you fuse a piece of P-type material to a piece of N-type material. This creates a Diode.

At the exact point they touch, electrons from the N-side fall into the holes on the P-side. This creates a neutral wall called the Depletion Region, which acts as a barrier to current flow.

Section 3: Diodes

Biasing the Diode

A diode acts as a one-way electrical valve based on how you apply voltage to it.

Forward Bias (ON)

If you connect a battery's Positive (+) terminal to the P-side (Anode) and Negative (-) to the N-side (Cathode), the pressure shrinks the depletion region.

Result: Current flows freely.

Reverse Bias (OFF)

If you connect the battery backward, the charges pull the electrons and holes further apart, widening the depletion region.

Result: Current is blocked completely.

Section 3: Diodes

Rectification: AC to DC

The most common use for a diode is turning Alternating Current into Direct Current.

Because AC constantly reverses direction, placing a diode in the circuit blocks the reverse half of the cycle (the negative trough). The output is a pulsing current that only flows in one direction: Pulsating DC. This is called Half-Wave Rectification.

Section 4: Transistors

The Transistor: The Brain of Electronics

If a diode is a one-way valve, a transistor is an electronically controlled throttle valve.

A transistor is created by sandwiching three layers of doped semiconductor material together (either N-P-N or P-N-P). This creates a device with three distinct leads:

  1. Emitter: The source of the electrons.
  2. Base: The thin middle layer. This is the "control gate."
  3. Collector: The destination where the electrons are gathered.

Section 4: Transistors

How a Transistor Works

By applying a tiny current to the Base, you control a massive current flowing from the Emitter to the Collector.

As a Switch

In digital electronics (computers), the transistor is fully ON or fully OFF.

If voltage is applied to the Base, the "gate" flies open, and current flows (a digital "1"). If voltage is removed, the gate shuts (a digital "0"). Modern computer chips contain billions of these microscopic switches.

As an Amplifier

In analog electronics (radios, radar), the transistor operates partially open.

A weak, fluctuating radio signal is fed into the Base. The transistor allows a massive, high-power current from a battery to flow through the Collector matching that exact same fluctuation, amplifying the signal perfectly.

Section 5: Electron Tubes

The Predecessor: Electron Tubes

Before solid-state silicon, electronics relied on fragile glass vacuum tubes.

Electron tubes (or Vacuum Tubes) operate on the principle of Thermionic Emission. When you heat a metal filament (the Cathode) inside a vacuum, it literally boils electrons off its surface into empty space. These free-floating electrons are then pulled across the vacuum to a positively charged plate (the Anode).

By placing a metal grid between the cathode and anode, engineers could control the flow of electrons through the vacuum—creating the first amplifiers and switches.

Section 5: Electron Tubes

So why do I care?

Transistors replaced tubes because tubes were huge, fragile, and generated massive amounts of heat. So why do I care?

Consumer electronics do not require or handle the massive power levels needed for military applications. While your smartphone uses billions of silicon transistors, military systems still rely heavily on specialized vacuum tubes like Magnetrons, Klystrons, and Traveling-Wave Tubes (TWTs).

The Reason: Solid-state silicon melts under extreme power loads. When a radar system needs to generate millions of watts of raw RF energy in a microsecond, specialized electron tubes are still the only devices physically capable of surviving the heat and voltage.
