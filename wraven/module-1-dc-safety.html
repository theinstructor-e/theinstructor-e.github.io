Section 1: Safety

Electrical Safety: The Grim Reality

Before we learn how to make equipment work, you must learn how to survive working on it.

There is a common saying in the field: "Voltage hurts, but Current kills."

It takes only 0.1 Amperes (100 milliamperes) of current crossing your heart to cause ventricular fibrillation (lethal cardiac arrest). For context, a standard 100-watt lightbulb draws about 0.8 Amps—eight times the lethal dose.

⚠️ You must adopt a zero-tolerance policy. Never bypass a safety interlock, never skip a tag-out, and never assume a circuit is dead just because a switch is thrown.
Section 1: Safety

Lockout/Tagout & The One-Hand Rule

Safety protocols are written in blood. Follow them without exception.

Lockout / Tagout (LOTO)

Before touching bare conductors, you must secure the power source, apply a physical padlock that only YOU have the key to, and measure the circuit with a multimeter to verify zero volts. Relays stick; switches lie. Trust your meter.

The One-Hand Rule

When you *must* take voltage measurements on live equipment, put your non-dominant hand in your pocket. This prevents creating a circuit path from one arm, directly across your heart, and out the other arm.

Section 1: Safety

Electrical Shock Rescue

If a teammate is shocked, your adrenaline will tell you to grab them. If you do, you will also become part of the circuit.

  1. Secure the Power: Hit the emergency stop (E-Stop) or throw the main breaker. This is always step one.
  2. Separate the Victim: If you cannot secure power, use a non-conductive object (dry wood, rubber, a web belt) to physically pry them off the live conductor.
  3. First Aid / CPR: Once separated, check for breathing and a pulse. Initiate CPR immediately if needed and call for medical support.

Section 2: Matter & Energy

The Structure of Matter

To understand electricity, we have to zoom in to the atomic level.

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. It exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas.

  • Element: A substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means (e.g., Copper, Gold, Oxygen).
  • Compound: A chemical combination of two or more elements (e.g., Water is H2O).
  • Atom: The smallest particle of an element that retains the characteristics of that element.
Section 2: Matter & Energy

Inside the Atom

An atom looks like a miniature solar system.

The Nucleus (Center)

Contains Protons (positive charge) and Neutrons (no charge).

The Orbits (Shells)

Electrons (negative charge) orbit the nucleus. The outermost shell is the Valence Shell.

The Golden Rule of Charges: Like charges repel each other (Positive pushes away Positive). Opposite charges attract each other (Positive pulls Negative).
Section 2: Matter & Energy

Conductors, Insulators & Semiconductors

The number of electrons in the outer valence shell determines how a material handles electricity.

  • Conductors (1-3 valence electrons): Because the outer shell is mostly empty, these electrons are loosely bound and can easily jump from atom to atom. (Examples: Copper, Silver, Gold, Aluminum).
  • Insulators (5-8 valence electrons): The outer shell is full or nearly full. The electrons are locked in place and refuse to move. (Examples: Rubber, Glass, Dry Wood, Plastics).
  • Semiconductors (Exactly 4 valence electrons): They are neither good conductors nor good insulators. They can be chemically altered (doped) to act as either. (Examples: Silicon, Germanium).
Section 3: Voltage

Voltage: The Electromotive Force (EMF)

Electrons won't move on their own. They need pressure to push them. That pressure is called Voltage (E).

Voltage is created by separating positive and negative charges. Nature wants them to balance out. The six ways we can force this separation to generate voltage are:

  1. Friction: Static electricity (rubbing shoes on a carpet).
  2. Pressure (Piezoelectric): Squeezing certain crystals (used in microphones and quartz watches).
  3. Heat (Thermoelectric): Heating the junction of two different metals (Thermocouples).
  4. Light (Photoelectric): Solar panels absorbing photons.
  5. Chemical: Batteries using chemical reactions to separate charges.
  6. Magnetic: Moving a conductor through a magnetic field (Generators/Alternators).
Section 3: Current

Current: The Flow of Electrons

If Voltage is the pressure, Current (I) is the actual flow of the electrons through the wire.

Current is measured in Amperes (A). One Ampere represents roughly 6.24 x 10^18 electrons moving past a single point in one second.

Direction of Flow:

  • Conventional Flow Theory: An old theory stating current flows from Positive to Negative. You will see this in old schematics.
  • Electron Flow Theory: The true physical reality. Because electrons are negatively charged, they are repelled by the Negative terminal and attracted to the Positive terminal. Current flows from Negative to Positive.
Section 3: Resistance

Resistance: The Opposition to Flow

No wire is a perfect conductor. Resistance (R) is the friction that opposes the electron flow, generating heat.

Resistance is measured in Ohms (Ω). Four factors determine the resistance of a wire:

  1. Material: Copper has less resistance than iron.
  2. Length: The longer the wire, the higher the resistance.
  3. Cross-Sectional Area (Thickness): A thick wire has LESS resistance than a thin wire (like a multi-lane highway vs a single-lane road).
  4. Temperature: For most conductors, as temperature goes UP, resistance goes UP.
Section 4: The Math

Ohm's Law

Voltage, Current, and Resistance are permanently mathematically linked.

The core formula is: I = E ÷ R

Where:
I = Current (Amps)
E = Voltage (Volts/EMF)
R = Resistance (Ohms)

If E goes UP

And R stays the same, Current (I) must go UP. More pressure equals more flow.

If R goes UP

And E stays the same, Current (I) must go DOWN. More restriction equals less flow.

Section 4: The Math

Electrical Power (Watts)

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is converted into another form (like heat, light, or mechanical motion).

Power is measured in Watts (W) or represented by the letter P.

The formula for Power is: P = I × E

(Power equals Current multiplied by Voltage).

Q: A radar transmitter operates at 100 Volts (E) and draws 5 Amps of current (I). How much Power (P) is it consuming?
Section 5: Circuit Types

Series Circuits

In a series circuit, there is only ONE path for the electrons to flow.

The Rules of Series Circuits:

  • Current is Constant: The current (Amps) is exactly the same at any point in the circuit.
  • Resistance Adds Up: Total resistance is the sum of all individual resistors (Rt = R1 + R2...).
  • The Fatal Flaw: If one component breaks (e.g. Load 1 burns out), the entire circuit stops working because the single path is broken.
Section 5: Circuit Types

Parallel Circuits

In a parallel circuit, there are MULTIPLE paths (branches) for the electrons to flow.

The Rules of Parallel Circuits:

  • Voltage is Constant: The voltage across every branch is exactly the same as the source voltage.
  • Current Divides: The total current splits up and travels down the different branches.
  • The Advantage: If Branch 1 breaks, Branch 2 keeps working. This is how buildings and homes are wired.
