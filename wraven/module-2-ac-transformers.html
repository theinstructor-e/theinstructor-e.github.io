Section 1: Alternating Current

Why Use Alternating Current?

Module 1 covered Direct Current (DC), which flows constantly in one direction like water in a pipe. But the modern world runs on Alternating Current (AC).

In AC circuits, the electrons constantly reverse direction. They flow forward, stop, flow backward, stop, and repeat.

The primary advantage: AC voltage can be easily stepped up to extremely high voltages for long-distance power transmission (reducing power loss), and then stepped down to safe levels for equipment. Doing this with DC is incredibly difficult and expensive.

Section 1: Alternating Current

The Sine Wave

The fundamental waveform of Alternating Current is the Sine Wave.

  • Cycle: One complete sequence of a positive alternation and a negative alternation.
  • Alternation: Half of a cycle (the part above the zero line, or the part below it).
Section 1: Alternating Current

Frequency and Period

How fast is the AC reversing direction?

Frequency (f)

The number of complete cycles that occur in one second. Measured in Hertz (Hz).

Standard US wall power is 60 Hz (60 full cycles per second). Aircraft and shipboard systems often use 400 Hz.

Period (T)

The time it takes to complete exactly one cycle. Measured in seconds.

Frequency and Period are inversely proportional:
T = 1 / f

Section 2: AC Values

Measuring AC: Peak vs. RMS

Because AC voltage is constantly changing from zero to maximum, how do we assign it a single number?

  • Peak Value: The absolute highest voltage reached during the cycle.
  • Peak-to-Peak (P-P): The voltage from the positive peak to the negative peak (2x the Peak value).
  • RMS (Effective) Value: This is the most important measurement. It is the AC voltage that produces the exact same heating effect as an equivalent DC voltage. When a multimeter reads "120V AC", it is reading the RMS value.
The Math: RMS = Peak × 0.707
Section 3: Circuit Properties

Inductance (L)

Wire coils (inductors) react uniquely to Alternating Current.

Inductance is the physical property of a circuit that opposes any change in CURRENT.

When AC current flows through a coil, it creates an expanding and collapsing magnetic field. This shifting magnetic field induces a Counter-Electromotive Force (CEMF) back into the wire, which pushes back against the original current.

  • Unit of Measurement: Henry (H).
  • Effect on Phase: Inductance causes the Current to lag behind the Voltage.
Section 3: Circuit Properties

Capacitance (C)

Capacitors store energy in an electrostatic field between two plates.

Capacitance is the physical property of a circuit that opposes any change in VOLTAGE.

When voltage pushes electrons onto one plate of a capacitor, it builds up a charge. The capacitor resists this changing voltage by acting as a temporary battery, absorbing energy as voltage rises, and releasing it as voltage falls.

  • Unit of Measurement: Farad (F).
  • Effect on Phase: Capacitance causes the Voltage to lag behind the Current.
Section 3: Circuit Properties

Reactance & Impedance

In an AC circuit, pure resistance isn't the only thing fighting the current.

Reactance (X)

The opposition to AC current caused by Inductors and Capacitors. It is measured in Ohms, but unlike resistance, it does not dissipate power as heat.

  • Inductive Reactance (XL)
  • Capacitive Reactance (XC)

Impedance (Z)

The total opposition to alternating current. It is the combined effect of true Resistance (R) and Reactance (X).

Measured in Ohms (Ω). In AC electronics, we use Z in Ohm's Law instead of R (I = E / Z).

Section 4: Transformers

Introduction to Transformers

Transformers transfer electrical energy from one circuit to another using magnetic fields, without any direct electrical connection.

AC voltage applied to the Primary coil creates a fluctuating magnetic field in the Core. This magnetic field cuts across the wires of the Secondary coil, inducing a new voltage into it. This process is called Mutual Induction.

Section 4: Transformers

Turns Ratio & Voltage

The ratio of wire wraps (turns) between the primary and secondary coils dictates the output voltage.

Step-Up Transformer

Secondary has MORE turns than the Primary.

Output Voltage is Higher than input voltage.

Step-Down Transformer

Secondary has FEWER turns than the Primary.

Output Voltage is Lower than input voltage.

If the Primary has 100 turns, and the Secondary has 500 turns, the ratio is 1:5. If you input 10V AC, the output will be stepped-up to 50V AC.
Section 4: Transformers

Transformer Power and Current

A transformer cannot create free energy. Power in equals Power out.

Assuming a perfectly efficient transformer, the Power (Watts) on the primary side equals the Power on the secondary side.

Remember from Module 1 that Power = Voltage × Current.

Because Power stays the same, Voltage and Current have an inverse relationship in a transformer:

  • If a transformer Steps-UP the Voltage, it must Step-DOWN the Current.
  • If a transformer Steps-DOWN the Voltage, it Steps-UP the Current available.
