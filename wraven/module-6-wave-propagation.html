Section 1: Electromagnetic Physics

Cutting the Cord

Up to this point, all of our electrons have been trapped inside copper wires. How do we get signals to travel through empty space?

When an alternating current (AC) flows through a wire, it naturally creates a magnetic field around it. If we push a very high-frequency AC signal into a specially designed wire (an antenna), the energy actually breaks free from the wire and radiates into space.

This radiated energy is called an Electromagnetic (EM) Wave. Radio, Radar, Wi-Fi, and even visible light are all just different frequencies of EM waves.

Section 1: Electromagnetic Physics

The Anatomy of an EM Wave

An electromagnetic wave consists of two distinct fields traveling together.

  • Electric Field (E-Field): Created by the voltage in the antenna.
  • Magnetic Field (H-Field): Created by the current flowing through the antenna.

These two fields are always orthogonal (at exactly 90-degree right angles) to each other, and they sustain each other as they travel through space.

Section 1: Electromagnetic Physics

Wavelength and Frequency

Just like AC in a wire, EM waves have a Frequency (Hz) and a Wavelength (λ).

Frequency (f)

How many full wave cycles pass a given point in one second. Measured in Hertz (Hz).

A 1 MHz signal has 1,000,000 cycles per second.

Wavelength (λ)

The actual physical distance in space between one peak of the wave and the next peak. Measured in meters.

Usually represented by the Greek letter Lambda (λ).

Section 1: Electromagnetic Physics

The Speed of Light

All electromagnetic waves travel at the exact same speed in a vacuum: The Speed of Light.

The speed of light (represented by 'c') is approximately 300,000,000 meters per second.

Because the speed is a constant, Frequency and Wavelength are inversely proportional. The formula is:

λ = c / f

  • Low Frequency = Long Wavelength. (e.g., VLF radio waves can be miles long).
  • High Frequency = Short Wavelength. (e.g., Radar microwaves are only a few centimeters long).
Section 1: Electromagnetic Physics

Wave Polarization

Polarization refers to the physical orientation of the EM wave as it travels.

By definition, the polarization of a radio wave is determined strictly by the orientation of its Electric Field (E-Field).

  • Vertical Polarization: The E-field is up and down. (Most walkie-talkies and AM radio towers).
  • Horizontal Polarization: The E-field is flat/parallel to the ground. (Most television broadcast antennas).
⚠️ Crucial Rule: For maximum signal strength, the receiving antenna must be polarized in the exact same direction as the transmitting antenna. If they are crossed, you lose almost the entire signal (Cross-Polarization fade).
Section 1: Electromagnetic Physics

Multi-Path Fading

What happens when a signal bounces off a building and arrives late?

If a direct Space Wave arrives at an antenna at the exact same time as a reflected wave (bounced off the ground or a building), they can interfere with each other.

  • In-Phase: If the peaks line up, they combine to make a stronger signal (Constructive Interference).
  • Out-of-Phase: If the peak of the direct wave hits at the exact same time as the trough of the reflected wave, they cancel each other out entirely (Destructive Interference).

This constant shifting of signal strength is called Multi-Path Fading.

Section 2: Atmospheric Effects

How Waves Travel

Once the wave leaves the antenna, it takes three primary paths.

  1. Ground Waves (Surface Waves): Hug the surface of the Earth. Used heavily for VLF submarine comms.
  2. Space Waves (Direct Waves): Travel in a straight line of sight from antenna to antenna. Used for VHF, UHF, and Radar.
  3. Sky Waves: Shoot up into the atmosphere, bounce off the ionosphere, and return to Earth far over the horizon. Used for global HF comms.
Section 2: Atmospheric Effects

The Ionosphere

A layer of the upper atmosphere heavily bombarded by solar radiation.

The sun's ultraviolet light knocks electrons loose from gas atoms high in the atmosphere, creating a massive shield of ionized gas called the Ionosphere.

When High Frequency (HF) radio signals hit this ionized layer, they act like light hitting water—they are refracted (bent) back down to Earth. This bending allows military HF radios to communicate globally without relying on satellites.

Section 2: Atmospheric Effects

The Layers of the Ionosphere

The ionosphere is not a single solid block. It is divided into distinct layers.

The D & E Layers

The lowest layers (approx 30-60 miles up).

They only exist during the day when the sun is hitting them directly. They act as a sponge, absorbing lower frequency signals rather than bouncing them.

The F Layers (F1 & F2)

The highest layers (up to 250 miles up).

These are the primary layers used for long-distance sky wave bouncing. At night, F1 and F2 merge into a single layer, changing the bounce angles.

This is why AM radio stations suddenly carry farther after sunset—the absorbing D layer vanishes, allowing signals to bounce off the higher F layer.
Section 2: Atmospheric Effects

Critical Frequency & Angle

Not every signal bounces back down.

  • Critical Frequency: The absolute highest frequency that the ionosphere can bend back to Earth. If you transmit above this frequency (like VHF or UHF), the signal will simply punch a hole right through the ionosphere and shoot out into deep space.
  • Critical Angle: If you aim an HF antenna straight straight up at 90 degrees, the wave might punch through. You must lower the angle of the antenna so the wave hits the ionosphere at a shallower angle, allowing it to "skip" off the layer.
Section 2: Atmospheric Effects

The Skip Zone

Bouncing signals creates dead zones where the radio cannot be heard.

The Skip Zone is the area of total silence between the exact spot the Ground Wave naturally dies out, and the exact spot where the refracted Sky Wave finally returns to Earth.

Section 3: Transmission Lines

Getting Power to the Antenna

A transmitter is useless if the power burns up in the cables before reaching the antenna.

We use Transmission Lines to safely carry high-frequency AC from the transmitter to the antenna.

  • Two-Wire Open Line: (Like old TV antenna ribbon). Cheap, but suffers massive radiation loss at high frequencies.
  • Coaxial Cable: A center conductor surrounded by a dielectric insulator, wrapped in a braided metal shield. The outer shield traps the electromagnetic fields inside, preventing radiation loss.
Section 3: Transmission Lines

Characteristic Impedance (Zo)

Cables aren't just empty pipes. They have their own electrical identity.

Every transmission line has a Characteristic Impedance (Z₀) determined strictly by its physical dimensions (the diameter of the wire, the spacing, and the type of plastic insulation). It has nothing to do with the length of the cable.

For military and commercial RF gear, the standardized impedance is 50 Ohms (50Ω).

⚠️ If the transmitter is 50Ω, the cable must be 50Ω, and the antenna must be 50Ω. This is called Impedance Matching. If they don't match, catastrophe ensues.
Section 3: Transmission Lines

Standing Wave Ratio (SWR)

What happens when the impedance isn't matched perfectly?

If a 50Ω transmitter pushes 100 Watts of power down a cable, but the antenna at the end is badly damaged and looks like 150Ω, the antenna cannot absorb all the power.

The rejected power reflects backward down the cable, colliding with the forward power. This creates a massive Standing Wave of voltage that can literally melt the cable or blow up the transmitter's final amplifier stage.

  • A perfect SWR is 1:1 (All power radiated).
  • An SWR of 3:1 or higher means severe reflected power. Shut down the transmitter immediately and check the antenna cables.
Section 3: Transmission Lines

Waveguides

At radar microwave frequencies, standard coaxial cable melts. We use hollow pipes instead.

When frequencies exceed 3 GHz, the resistance of copper wire causes massive signal loss. To solve this, we remove the copper wire entirely.

A Waveguide is a hollow, rectangular metal pipe. Instead of electrons flowing through a wire, the electromagnetic waves bounce down the inside of the hollow tube. They are highly efficient but physically bulky, limiting them mostly to shipboard or fixed radar installations.

Section 4: Antennas

The Reciprocity Theorem

A good transmitter is a good receiver.

The Reciprocity Theorem states that the various properties of an antenna apply equally whether the antenna is transmitting or receiving.

  • If an antenna focuses its transmit power in a narrow beam to the North, it is also "deaf" to signals coming from the South.
  • If an antenna has high gain for transmitting, it has the exact same high gain for pulling in weak signals.
Section 4: Antennas

The Hertz Antenna (Half-Wave Dipole)

The fundamental building block of almost all complex antennas.

The Hertz Antenna is a wire cut to exactly one-half of a wavelength. It is split in the middle to allow the feedline to attach, creating two 1/4 wavelength elements (a Dipole).

It operates completely independently of the ground, making it perfect for high-frequency setups, aircraft, and space communications.

Section 4: Antennas

The Marconi Antenna (Quarter-Wave)

When a half-wavelength is physically too massive to build.

If you are transmitting at very low frequencies (like AM Radio), a Half-Wave Hertz antenna could be thousands of feet long. This is physically impossible to suspend in the air.

The Marconi Antenna solves this. It is a single vertical wire cut to only One-Quarter Wavelength (1/4 λ). It is mounted directly against the conductive earth.

The Earth acts as an electrical mirror, creating an "image" of the missing lower half, fooling the transmitter into thinking a full Half-Wave dipole exists.

Section 4: Antennas

Ground Planes & Radials

What if you want to use a Marconi antenna on a truck or a rooftop where you don't have access to the physical earth?

Because the Marconi relies on the ground to act as the "other half" of the antenna, you must provide an artificial ground if the earth isn't available.

A Ground Plane consists of metal rods (radials) or a flat metal sheet placed at the base of the quarter-wave vertical. This artificial ground perfectly reflects the RF energy, allowing the antenna to function correctly on vehicles or masts.

Section 4: Antennas

Directivity and Gain

A standard dipole radiates energy like a donut—in all directions equally. For radar and comms, we want to focus that energy.

Directivity

The ability of an antenna to focus its radiated energy into a specific, narrow beam (like turning a bare lightbulb into a flashlight).

Antenna Gain

By focusing the energy into a narrow beam, the signal strength in that specific direction is massively increased, even without adding more power to the transmitter. This relative increase is called Gain.

Section 4: Antennas

Loading Coils

How do you put a 30-foot antenna on a 10-foot truck?

Sometimes you need to transmit on a low frequency (requiring a long wavelength), but physical constraints prevent you from using a full-sized antenna.

You can electrically "lengthen" an antenna by installing a Loading Coil (a large inductor) at the base or in the middle of the whip. The coil slows down the RF energy, tricking the transmitter into thinking the antenna is physically longer than it actually is. It reduces efficiency but allows for mobile operations.

Section 5: RF Safety

HERP: Hazards of EM Radiation to Personnel

High power RF energy cooks from the inside out.

Unlike X-rays, RF radiation is Non-Ionizing, meaning it doesn't cause immediate cellular mutation. Instead, it causes dielectric heating. It literally acts like a microwave oven on human tissue.

Because the body's internal organs (especially the eyes) have poor blood flow to carry away heat, prolonged exposure in front of a high-power radar dish can cause deep internal burns or cataracts before you even feel warm on your skin.

Section 5: RF Safety

HERO: Hazards of EM Radiation to Ordnance

RF energy doesn't just heat tissue; it induces stray voltage.

If you stand near a powerful transmitter holding a piece of ungrounded metal, the metal acts as an antenna and absorbs the RF energy. This can induce enough voltage to cause a spark.

HERO is the strict safety protocol for handling explosives or firing caps near active antennas. The induced RF voltage can accidentally detonate explosive squibs or ignite fuel vapors (HERF).

⚠️ Never handle blasting caps, ordnance, or perform fueling operations when HF or Radar transmitters are radiating nearby.
Section 5: RF Safety

Waveguide Safety

A severed waveguide is a loaded gun.

As discussed, waveguides are hollow pipes used to carry massive microwave energy to radar dishes.

If a waveguide is disconnected or broken while the transmitter is active, the open end acts as a highly focused, high-gain antenna, blasting lethal microwave energy directly into the workspace.

Rule: NEVER look down an open waveguide. Always perform complete Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) on the transmitter before disconnecting any RF plumbing.
